Short answer: don't pick either side. Gerrit and Ilse are arguing over a false choice, and your own numbers show a third option.

**1. The grant rule is looser than Ilse reads it.**
- The condition is 20% of *works*, not a fifth of *concerts* as contemporary programmes.
- At 16 concerts with 3 to 4 works each, you program roughly 48 to 64 works, so about 10 to 13 works must be by living composers.
- Spread one short living-composer piece across most concerts and you meet the condition. You don't need any all-contemporary programmes.
- A Beethoven or Tchaikovsky concert can open with a 7 to 12 minute piece by a living composer. Gerrit's popular-classics season and the grant can coexist.
- Assumption: your count includes every work on the programme. Confirm with the arts council how they count works. Get that in writing before March.

**2. Ilse's point about identity and players is real, but it doesn't require a contemporary-heavy season.**
- Players leaving is a real risk, and a greatest-hits-only season would raise it.
- A season with substantive living-composer pieces woven in, plus a strong Unbound, answers her concern better than whole contemporary programmes, which are the weakest sellers in the main hall.

**3. Gerrit's point is real too, but the data doesn't say "classics fix it".**
- Friday subscription concerts are at 81%. The problem is Saturday single tickets at 48%, and that is where the 22% decline sits.
- Your only sell-out was film and video-game music by living composers. Ilse's "one-off" label is fair about repertoire, but it shows an audience that will come for living composers when the music is familiar and accessible.
- Unbound sold out all six nights, with a median audience age of 31 against 64 in the main hall. That is your clearest sign of future audience.
- Nothing here is evidence that Beethoven alone brings single-ticket buyers back. Treat "classics fix Saturday" as untested.

**Recommended direction for 2027-28: "Familiar core, living voices"**
1. **Saturday single-ticket slots:** recognizable anchors, such as a big concerto or a Beethoven or Tchaikovsky symphony, each paired with one accessible living-composer work. Include one or two film or game score programmes, which are sell-out proven and count toward the 20%.
2. **Friday subscription concerts:** keep the 81% base steady, with somewhat more adventurous programming, since that audience already trusts you.
3. **Living-composer quota:** plan for about 14 to 15 works, not 11 to 12, as a safety margin.
4. **Unbound:** expand it, for example from 6 to 8 or 10 nights. It is your youth pipeline and a morale asset for the players. Ask whether Unbound works count toward the grant.
5. **Commissions:** at EUR 35,000 each, commission selectively, perhaps one or two a season. The 20% doesn't need commissions; most living-composer works can be existing repertoire. Use a commission for a flagship piece players care about, which also helps retention.
6. **Players:** involve principals in choosing the living-composer works and Unbound programming. It is cheap and speaks directly to the departure risk.

**How to present it to the board**
- Show the arithmetic: 20% of works means about 10 to 13 pieces, and this plan clears that.
- Show the risk split. The grant is 38% of income, so don't hazard it. Single-ticket revenue is the gap. Test the Saturday hypothesis with a measurable target, such as Saturday occupancy rising from 48% to 60% or more, with a mid-season review.
- Frame it as a test of Gerrit's thesis inside Ilse's constraints, so both can say they were heard.

**Before March, check these:**
- The arts council's definition of "works" and whether Unbound counts.
- Saturday results by programme type over past seasons, to see whether classics already sell better than the rest.
- What the principals considering leaving actually want. Ask them directly; I'm inferring from Ilse's warning.
