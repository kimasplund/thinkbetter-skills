My recommendation is neither camp's plan as stated. Build 2027-28 as a hybrid. Put popular classics where single-ticket sales are weak, and meet the grant through living-composer works that audiences already show they will come for. Treat the dispute as two separate questions, because your numbers answer them differently.

**1. The grant is about works, not concerts.**

Ilse reads the condition as "a fifth of our concerts must be contemporary programmes." The text says "at least 20% of works programmed across the season must be by living composers." That is a count of works across the whole season, and it is a lower bar than her reading.

- Last season you gave 16 concerts with 3 or 4 works each, so roughly 48-64 works. 20% is about 10-13 works by living composers. Check how the council counts. I'm assuming an overture or an encore counts as a work, and that arrangements and film scores by living composers count. Confirm both with the council in writing before March.
- Those 10-13 works do not need to sit in 10-13 "contemporary concerts." One short living-composer piece on most programmes would meet the threshold. That piece could be an opener of 8-12 minutes next to Beethoven or Tchaikovsky.
- This means Gerrit's programme and the grant are compatible. The grant is 38% of income, so a plan that risks it is not acceptable. A plan that meets it with margin is acceptable.

Build in a safety margin, for example 25% of works, in case a work is cut or the council counts differently. Also ask the council whether it wants those works spread across the season or concentrated.

**2. What the data says about audiences.**

- Friday subscription concerts averaged 81% full. Saturday single-ticket concerts averaged 48%. The 22% decline in single-ticket sales is concentrated where you sell to people who choose concert by concert. Subscribers are loyal and you should not disrupt that base.
- The only main-stage sell-out was the film and video-game concert, all living composers. Ilse calls it a one-off, and it may be. But one sell-out is also evidence that living-composer repertoire is not what keeps people away. It shows that familiar-sounding, accessible music sells. It does not show that contemporary concert music sells, so do not over-read it.
- Unbound sold out all six nights at 180 seats, with a median age of 31 against 64 in the main hall. That is a young audience, and it is mostly new music. Be careful: the room is small, the format is late-night and informal, and six nights is a small sample. It shows demand for new music in a different format, not that those people would buy main-hall tickets.

So the evidence weighs against both extreme positions. "Contemporary programming is what empties the hall" is not supported, since your two best-attended events were mostly or entirely living-composer. "Popular classics will refill Saturdays" is also not proven, because you have no test of it in your data. Your Saturday programmes may be weak for reasons other than repertoire, such as timing, price, marketing or the way programmes are framed.

**3. Why a pure greatest-hits season is risky.**

- Ilse's warning about principals leaving is a real operating risk, though unquantified. Losing principals costs auditions, interim hires and quality, and quality drives ticket sales. Ask her which players, and test it with a short conversation with section principals rather than relying on her report.
- A season of only well-known works competes with every recording and larger orchestra in the region, and you have less to differentiate you.
- It would also give up the one audience you have shown you can grow, the under-40s at Unbound.

**4. Why a pure contemporary-heavy season is also risky.**

Ilse's position, taken to the extreme, would put more contemporary programmes on Saturdays, where you are already at 48%. You have no evidence that this fills seats. The grant does not require it.

**5. Proposed direction.**

- **Saturday single-ticket slots:** Make these the accessible, name-recognition programmes, with a Beethoven, Tchaikovsky or big concerto anchor. This is Gerrit's strongest point, and it targets the weak spot.
- **Living-composer works on nearly every programme:** About 25% of works overall, as short pieces beside the classics. Choose accessible, strong pieces. Include at least one film or game-score programme, or a half-programme, since that is your proven seller.
- **Commissions:** Budget for 2 commissions, about €70,000, which is a small fraction of a budget in which the grant is 38%. Premiere them in programmes with a popular anchor so the new work gets heard by a full house. Consider co-commissioning with other orchestras to share the cost.
- **Unbound:** Keep it and expand it modestly, for instance 8-10 nights, with players leading the programming. It supports Ilse's identity argument and the retention of players. Track whether Unbound attendees cross over to the main hall by collecting emails at the door.
- **Subscriptions:** Keep Friday programmes close to the current mix so you do not disturb your 81% base.

**6. How to put it to the board.**

The board is split, so frame it as a test. Gerrit gets popular programmes where single-ticket sales are weakest. Ilse gets the grant secured, the identity kept, and the principals' concerns addressed. Set measurable checks in advance, for example Saturday occupancy against the 48% baseline after the first third of the season, and a decision point to adjust the second half.

**What would change my view:**

- The council says the 20% counts concerts, or excludes arrangements or short pieces. Then Ilse is right on the grant and you need more full contemporary programmes.
- Principals confirm they would leave over a heavy popular-classics season. Then weight the mix further toward Ilse.
- Past Saturday data shows that popular-classics nights also underperformed. Then repertoire is not the main problem, and you should look at pricing, marketing and the Saturday time slot.

**First steps before March:** get the council's definition in writing, pull per-concert sales history by repertoire type, and talk to the principals.
