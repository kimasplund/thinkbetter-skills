Short answer: each is right about something different, and neither proposal should go to the vote as written. Halloran has the diagnosis right and Osei has the conclusion right (don't simply walk away from overnight). But Osei's main argument doesn't hold up against your own facts, and Halloran's remedy treats a rota-design problem as if it were a service problem.

**Where Halloran is right**

Three vets left in 18 months and all three named nights. That is a retention problem you can't ignore, and a €190,000 annual loss on top of it is a real drain. If nothing changes, you will keep losing people, and each loss makes the remaining rota heavier.

**Where Osei is right**

Closing overnight leaves a 70 km gap in emergency cover for your own clients, and about a fifth of your overnight patients (roughly 390 a year) belong to Thornby, who rely on you because they have no out-of-hours service. Brackley's director has also told you they are already stretched, so "refer to Brackley" may mean longer waits or refusals for animals that can't safely travel 70 km. You'd be giving up a position nobody else in the valley holds, and clients who are turned away in an emergency tend not to come back for routine work either.

**Where Osei's argument breaks**

The dairy contracts are the strongest-sounding point, and your facts undercut them. Calvings are attended on the farm by your farm-animal vets, who take calls from home on their own rota, and they have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. The round-the-clock calving cover those contracts require is, on what you've told me, delivered by the farm team, not by the clinic. Closing the small-animal overnight service shouldn't by itself put the €260,000 at risk.

I would check two things before 28 November, because this is the point on which Osei's case stands or falls:
1. Read the four contracts' exact wording. Do they require cover from the farm team only, or do they mention hospital access, surgical backup, or a named emergency facility? A caesarean or a downer cow that needs hospitalisation is the case where Kilbride might matter.
2. Ask the four herd owners directly, framed neutrally ("what do you rely on at night?"), rather than assume.

If the contracts only need farm-call cover, Osei's financial argument largely falls away. If they need hospital backup, the contracts are an argument for keeping some overnight capability, though not necessarily the full 7 pm to 8 am.

**Where Halloran's remedy breaks**

Your exit interviews say nights drove people out. They don't say that having overnight service at all drove them out. The way you staff nights is the specific thing you can change. All 11 small-animal vets do one or two night shifts a month on top of a full day rota, so nobody ever recovers from a night, and you turned down an experienced emergency vet last spring because she would only work nights. The policy was fair when you opened. It is now producing exactly the attrition Halloran describes, and you refused the person who could have fixed it.

So the real choice isn't "close or keep losing vets". It is "keep nights as an add-on to every vet's day job, or make nights a staffed service".

**What I'd propose instead**

1. **Take nights off the day rota.** Hire one or two dedicated emergency or night-focused vets, with proper rest days and a pay premium, and re-open the door to the vet you turned down. Then drop the day vets' mandatory night shifts, or make them voluntary and paid at a premium. This goes at the cause of the three resignations.
2. **Shorten the staffed window to match demand.** 71% of overnight patients (about 1,390) arrive before 1 am. After 1 am you average under two a night. Staff the clinic 7 pm to about 1 am, or 7 pm to 2 am once you check the real arrival times. For the rest of the night, run a single on-call vet from home with a defined call-out rule, with Brackley as the fallback only for cases that don't need Kilbride's facilities. That makes roughly 570 cases a year the thing you hand to a lighter model, instead of a 70 km referral for everyone.
3. **Make Thornby pay or partner.** A fifth of your overnight caseload is a practice that has no service of its own. Offer a formal out-of-hours arrangement with a retainer or a per-case fee. At a minimum they should be contributing to a service they depend on. If they won't, that tells you something about its real value to them.
4. **Re-price the overnight service.** Review your emergency consultation and out-of-hours fees against Brackley's and against what clients will bear. A €190,000 loss is partly a price problem, and part of it is the cost of staffing the quiet hours.

**What this does and doesn't solve**

- It keeps the overnight service, which protects the clients, Thornby, and any dairy-contract exposure, while removing the thing vets are leaving over.
- It cuts the quiet-hours cost (the staffed hours after 1 am) without cutting the busiest 71% of demand.
- It will not make the service profitable on its own. Expect the loss to shrink, not vanish. I can't tell you by how much without your staffing costs, so build the numbers: dedicated vet salaries plus premium, minus saved late-night hours, plus Thornby income and fee changes. If the new model still loses more than you're willing to carry, you then have a clean basis for a fuller closure decision, with the dairy contracts checked and Thornby informed in advance.
- The on-call-from-home arrangement after 1 am has its own risk: if call-outs are frequent, you recreate the burnout. Track them for three months and set a trigger (for example, more than three call-outs a week on average) that sends you back to staffing the clinic later.
- Hiring is the main uncertainty. Emergency vets are scarce, and you may not find anyone by January. A temporary measure for the gap would be locum emergency cover, or a reduced overnight window until the hires land.

**For the 28 November vote**

I would not ask the partners to choose between the two positions. I'd put a single motion: adopt the restructured model from January (or a phased start in the first quarter), with the contract review and herd-owner conversations completed before the vote, a hiring plan, a Thornby proposal, and a review date after three to six months with a pre-agreed trigger for revisiting closure. That gives Halloran what she is really after, which is an end to the night shifts that are driving vets out. And it gives Osei what he is really after, which is that you don't abandon your clients or put the dairy work at risk without evidence.

One caveat on all of this: I'm working from the figures in your message, and I've assumed the €190,000 loss is a fair picture of the clinic's overnight economics. If a large part of it is allocated overhead rather than the actual extra cost of running nights, the true loss is smaller and the case for keeping the service gets stronger.
