Short answer: each is right about a real danger and wrong about the remedy. Halloran is right that the current night model is what is driving vets out. Osei is right that you shouldn't walk away from overnight cover. But your own facts weaken both of their arguments, and I think there is a third option for the 28 November vote.

**Where Osei's argument is weakest: the dairy contracts**

Your farm-animal vets attend calvings on the farm, take calls from home on their own rota, and have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. On those facts, the round-the-clock calving cover the four dairy contracts require is provided by the farm team, not by the Kilbride night service. Closing Kilbride overnight probably doesn't breach them.

Before the vote, have someone read the four contracts line by line. Look for any clause that mentions hospital access, surgical or caesarean backup, or an emergency facility, as opposed to attendance on farm. A difficult calving that needs a clinic at 3 am would be the case that matters. If the wording is only about on-farm attendance, the €260,000 risk largely disappears from the debate. If it isn't, that changes the picture, and you need to know which before you vote.

The 70 km gap is still a real argument, but it's about your small-animal clients and your reputation, not the dairy revenue.

**Where Halloran's argument is weakest: closure fixes the wrong thing**

The three vets who left named nights. Your facts suggest the problem is how nights are staffed, not that nights exist:

- All 11 small-animal vets do one or two overnight shifts a month on top of a full day rota. That is "fair", but it asks every vet to do a job many of them never chose, and it's the model most associated with burnout.
- You turned down an experienced emergency vet last spring because she would only work nights. People who want that work exist, and the current policy excluded one.
- Demand is front-loaded. 71% of the 1,950 patients (about 1,385, or under four a night) arrive before 1 am. After 1 am you average under two a night, so for roughly seven of the eleven hours a vet is mostly waiting, which is the most draining kind of night.

Closing entirely also shifts the load onto Brackley, which says it is already stretched and is 70 km away. For an emergency, a 70 km drive is a serious welfare and goodwill cost. You'd also lose service to Thornby's clients, about a fifth of overnight patients (roughly 390 a year), who currently come to you. That's a relationship and a referral stream you'd be handing over.

**What I'd put to the vote instead**

Change who works nights and when, rather than whether Kilbride opens.

1. Stop the compulsory night rota for the 11 day vets from January. This removes the thing people are leaving over.
2. Staff the busy window with people who want nights. Hire one or two dedicated emergency vets (or a night-only contract team) and pay for it as a specialty. The woman you turned down is the model. Run the full clinic from 7 pm to about 1 am, which covers 71% of your caseload.
3. Treat 1 am to 8 am separately. At under two patients a night, you don't need a fully staffed clinic. The options are a dedicated vet on call from home with a nurse on call, or phone triage with referral to Brackley only for the cases that truly can't wait. That sends Brackley a small fraction of your volume (very roughly 550 to 600 cases a year, not 1,950) instead of everything, which is much easier for a stretched service to absorb. Ask Brackley what number they can actually take before relying on this.
4. Make Thornby pay or share. A fifth of your overnight caseload comes from a practice that has no out-of-hours service. Propose a retainer, a per-case fee uplift, or a shared rota in which one of their vets takes some of the on-call. They are using your service for free in effect.
5. Price the loss properly. The €190,000 figure is the baseline. Test whether a shortened full-service window, higher emergency fees and a Thornby contribution close part of that gap. I can't compute it from what you've given me, but those levers are untouched so far.

**Things to check before 28 November**

- The four dairy contracts: exact wording, as above.
- Whether the 11-vet rota arithmetic holds. Eleven vets doing one or two shifts a month gives 11 to 22 shifts, but there are about 30 nights a month. If shifts are split or doubled-up, that's an even bigger staffing burden than it looks. If some nights are covered another way, that matters.
- Whether you could realistically recruit a night-focused vet in your area, and at what salary. Test the market with a job advert now, not after the vote.
- Brackley's real capacity for a reduced after-1-am referral flow.
- Whether your last three leavers would have stayed if they were no longer on the night rota. You could ask them informally.

**Which of them is right?**

On the narrow question of whether the current model has to change, Halloran. On whether you can simply walk away from the service and the clients who depend on it, Osei is closer, though not for the reason he gives. Neither wins outright. A vote of "close" versus "keep as is" forces a choice between two bad outcomes, so I'd bring the restructured proposal to the table as a third motion, and set a review point (say six months in) with clear measures: vet retention, caseload by hour, and the net cost of the service.

What I can't resolve from here is the money, since I don't know the cost of dedicated emergency vets in your market or what Thornby would pay. If the restructured service still loses most of the €190,000 and Thornby won't contribute, then a daytime-plus-early-evening service with closure after about 11 pm or 1 am becomes the fallback, and Halloran's instinct would have been right in a smaller form.
