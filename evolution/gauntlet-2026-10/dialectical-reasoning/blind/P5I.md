**Short answer:** Don't choose between Gerrit and Ilse. Build the season around popular classics on the nights that need them, and meet the grant through works by living composers placed inside those same concerts. Your own numbers show the two positions collide much less than the board thinks.

## The grant rule is about works, not concerts

Ilse reads the condition as "a fifth of our concerts must be contemporary programmes." The text you quoted says "at least 20% of works programmed across the season must be by living composers."

- 16 concerts at 3 to 4 works each is roughly 48 to 64 works. Twenty percent is about 10 to 13 pieces.
- Those pieces can be spread across the season. A 10-minute piece by a living composer opening a Tchaikovsky concert counts the same as a piece in an all-contemporary programme.
- Nothing in the wording requires a contemporary-only concert. That is the one assumption that makes the board's argument look like a real either/or.

Two things to confirm in writing with the arts council before March:
1. Whether the count is by work or by running time, and how arrangements are treated.
2. Whether Unbound, the foyer series, counts as "programmed across the season." If it does, a good share of the quota is already met. Don't plan on it until they say so.

## What each side has right

**Gerrit.** Your problem is concentrated in single tickets. Saturdays average 48% against 81% on Fridays. The subscription base is healthy, and the weak spot is the casual buyer, who wants a name they recognise. Beethoven, Tchaikovsky and a big concerto are the cheapest way to reach that buyer.

**Ilse.** Her warning about the grant is overstated, but the other two concerns hold up. A season of greatest hits would wear down the players and the orchestra's reputation, and the principals who might leave are a real cost. A popular-only season would also leave you with no answer to the next 22% decline, because you would have used up the one lever you had.

## What the data says about the middle

- **Your only main-stage sell-out was all living composers.** Ilse calls it a one-off, and it may be. But it is also the one concert in the year that filled the hall. It shows that "living composer" and "doesn't sell" are not the same thing. Film and game scores are the mildest case, so treat it as one data point, not a formula.
- **Unbound sold out six of six, with a median audience age of 31 against 64 in the hall.** This is mostly new music. You have an audience for new work that you are not yet bringing into the main hall.
- **The audiences differ by night.** Subscribers on Fridays already fill the hall and presumably trust the programming. Saturday is where you need a pull.

## Recommended season design

1. **Saturdays carry the draw.** Make most Saturday programmes anchored by a recognised classic or a big Romantic concerto. This is Gerrit's season, applied where the sales problem is.
2. **Each concert gets a living-composer piece, not a separate "contemporary night".** Target about 14 works rather than the bare 11 to 13, so one withdrawn piece or a counting dispute doesn't put the grant at risk. Choose short, accessible pieces that sit well next to a classic. Several living composers write concertos and overtures that fit this role.
3. **Keep one film-and-game programme, and consider a second.** It is your best-selling format and it counts toward the quota. Ilse's objection that it isn't "real repertoire" is fair as a statement about identity, so keep it to one or two nights, not the backbone of the season.
4. **Give Ilse a signature.** Commission two or three new works, about €35,000 each (roughly €70,000 to €105,000). Place each beside a major classic on a Friday, where the audience is more willing to take a risk. Ask a funder or the council whether commissions can be co-funded, since they serve the grant's purpose. This is the part of the season that keeps the principals and the identity intact.
5. **Use Unbound as a pipeline.** Pick the strongest pieces from Unbound and move them into the main hall as short openers, with Unbound players leading them where possible. This tests new work cheaply and gives your younger audience a reason to come to the main hall.

## Decision rules for individual slots

- A Saturday single-ticket slot gets a familiar anchor plus at most one short living-composer piece.
- A Friday subscription slot can carry the more demanding commission or a longer contemporary piece.
- A piece qualifies for the main hall from Unbound if it sold well there and the players want to take it up.
- Review after the first quarter. If Saturdays are still under about 60%, shift the balance on those nights further toward familiar repertoire and keep the quota pieces short. If a contemporary piece lifts a night above its usual number, repeat that kind of pairing.

## What this doesn't resolve

- **Whether it will fix the 22% decline.** A better-labelled Saturday programme may not be enough. Check the real cause with your ticketing data, for example price, day of the week, or marketing, before assuming repertoire is the whole story.
- **Ilse and Gerrit may still disagree on the proportion.** The plan above makes room for both but does not decide how popular or how adventurous the average night should be. Agree a few measurable tests up front, such as Saturday fill rate and grant compliance, so the argument becomes about results.
- **The retention risk is hard to quantify.** Ask Ilse which principals she is worried about and why. If the concern is about artistic ambition, the commissions and the Unbound link address it directly. If it is about pay or workload, they won't.

**Confidence:** high that the grant is satisfiable without a contemporary-only series, because it rests on the wording you quoted, subject to the council's confirmation. Moderate on the sales impact, because it rests on one sell-out and one foyer series.
