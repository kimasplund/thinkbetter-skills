Short answer: neither is fully right, and the vote on 28 November probably shouldn't be "close or keep". Your own facts weaken both positions, and there is a third option that fits them better.

**Where Dr Osei is wrong**

- The dairy contracts are the strongest argument for keeping overnight cover, but the facts you gave undercut it. Calvings are attended on the farm by your farm-animal vets, who take calls from home on their own rota. They have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. Closing the small-animal overnight clinic doesn't, on its face, remove round-the-clock calving cover.
- Check the contract wording before the vote. If it requires 24-hour farm-animal cover, closing Kilbride overnight doesn't breach it. If it requires an out-of-hours hospital or hospitalisation facility, that changes things. Ask the four clients, or reread the clauses, so this is settled and not assumed.
- The risk that does remain is indirect. Some dairy clients may also use Kilbride for farm dogs or for the occasional large-animal case needing facilities. Find out how many.

**Where Dr Halloran is wrong**

- Referring clients to Brackley, 70 km away, is not a real fallback. Brackley's director says their overnight service is already stretched. You would be sending roughly 1,950 patients a year to a clinic that may not cope, over a distance that is dangerous for a sick animal.
- About a fifth of those patients (roughly 390) belong to Thornby Vets, which has no out-of-hours service and depends on you. Closing strands them. It also hurts Thornby, and it ends a referral relationship you'd probably want to keep.
- There is a reputational and welfare cost to being the practice that withdrew the only emergency service in 70 km. It is hard to put a number on, but it exists.

**Where she is right**

- You have lost three vets in 18 months, and all three named nights. That is the most important fact in the brief. The retention problem is real and will continue. Replacing a vet costs a lot, and each departure adds load on the rest.
- €190,000 a year is a large loss for a group your size.

**The root problem is the rota design, not the existence of the service**

- Eleven vets each doing one or two nights a month on top of a full day rota is "fair", but it is also what is driving people out. Fairness of distribution has been treated as the goal, when the real goals are keeping vets and keeping the service.
- Last spring you turned down an experienced emergency vet because she would only work nights. That is a significant fact. A dedicated night vet is exactly what could fix the problem, and you said no because of the shared-rota policy.

**A third option to put to the partners**

1. Move to a dedicated or small-core overnight team. Hire one or two emergency-focused vets (or locums or a night-only contract) and release the 11 day vets from overnight duty, or leave it voluntary with a pay premium. This addresses the reason vets are leaving.
2. Shorten the hours. 71% of patients arrive before 1 am, and after 1 am you average under two a night. Moving to, say, 7 pm to 1 am or 2 am, with a telephone triage line and an on-call vet after that, would cut staffing cost substantially and keep most of the demand. Test this against the actual hourly data first.
3. Charge appropriately. Emergency out-of-hours care is usually priced at a premium. Check that your overnight fees reflect the cost, and consider a referral or access fee from Thornby, or a formal agreement with Thornby (a retainer or per-case payment) since it relies on you.
4. Keep the farm-animal rota as it is, and confirm the contract position.

**What I'd want to know before 28 November**

- The real cost structure of the €190,000 loss: how much is staff, how much is unfilled capacity, and what a reduced-hours or dedicated-team model would do to it.
- The hour-by-hour caseload, and what happens after 1 am.
- The exact dairy contract terms and whether those clients actually use Kilbride.
- Whether Thornby would contribute, and whether Brackley could take overflow in a shared arrangement.
- The cost of losing and replacing another vet, compared with the cost of paying a night premium.

**Assumptions**: I've assumed the €190,000 loss is after all overnight revenue, that your day-clinic finances are otherwise sound, and that a night-focused hire is available in the region, which you'd need to check.

**Bottom line**: Dr Halloran is right that the current model is not sustainable, and Dr Osei is right that simply closing abandons clients and may harm the business. But the dairy argument looks weaker than she fears, and the Brackley referral is weaker than he fears... or rather weaker than she assumes. A restructured, shortened, staffed-differently overnight service answers both concerns better than either closing or carrying on as you are. If you must pick between the two as stated, I would not vote to close in January without first testing the shortened-hours and dedicated-vet options, because closing is hard to reverse and the facts show cheaper alternatives you haven't tried.
