Yes, there are other options, and your suspicion is half right: the two proposals are weak, but the problem is easier than it looks. The hut isn't short of water so much as it uses far too much of it for a spring that fails.

## The numbers behind the problem

- 6 m³ a day for 48 beds is about 125 litres per guest per night. A hut without flush toilets and showers typically runs at roughly 20 to 30 litres. The 2016 upgrade is almost certainly where most of your demand comes from.
- Your 12 m³ tank holds two days at full use.
- Helicopter water at full demand: 6 m³ is 7.5 rotations, about €8,250 a day. Last August's 19-day outage would have cost roughly €157,000 to cover by air. That is why the second proposal looks hopeless.
- The same helicopter arithmetic at a hut using about 1 m³ a day is about €1,400 a day, and at 0.5 m³ a day about €700. The helicopter becomes a sensible backstop once demand falls.
- The borehole is €140,000 at 60% odds, so about €233,000 per working well in expectation. If a closure costs you something like €15,000 to €20,000 in lost takings and refunds (use your own figures) and happens in three years out of five, you lose roughly €10,000 a year. The borehole takes well over a decade to pay back, and only if it works.
- The shortage comes in late summer, when occupancy is already about 55%. The outage will usually hit when you have about 26 guests, not 48.

## Options other than the two on the table

**1. Cut demand, which is the cheapest and biggest lever.**
- Remove or restrict the showers: tokens, timers, or off from mid-August or whenever the tank drops below a set level.
- Replace flush toilets with waterless urinals plus vacuum, composting or low-water toilets. Even 1 to 2 litre flush systems cut toilet use sharply.
- Fit push-button or spray taps, and recycle basin and shower greywater to flush the remaining toilets.
- Realistic result: around 1 m³ a day at 26 guests, and about 0.5 m³ a day in a declared restricted mode (no showers, flush-light toilets, simpler menu).
- Cost: probably in the low tens of thousands of euros, depending on the toilet choice. Get quotes.

**2. Store the water that currently floods the path.**
- Your spring overflows in late June. Add a buried or insulated cistern of roughly 30 to 40 m³ (on top of the 12 m³) and fill it before and just after opening.
- At about 1 m³ a day, 45 m³ covers a 19-day outage on its own. With a restricted mode it covers a month or more.
- Caveats: freezing, stagnation and hygiene (you will need treatment and a flushing routine), and getting the tank up by helicopter. Indicative cost is a few tens of thousands of euros, so get a real quote.
- This addresses the actual failure, a shortfall of weeks, and it is a one-off cost.

**3. Collect rainwater from the roof.**
- A roof of about 300 m² (check yours) with 300 to 400 mm of summer precipitation gives roughly 90 to 120 m³ a season. That is around 1 m³ a day on average.
- It is suitable for flushing and cleaning with basic filtering, and it feeds the same cistern. It is not a full solution because it is rain-dependent, but it helps in the weeks when the spring is low.

**4. Pump from the Grauberg alp.**
- The alp is 190 m lower, so water must be pumped up, not piped down. The energy is small: about 1 kWh per m³ including losses. Your spare solar capacity covers it easily.
- The real costs are 1.1 km or more of pressure-rated pipe, a high-head pump, and an agreement with the family. A hose laid on the surface each season and drained in autumn is much cheaper than a buried line.
- Before the AGM, ask the family for their spring's measured minimum flow, whether they have surplus above the dairy's needs, what they would charge, and who holds the water rights. Their spring has never failed, but it sits in the same climate, so check its low-flow record.
- A supply contract that you only draw on in shortage weeks could cost far less than either proposal. It is worth a conversation this winter.

**5. Improve the source you have.**
- Commission your hydrologist to look for a second intake, such as a nearby seep, a stream below the snowfield, or a lower capture point on the existing spring.
- Check the 300 m pipe and the intake for leaks and silting before blaming the spring alone.
- Ask whether the 19-day stoppage was true dryness or partly a blockage, freezing or an intake problem. The fix is very different.

**6. Plan the late season around water.**
- Replace the sudden closure with a standing rule. From mid-August, if tank level falls below a set mark, move to restricted service: no showers, limited beds or pre-booked guests only, and guests told to carry some water.
- Closing for 11 days with refunds is the worst outcome. A planned reduced service loses much less income.
- You could also consider ending the season earlier in years when the spring is clearly failing. With demand at 55% and a crevassed glacier route, the last weeks may earn little anyway. Check your own figures.

**7. Treat grey water for reuse.**
- Small biological or membrane treatment units exist for huts. They are costly and need skilled upkeep, so I would keep this as a later option, not a first step.

**8. Borehole, staged.**
- If the committee still wants it, start with a geophysical survey or a cheaper test drilling rather than committing €140,000 at once. Do it only after the options above have been priced, and only if the numbers show a continuing gap.

## What I would put to the AGM

| Goal | Option | Rough cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fix the root cause | Demand cut (showers, toilets, taps) | Low tens of thousands | Reduces every other cost |
| Cover a multi-week outage | 30 to 40 m³ cistern, filled in June | Tens of thousands | Needs hygiene and freezing plan |
| Add a second supply | Supply from Grauberg, or a second intake | Unknown until quoted | Ask the family for flow data and terms |
| Last resort | Helicopter backstop | €700 to €1,400 a day at low demand | Budget a few thousand euros a year |
| Defer | Borehole | €140,000 at 60% odds | Survey first |

My recommendation is to present members with a package of demand cuts, a cistern and a standing late-season rule, with a helicopter budget as a backstop and a quote from Grauberg to be prepared in time. That package will probably cost well under half your €75,000 reserves, and it handles a repeat of last August without a closure. The figures above are my estimates from the details you gave. Get quotes for the cistern, toilets and pipe, plus the family's flow data, before the vote, so the AGM sees real costs and not mine.
