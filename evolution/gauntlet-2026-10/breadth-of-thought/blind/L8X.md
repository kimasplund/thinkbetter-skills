You have far more than two options. The two proposals both treat the problem as "get more water". But 6 m³ a day is about 125 litres per guest, and your tank holds only two days at that rate. The cheapest lever may be how much water you use, not where it comes from.

## The numbers that frame the choice

- **Helicopter at current use is not affordable.** At full occupancy, 6 m³ a day is 7.5 rotations, about €8,250 a day. At the 55% late-August level it is roughly 4 rotations, about €4,500 a day. A 19-day outage would cost about €85,000 even at 55%, which is more than your reserves.
- **Helicopter with much lower use is cheap.** Drinking and cooking only needs roughly 5 litres per guest per day. For about 26 guests that is 0.13 m³ a day, so one rotation lasts about a week and 19 days costs around €4,000 to €5,000.
- **The borehole costs €140,000 against €75,000 in reserves.** It has a 40% chance of finding nothing, and it would probably tap the same snowfield-fed system that is shrinking. You would need a loan or grants, and you would be paying for a coin flip.
- **Pumping from Grauberg is energy-cheap.** Lifting 6 m³ a day through 190 m takes about 3 kWh of hydraulic energy, perhaps 5 to 6 kWh at the socket. Your spare solar capacity covers that easily. The costs are the pipe, the agreement, and the water rights.

Assumptions: I don't know your actual split between toilets, showers and kitchen. I haven't seen the terrain between the hut and Grauberg. All costs below, apart from your two quotes, are my rough guesses and need real quotes.

## The options

**1. Use much less water (reduce demand)**
Meter the hut for a week to see where the 6 m³ goes. The 2016 flush toilets and showers are the likely cause. Measures range from timed or token showers and waterless urinals, to low-flush or vacuum toilets, to going back to dry or composting toilets. Greywater from sinks and showers can also be filtered and reused for flushing, powered by your spare solar. A target of 40 to 50 litres per guest a day would cut use by 60 to 70%.
- Cost: probably low to moderate, from a few thousand euros to tens of thousands for the full toilet change.
- Weakness: guests asked for the flush toilets and showers, so you need a plan for how to sell the change.
- Strength: it improves every other option, and it works every year, not just in shortage years.

**2. A tiered "water-short" mode**
Set a tank-level trigger. When the spring drops, showers close, toilets switch to a reduced mode and the hut serves a simplified menu. Guests are told when they book that service may be reduced in late summer. This turns a sudden closure into a planned and priced reduction. It is almost free.
- Weakness: it only stretches the supply and doesn't create water. Used alone, it won't cover a full 19-day outage.

**3. Bank the June overflow (more storage)**
The spring floods the path in late June and then fails in August, so you could store the surplus. With demand cut as in option 1, 26 guests at 40 litres is about 1 m³ a day, so 19 days needs about 20 m³. With your existing 12 m³ tank, roughly 10 to 15 m³ of extra storage could carry you through a full outage. Tanks must be drained or frost-proof over winter and the water kept fresh.
- Cost: moderate, perhaps €15,000 to €40,000 depending on tank type and how the site is reached.
- Weakness: it depends on option 1 (without it you would need about 100 m³ of storage). It also protects only against outages of the length you plan for.

**4. Pipe water up from Grauberg**
Run a seasonal pipe 1.1 km and pump the 190 m rise with solar power. The farm's spring has never failed, and the family is friendly. This could be a standing supply, a backup valve that opens only when your spring drops, or a paid arrangement for a fixed number of cubic metres. The main questions are whether their spring has enough surplus (their dairy comes first), who holds the water rights, whether the pipe can be laid across the terrain, and whether a surface pipe survives snow and rockfall.
- Cost: unknown, likely mid five figures to low six figures if the route is difficult. Get a quote.
- Weakness: it depends on the family's goodwill over the long term, so put the agreement in writing. Permissions and a pump station add effort and a failure point.
- Strength: it is the only option that solves the problem at full demand.

**5. Fetch water from Grauberg by other means**
If there is a track, a mule, a tracked vehicle or a short helicopter hop from the farm is cheaper than flying from the valley. Ask the helicopter company for a price on a shorter rotation. Short runs may cost a lot less than €1,100.
- Weakness: still labour or flight costs, and only suitable for small volumes.

**6. Improve what you already have at the spring**
Ask the hydrologist whether a deeper or higher intake, a second source, or repairs to the intake and pipe would raise late-summer flow. Check for leaks and freezing. A small catchment and roof rainwater collection will add a little too.
- Cost: low, mostly the hydrologist's time. The gain is uncertain and probably modest, since the cause is the shrinking snowfield.

**7. Change the calendar and the contracts**
Occupancy halves after mid-August anyway. You could plan a lighter late-August service, or shorten the season, and open earlier in June if access allows, when water is plentiful. Rewrite booking terms so a water shortage means rebooking or partial credit instead of full refunds, and ask your insurer about interruption cover. Make guiding companies aware of the risk in advance.
- Weakness: lost revenue if you shorten the season, and the guiding companies need to agree. Earlier opening depends on snow and access.

**8. Helicopter as a backup only**
Keep the committee's second proposal, but as the last layer. With options 1 to 3 in place, it is a modest top-up. With no demand cuts, it is the most expensive thing on this list.
- Cost: €1,100 per rotation, nothing up front.

**9. Borehole, staged**
Keep it as a later option and not a November decision. A cheaper first step is a geophysical survey, or asking the geologist what raises the 60%, such as a better site or a different depth. Only commit if grants cover much of the cost, or if the other routes fail. If you do drill, ask what happens to the money if the hole is dry, and whether the fee depends on results.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Use less and store more** | 1 + 2 + 3, with 8 as backup | Moderate, within reserves | Yes, if use drops to about 40 to 50 L per guest | Guest reaction to changes |
| **B. Get a second source** | 4, with 1 as a cheap extra | Unknown, probably high | Yes, even at normal use | Agreement and terrain |
| **C. Cheapest start** | 1 (partial) + 2 + 7 + 8 | Low | Mostly, if a rotation fills gaps | Still needs flights in bad years |
| **D. Borehole** | 9 | €140,000 | Only if it works (about 60%) | Cost above reserves, shared aquifer |
| **E. Helicopter only** | 8 | Up to about €85,000 per outage | Yes, expensively | Drains reserves in one season |

If I had to rank them for your situation, I would put A first, then B, then C. I would treat D and E as last resorts. A is the best fit because it fixes the root cause (125 litres a head), stays within your reserves, and handles the 19-day case.

## What to find out before November

1. **Meter the hut for a week** to get real figures for toilets, showers and kitchen. This is the most important single number.
2. **Talk to the Grauberg family** about their spring's surplus, their rights, and whether they would sell or share water. Walk the route with someone who can price a pipe.
3. **Get two quotes** from the helicopter company, one for the usual flight and one for a short hop from the farm. Ask about a contract rate.
4. **Ask the hydrologist** how long outages are likely to last in future, not only that they will happen. A 30-day outage changes the storage you need.
5. **Look for funding**, since alpine club federations and regional water or tourism programmes often support hut water supply. I can't tell you which apply to you.
6. **Price the cost of closing** from last August (refunds, cancelled groups, lost income) so members can compare every option against it.
