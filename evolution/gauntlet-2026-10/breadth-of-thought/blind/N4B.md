There are far more than two choices, and several of them cost a fraction of the borehole. The reason is in your own figures.

## What the numbers say

- You use about 125 litres per guest per day (6 m³ for 48 beds). That is a lot for a hut at 2,640 m. It is roughly what you'd expect after adding flush toilets and showers in 2016.
- Your 12 m³ tank holds only two days at full use, so any spring failure reaches the guests almost immediately.
- Flying water in at full occupancy needs about 7.5 rotations a day, roughly €8,250 a day. A 19-day outage would cost about €157,000, more than twice your reserves.
- Mid-August to 20 September you're at about 55%, so about 26 guests. At today's per-head use that is 3.3 m³ a day, or about €4,500 a day by helicopter. A repeat of last August would still cost around €85,000.
- Helicopter water is therefore a short-term bridge, not a supply.
- The borehole costs €140,000 against €75,000 in reserves. At a 60% chance of success, the expected cost of one working borehole is nearer €230,000. It may also draw on the same shrinking catchment as the spring, so a dry late summer could hit both.
- Meanwhile you waste water in June. The spring overflows when you have no use for it, and fails when you need it most.

The real problem is a mismatch in time and in demand. A reliable second source is one fix, but not the only one. You can also use less, store more, or buy a backup.

## The options

Costs are my rough estimates for scale. Get quotes before the AGM.

**1. Cut demand in the dry spell (about €3,000-15,000)**
- Switch the showers off, or make them token-operated, once the spring drops.
- Fit low-flush or waterless fittings, tap aerators and push taps.
- Offer a "dry mode" with drinking and kitchen water only, flush toilets for part of the day, and a clear notice in the booking terms.
- If you can get guests down to 40 litres a head, 26 guests need about 1 m³ a day, and your current tank covers about 12 days on its own.
- Weakness: it is a comfort downgrade, and members asked for these facilities in 2016. Staged rules, applied only when the tank level falls, are easier to defend than a permanent rollback.

**2. Store the June surplus (about €25,000-60,000 for 30-60 m³)**
- Add one or more insulated or buried cisterns beside the tank, filled while the spring overflows in late June and July.
- With 40-50 m³ of extra storage and dry-mode use, 19 days is covered without a helicopter.
- Check two things. First, whether the spring really fills the stores in time given the late-season decline. Second, the cost of getting the material up there, because that is largely helicopter flights.
- Weakness: it fails if the spring dwindles earlier or lasts longer than the stored volume allows.

**3. Pump from Grauberg as a backup (about €40,000-90,000 plus an agreement)**
- The alp is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower. The water has to go uphill, but solar pumping to 190 m is cheap: the energy needed for 3-6 m³ a day is only a few kWh. Your spare solar capacity can cover it.
- A surface-laid pipe that is drained each autumn avoids frost damage. A buried line would be a bigger project.
- You need a written deal with the farming family: how much they can spare in a dry August, what you pay, and whether the water supply is legally theirs to share. Their dairy has priority.
- An agreement and a pipe you rarely use is a better emergency plan than a helicopter, but only if their spring has real spare capacity at the end of summer.
- Variant: if no pipe is possible, use the same agreement to fill containers or bladders at the alp for a helicopter underslung load. This is far cheaper per load than flying water from the valley.

**4. Make the spring itself work better (about €5,000-20,000)**
- Have the hydrologist look at the intake. A deeper or relocated collection point, a better spring box or a drained catchment may give you more flow in the dwindling weeks.
- Fix leaks in the 300 m pipe and the tank. Even a few percent loss matters when you are down to the last days.
- Fit meters so you know where the 125 litres per guest go. If showers and toilets use half of it, the case for options 1 and 2 becomes clear.

**5. Look for a second surface source that rises in August (about €10,000-40,000)**
- Glacier and snowfield meltwater peaks in late summer while the spring falls. A stream below the snowfield or glacier might be tapped with a settling and filtration step.
- The risks are silt, flood damage to the intake, the need for treatment, and the legal position on use. Have the hydrologist say whether it is practical.

**6. Collect rainwater from the roofs (about €5,000-30,000)**
- Useful for toilets and washing. It is unreliable in the very dry weeks when you need it most, so treat it as a top-up.

**7. Change the season or the product (little capital, some lost income)**
- Add a water-shortage clause to bookings, including refund and rebooking terms. Last August's refunds and cancelled groups were the largest cost.
- Close early in the autumn only when the tank falls below a set level, rather than simply cancelling bookings.
- Sell a "basic" tariff for late-season stays.
- Occupancy after mid-August is already about 55%, so the revenue at risk is less than the headline figure.

**8. Bridge with helicopter water (pay as you go)**
- Agree a rate in advance and keep a reserve for this, limited to emergencies and to the first days of a shortage.
- Check whether water can go in underslung bags with other supply flights, which would lower the cost per rotation.

**9. Borehole, staged (€140,000, or less in stages)**
- Don't ask the AGM for a yes or no on the full drilling. Commission a cheaper geophysical or hydrogeological survey first and ask the geologist to improve the 60%.
- Ask for the quote in stages, and whether a failed hole carries any refund or reduced fee.
- Raise the funds first: club grants, regional alpine funds, members' donations. Reserves alone don't cover it.
- Keep it as a possible later step if the cheaper options fall short.

## Combinations to put to the AGM

1. **Low-cost resilience (about €30,000-50,000):** options 4 and 1, plus a modest cistern from option 2. This gets you through most outages by a combination of metering, careful use and extra storage. It keeps most of your reserves. Add the booking clause from option 7.
2. **Resilience with a backup (about €60,000-110,000):** option 1 and a larger version of option 2, plus the Grauberg line from option 3. Most robust, and it may need extra fundraising.
3. **Borehole path:** a survey, then a decision with funding in place, while options 1 and 4 run in the meantime.
4. **Minimum:** keep things as they are but add the booking clause and a helicopter standby agreement. It keeps the club's money but leaves you with another expensive August if the spring fails again.

## My recommendation

Don't put the borehole against the helicopter. Put option 1 (the low-cost package) before the AGM as the default, with Grauberg as the next step if the family agrees. Ask the hydrologist for the meter data and the intake review before November, and ask the Grauberg family one question: how much can you spare in a dry late August?

## What I'd check first

- How the 125 litres per guest split between showers, toilets, kitchen and drinking. This decides how far demand can fall.
- Whether the spring is dry for 19 days or has a long tail beforehand. That decides how big the storage has to be.
- The legal and practical position on water rights at Grauberg and at any glacier or snowfield source.
- The cost of getting cisterns and pipe up to the hut. A large share of the price may be helicopter freight.
