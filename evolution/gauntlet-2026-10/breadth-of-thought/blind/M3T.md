There are more realistic routes than buy or don't buy, and several of them are cheap enough to start before January. Some of the council's own land and paperwork may be worth more than the field.

## The size of the gap

- At 95 burials a year, 25 years needs about 2,400 grave spaces. You have 360, so the gap is roughly 2,000.
- Cremation will not close it. At 70%, 95 burials implies about 315 funerals a year. If cremation rose to 75%, burials would fall to about 80. That takes the gap from about 2,000 to about 1,600, and it has been flat for six years. Councillors shouldn't rely on it.
- The field is far bigger than you need. Fourteen hectares would hold something like 10,000 or more graves, which is a century or more of supply at your rate. The question is how to pay for 2,000 graves without paying for 10,000.

I've assumed the 360 count excludes the 380 reserved plots, that burials will stay near 95 a year, and that Ferney Bank is within reasonable reach of Sorrel Lane. Please check each of these with the bereavement officer.

## Realistic options

**1. Recover the unused reserved graves in the east section**

- The 380 reserved plots are about as many as your whole remaining stock.
- Most were sold in the 1960s and 70s on 75-year rights, so they expire between roughly 2035 and 2050, inside your planning window.
- Until a right expires or the holder surrenders it, you can't resell the plot. But you can trace holders and heirs, advertise, and offer a modest refund for surrender. You can also confirm exactly when each right lapses and what the deed and the 1977 Cemeteries Order require before re-granting.
- Expect some to be claimed by families, so not all 380 will come back. This buys years, not decades, and it costs little.
- Get legal advice on the notice requirements before writing to anyone.

**2. Find capacity inside Sorrel Lane**

- The north section is "full" on the plan, but a survey (including ground-penetrating radar) often finds unused ground along edges and paths, and graves dug deep enough for a further interment.
- Burying more in an existing grave needs the right holder's consent, or an expired right. Re-using old graves is restricted outside London, so take advice before assuming it.
- Any work there needs the Friends of Sorrel Lane on side. Their twice-yearly mowing means they may also care about wildlife and heritage.
- Treat this as a modest top-up, not a solution.

**3. Make the space you have go further**

- Sell only double- or triple-depth graves in new sections, so one plot takes two or three interments.
- Offer small plots for cremated remains, which take a fraction of the space. First check whether your 95 includes these.
- Set a non-resident fee and a clear family-plot policy.
- Review the £1,450 fee. It covers a 75-year right, and new land will cost more per grave than the old land did.
- Every grave stretched by these measures cuts the land you eventually need.

**4. Use Ferney Bank, the closed pitch-and-putt (3 hectares)**

- The council already owns it, so there is no purchase price.
- After paths, buffers and drainage, 3 hectares might yield around 1,500 to 2,500 graves, more with deeper graves. That is roughly the 2,000 you're short, though only a layout survey will say.
- Questions to settle:
  - Planning permission for change of use.
  - A groundwater and Environment Agency assessment. This is the usual make-or-break for new burial land.
  - Any covenants or grant conditions on the land.
  - The formal steps for moving it out of recreation use.
  - Site access.
  - Whether a second site changes how you staff and maintain.
- A natural or woodland burial section here could also earn income and lower the costs.
- This is the most promising route for long-term supply, subject to the groundwater result.

**5. Buy space from, or share with, Ashby Combe**

- Their 2017 extension is almost unused and three miles away.
- Options range from a simple agreement to send residents there, through buying a block of grave rights, to a joint arrangement or a transfer of part of the land.
- It brings income to them and breathing room to you.
- Find out why it's empty, and whether it has capacity, restrictions or a price in mind.
- Mourners would have to travel, and residents may resist leaving the town. It is a good bridge for the middle years.

**6. Buy the field, but restructure the deal**

- £1.15 million for 14 hectares is about £82,000 a hectare, which looks several times typical farmland prices. Ask for an independent valuation.
- You have £280,000, so borrowing of about £870,000 would be needed, with approval for a parish-level council to borrow. As a rough guide, over 40 years at around 5% that is about £50,000 a year. Spread across the town's council tax base, that might be around £9 per household. Please have the finance officer check this.
- Fee income is the offset. Even at a fraction of 95 new rights a year at £1,450, the cemetery earns real money. It must also cover upkeep.
- The all-or-nothing condition is the real problem. Ways to ease it:
  - Buy all 14 hectares and lease back or resell the surplus to the farmer, an allotment or community group, or for rewilding or solar.
  - Take an option to buy in phases.
  - Offer a price based on use as a cemetery rather than hoped-for development.
  - Tell the farmer you have compulsory purchase powers as a last resort.
- Buying the whole lot is workable only if most of it earns money or can be sold on.

**7. Other land**

- Ask the district council about burial land in the Local Plan, and about requiring cemetery land from nearby housing developments.
- Approach the diocese, other landowners or a neighbouring parish about a smaller parcel or a joint purchase.
- This is slower and less certain, but it removes the farmer's lever.

**8. Stop taking new burials**

- This is the floor. A council has the power to provide burials, not a duty.
- Residents would use Ashby Combe or elsewhere. You'd keep maintaining existing graves and lose fee income.
- It is a legitimate option but politically hard, and it should stand in the paper so councillors see what the alternative to action is.

## Options I set aside

- Re-using graves early. It isn't generally available outside London.
- Relying on cremation to rise.
- Waiting for the farmer to cut the price. He doesn't have to, and four years is short.

## How they compare

| Option | Spaces gained | Cost | Speed | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reserved graves | up to 380, over time | Low | Slow, starts now | Families claim plots |
| Inside Sorrel Lane | Small | Low | Quick | Legal limits, Friends |
| Stretch existing land | Multiplies whatever you have | Low | Quick | Public acceptance |
| Ferney Bank | About 1,500 to 2,500 | Moderate | 2 to 4 years | Groundwater, planning |
| Ashby Combe | Whatever they offer | Moderate | Quick | Travel, their terms |
| Buy the field | Over 10,000 | £1.15m, offset by resale | 1 to 3 years | Price, borrowing |
| Other land | Varies | Varies | Slow | Uncertain |
| Stop new burials | None | Lost income | Immediate | Public reaction |

## What I'd put to council in January

No single option closes the gap, so propose a staged plan:

1. **Now to 12 months.** Survey the reserved graves and the north section. Adopt deeper graves and a cremated-remains section. Review fees. Open talks with Ashby Combe.
2. **In parallel.** Commission feasibility work on Ferney Bank, including the groundwater assessment, and ask for an independent valuation of the field.
3. **Decide in about a year.** If Ferney Bank passes, it becomes the main route with Ashby Combe as a bridge. If it fails, return to a restructured field purchase, now with a valuation and a clear sense of the need.

The field stays on the table as the backstop rather than the first move. With four years of space and a staged plan, the council has time to ask for better terms.
