There are more realistic options than "buy the field or don't". Several of them cost little and some can start before January. I've assumed you're in England and that the council is the burial authority under the Local Authorities' Cemeteries Order 1977. Please check the legal points below with your solicitor or NALC before they go in a paper.

## First, test the "four years"

360 spaces at 95 burials a year gives about 3.8 years. That only holds if all 95 burials use a new space. Some will be second interments in existing family graves, and some will be cremated remains. Ask the bereavement officer how many of the 95 actually consume a new space. If it's 70, you have about five years. If it's 55, you have about six and a half. The answer also tells you how many spaces you need over 25 years. The ceiling is roughly 2,400 (95 x 25), and the true figure is probably lower.

Plan on cremation staying at about 70%. It has been flat for six years, and nothing in the figures supports waiting for it to rise.

## The options

**1. Recover unused reserved graves in the east section (up to 380 plots)**
- Those 380 plots are more than a year's worth of spaces beyond your 360 and are already laid out. I'm assuming the 360 doesn't include them, so please confirm.
- You can't cancel a right of burial before it expires. Rights sold in the 1960s and 70s for 75 years run out between about 2035 and 2054, so most of them fall due inside your 25-year window.
- Before then, you can write to the last known holders or their heirs, put notices on site, and offer a voluntary surrender with a refund. Councils do this, and it often returns a useful share. The share is unknown until you try.
- The mailing costs little. Even a modest return is worth having.
- You could also stop or limit new advance sales of plots, and cap the term on any new ones.

**2. Get more out of what you already own**
- Dig new graves to a greater depth, so each one takes two or three interments instead of one.
- Offer cremated-remains plots, which use a fraction of the ground, and price them to attract ashes interments.
- Survey the north section. "Full for a century" often still leaves gaps, edges and unused corners. Rights granted from 1888 on should all have expired by now, so ask what the position is on graves with room left at depth.
- Outside London, the law on reusing old graves is restrictive, so take advice before counting on this.
- The Friends of Sorrel Lane and any heritage or wildlife value on that land would need consulting.
- The depth and survey measures together could add real capacity cheaply. I can't put a number on them without the survey.

**3. Develop Ferney Bank (3 hectares, council-owned)**
- A conventional layout typically yields very roughly 800 to 1,200 graves a hectare before roads and paths. That makes about 2,000 to 3,000 graves, or around 20 to 30 years at your rate.
- It is the only option that could close the gap without a large purchase.
- It needs a ground and groundwater survey first. The Environment Agency will want to see the water table, nearby watercourses and any abstractions. A former golf course may have drainage or landfill history.
- It needs planning permission for change of use, and possibly formal appropriation of the land if it is held as public open space. Check for covenants or grant conditions.
- Allow for access, fencing, parking and paths.
- It is a separate site from Sorrel Lane, so it means a second site to maintain.
- Getting a feasibility report is cheap compared with everything else here.

**4. Buy the field, in one of several ways**
- *Buy all 14 hectares outright.* This is a long-term answer, with far more land than you will need for burials. You would borrow about £870,000 on top of the reserve. As a rough illustration, a 25-year loan at 5% costs roughly £62,000 a year to repay. You would need government borrowing approval.
- *Buy it all, then sell or lease back what you don't need.* This reduces the net cost, but see the price point below.
- *Negotiate a smaller or staged deal.* This could be 3 or 4 hectares now with an option on the rest, or a back-to-back sale in which another buyer takes the remainder. "Only as one lot" is often an opening position. Ask why he needs it. It may be tax or a wish to retire, and that may suggest a structure.
- *Price.* £1.15 million for 14 hectares is about £82,000 a hectare, or £33,000 an acre. As I understand it, that is well above ordinary farmland values, so he may be pricing in development hope. Get an independent valuation, and check the field's planning status and flood risk before talking numbers.

**5. Burial elsewhere through Ashby Combe**
- Their 2017 extension is almost unused, which suggests spare capacity three miles away.
- You could agree reserved blocks of plots, joint working, or a service agreement. A local council can contribute to another authority's burial ground.
- Find out how much capacity there really is, and why it has been so little used. The reason could be a poorly drained site, price, or a small catchment.
- The downsides are travel for mourners, the loss of a burial place in the town, and dependence on another council's decisions.
- It makes a good bridge while you build the longer-term answer.

**6. Let someone else provide it**
- The farmer, a private operator or the church could open a natural burial ground nearby.
- You would pay nothing, but you'd lose control of price and quality, and it may not materialise.
- The council could support it in the planning process.

**7. Natural burial or woodland section at Ferney Bank**
- This is a variation on option 3 that uses less formal infrastructure and may appeal to some families.
- Densities are usually lower than conventional layouts, so it adds fewer spaces. It is better as part of the mix than as the main supply.

**8. Fees and policy**
- Raise the non-resident rate if it isn't already higher than the resident one.
- Review the £1,450 exclusive right against neighbouring councils. Consider shorter terms with renewal, which bring plots back sooner.
- Set aside part of each fee in a sinking fund for future land.

## How they compare

| Option | Capacity | Cost | Time to deliver | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reclaim reserved graves | Up to 380; rest from 2035 | Very low | Months | Return rate unknown |
| Deeper graves, ashes plots, north survey | Unknown until surveyed | Low | Months | Legal limits on reuse |
| Ferney Bank | About 2,000 to 3,000 | Moderate (survey, works) | 2 to 3 years | Groundwater, planning, covenants |
| Buy the field | Far more than needed | About £1.15m | 2 to 4 years | Price, borrowing, planning |
| Ashby Combe | Unknown | Low to moderate | Months to a year | Capacity, other council's agreement |
| Private or church provider | Unknown | None | Uncertain | Not in your control |

## What I'd bring to council

Present it as a sequence rather than a choice between two things.

1. **Now, to the end of the year:** the true consumption figure, a reserved-grave campaign, a north-section survey, deeper-grave and fee changes, and a first conversation with Ashby Combe.
2. **Alongside:** commission the Ferney Bank feasibility and groundwater survey, and ask the farmer about a staged or smaller deal.
3. **A decision point once those results are in:** if Ferney Bank passes its survey and some reserved graves come back, you may cover most of the 25 years without buying anything. If it fails, you go to the field with evidence, and a stronger hand on price.

Even a cheap route takes two to three years to deliver, so work needs to start this year. You have about four years of spaces, and every option other than reclaiming plots and digging deeper needs that long to complete.
