There are well over two options, and the two on the table are the weakest. Neither addresses the real problem, which is that the hut uses far more water than a spring this size can reliably supply.

## What the numbers say

- **Your use per guest is high.** 6 m³ a day across 48 beds is about 125 litres per person per day. Huts with dry toilets and no showers typically run at 20 to 40 litres. The 2016 flush toilets and showers are almost certainly most of your demand. This is the biggest lever you have, and it costs far less than a borehole.
- **The tank is small compared with the risk.** 12 m³ is two days at full occupancy.
- **Helicopter water is not affordable as a main strategy.**
  - At full occupancy, 6 m³ a day is 7.5 rotations, or about €8,250 a day.
  - At the roughly 55% occupancy of late summer, it is about 3.3 m³ a day, 4 rotations, or about €4,500 a day.
  - A repeat of last August's 19-day outage would cost roughly €85,000. That is more than your reserves, and the cost would be paid every bad year.
- **The borehole is a poor bet as framed.**
  - It costs €140,000 against €75,000 in reserves.
  - It has a 60% chance of success, so there is a 40% chance of spending the money and getting nothing.
  - If the groundwater is fed by the same shrinking snowfield, it may fail in the same late-summer weeks as the spring.
  - Ask the geologist and hydrologist whether it would draw on a different source from the spring.
- **You have two resources you are not using.** One is the June overflow, which is wasted. The other is the solar surplus.

## Options

**1. Cut demand (cheapest, fastest)**
- Return to dry or composting toilets, at least in part. Alternatively, use low-flush or vacuum toilets.
- Put the showers on tokens or timers, or limit them to certain hours. Alternatively, switch them off from mid-August, or in any declared water shortage.
- Fit tap aerators and push-button taps.
- Rough target: 40 to 50 litres per guest per day. That is about 2.4 m³ a day at full occupancy and about 1.3 m³ a day at 55%. Your current 12 m³ tank would then last 5 to 9 days instead of 2.
- Cost: modest, in the low tens of thousands of euros, depending on how much sanitation you change. Get quotes.

**2. Store more water**
- Add storage to capture the June overflow and top it up through July.
- At roughly 1.3 m³ a day, a 19-day outage in late summer needs about 25 m³. A 30 to 40 m³ buried or insulated cistern plus the existing tank would cover that.
- This works best combined with option 1. Without demand reduction you would need about 60 m³ or more.
- Check how long the spring actually has surplus. If July is also thin, storage will not fill.
- It needs a helicopter lift for materials. Get a quote.

**3. Draw water from Grauberg**
- Grauberg is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower, and its spring has never failed. Water would have to be pumped up, not piped down.
- Your spare solar capacity makes this plausible. At 2 to 3 m³ a day, lifting about 200 m takes only a few kWh a day.
- It would need an agreement with the farming family, covering how much they can spare, a fee or shared costs, and legal rights.
- It would need a buried or insulated pipeline, drained each autumn, and a permit.
- Two tests to run first: does their spring have headroom beyond the dairy, and what does a quote for the pipeline and pump come to?
- It could also work as a backup that you switch on only in dry spells, rather than a permanent supply.

**4. Improve the existing supply**
- Have the hydrologist look at the spring catchment. Could the intake be deepened, or could you tap other springs or seeps nearby?
- Add roof rainwater collection, which helps in thunderstorm weather.
- Recycle greywater from showers and basins into toilet flushing. This is only worth doing if you keep the flush toilets, and it needs solar power for pumping and treatment.

**5. Adjust the season and the commitments**
- Occupancy falls from mid-August anyway. You could plan an earlier close, for example around 31 August or mid-September. This is cheaper than an unplanned closure.
- Add water-shortage terms to booking conditions so you can offer a reduced service, such as no showers or bring-your-own water, instead of refunding everything.
- Reduced service in a dry spell could keep the hut open and the income coming in.
- Consider insurance or a pre-agreed rule for when to close.

**6. Helicopter as a backstop only**
- Keep this as a small contingency, not a plan. With demand cut to about 1.3 m³ a day, a shortage costs about 2 rotations a day, roughly €2,200 a day. A short gap becomes tolerable.

**7. Borehole, postponed or de-risked**
- Do not decide in November. Ask for a cheaper hydrogeological survey first, or a staged or smaller test bore. Look for external funding, such as club federation or regional grants for water infrastructure.
- Revisit it only if options 1 to 4 turn out not to be enough.

## Packages for the AGM

- **A. Lean (low cost):** options 1 and 5, plus helicopter as backstop. This is the cheapest and quickest to deliver, but with no storage it leaves a thin margin in a long outage.
- **B. Resilient (my suggested lead):** options 1, 2 and 4, plus 5 and 6. Demand falls, summer overflow is captured, and you have a margin. Probably fits within the €75,000 reserves, but this needs costing.
- **C. Secure supply:** option 3 added to B, or in place of storage if Grauberg agrees. This gives the strongest protection if the climate trend continues.
