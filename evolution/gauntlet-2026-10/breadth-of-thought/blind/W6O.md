You have far more than two choices. The main thing I'd put to members is that the two proposals only attack supply, and your own numbers show the bigger lever is how much water the hut uses and how much it can store.

## What your numbers say

- **Use per guest.** 6 m³ a day for 48 beds is about 125 litres per person per day. Huts with flush toilets and showers usually manage on well under half of that, and huts on dry toilets on much less. The 2016 upgrade probably roughly doubled your demand.
- **Storage.** The 12 m³ tank is two days of water at full occupancy. When the spring fails, you are in trouble within 48 hours.
- **The helicopter at today's use is not a real plan.** Take a 19-day outage in the second half of the season, at about 55% occupancy (26 guests):

| Water use per guest per day | Total need over 19 days | After using a full 12 m³ tank | Helicopter rotations | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 125 L (today) | about 63 m³ | about 51 m³ | about 64 | about €70,000 |
| 40 L (water-saving mode) | about 20 m³ | about 8 m³ | about 10 | about €11,000 |
| 15 L (drinking and cooking only, dry toilets) | about 7.5 m³ | 0 | 0 | €0 |

  At today's use, one bad August would swallow nearly all of your €75,000 reserves, and your hydrologist expects such Augusts to become the norm. At full occupancy it would cost even more.
- **The borehole** costs almost twice your reserves. It has a 40% chance of finding nothing. It may also draw on the same snowmelt-fed groundwater that is now declining, so ask the hydrologist whether a successful borehole would stay reliable in the dry years you are worried about.

## The options

**A. Cut demand permanently (low cost, fast).**
- Fit push-button or timed taps and flow restrictors.
- Put showers on tokens or a small fee.
- Fit low-flush or vacuum toilets, or return some toilets to dry or composting.
- Rough cost: a few thousand euros to the low tens of thousands. Please get quotes.
- Getting to around 40 L per person per day would roughly cut the helicopter exposure in the table above by 85%. Guests may complain, as they did before 2016, but a €3 shower that works beats a refund and a closure.

**B. Store the June surplus (moderate cost).**
- The spring floods the path in late June and is reliably strong early in the season. Capture that surplus in a much larger buried or insulated store, for example 30 to 40 m³ on top of the existing tank.
- Combined with A, a full store covers most or all of a 19-day outage with no flights.
- My rough guess is €15,000 to €35,000 including delivery by helicopter. Treat that as a placeholder until you have a quote.
- It only works if the spring refills the store in July. Your records of flow by week should show this.

**C. Draw from the Grauberg spring (potentially the strongest supply fix, but it needs their agreement).**
- Their spring has never failed, and they are friendly.
- The alp is 190 m below you, so the water has to be pumped uphill. The energy is small, a few kWh a day, and your spare solar can cover it.
- The real costs are about 1.1 km of high-pressure pipe, the pump station and the legal side.
- Rough build cost: €60,000 to €120,000. That figure is a guess.
- A cheaper version is a seasonal, drought-only hose and pump set. Check that it is permitted and robust enough for the pressure.
- Before November, someone should simply ask the family four things: their spring's lowest-ever flow, their own needs, whether they would sell or share, and what water rights apply.
- Even if C is not built now, a written agreement as an emergency backstop is worth having.

**D. Add a second source on your own ground.**
- Have the hydrologist look for other seeps, a deeper or better spring capture, or leaks in the 300 m pipe run. A leaking intake can look like a dwindling spring.
- If the glacier or its meltwater stream is close, its flow tends to peak in late summer when the snowfield spring fades. An intake there needs settling and filtration or UV treatment, and a safety review of the stream.
- Cost can be small if the capture only needs improving, and much higher if a new pipe run is needed.

**E. Collect rainwater and recycle greywater.**
- Roof runoff can serve the flush toilets, so potable water is kept for drinking, cooking and washing.
- Shower and basin water can also be reused for flushing. A small treatment unit for this would likely cost tens of thousands of euros.
- It reduces total demand without changing guest comfort much, but it adds a system to maintain at altitude.

**F. A planned drought mode (almost free).**
- Set triggers, for example spring flow or tank level falling below a threshold. Past that point the hut switches to a water-saving routine: showers off, portable or dry toilets, bottled or carried drinking water.
- It could also mean reduced bed numbers, with guests warned in advance.
- Add a cheap flow meter and tank level sensor, powered by your solar, so you get early warning.
- Add a water-restrictions clause to booking terms, so a shortage means reduced service rather than refunds.

**G. Adjust the season.**
- Shorten the end of the season, or run it at reduced capacity after mid-August when guiding companies stop booking the glacier route anyway.
- Alternatively, open earlier if the approach allows, to use the strong June water.
- You'd lose some revenue, but a planned partial closure costs less than an emergency one with refunds and cancelled groups. You know your August income better than I do, so cost it out before the AGM.

**H. Drill the borehole, but stage it.**
- Before spending €140,000, commission a cheaper geophysical survey to improve the 60% odds.
- Ask the driller for staged payment or a lower price if no water is found.
- Look for grants: your national alpine club's hut fund, regional water or tourism development money.
- The borehole stays on the table, but as a later step rather than the first one.

**I. Helicopter water as a backstop only.**
- It makes sense as an insurance layer once demand is down, not as a main plan.
- Check whether a water-bag rotation can be bundled with your normal supply flights at a lower marginal price.

## What I'd put to the AGM

Offer members a staged package, with a clear choice at the end.

1. **Now (about €20,000 to €50,000):** options A, F and B, with sensors. This makes the existing system survive a normal bad August and caps the helicopter bill at a few thousand euros.
2. **Before November:** call the Grauberg family, ask the hydrologist the groundwater question and request flow records, and get quotes for storage and the water-saving retrofit. Option C could then be costed properly as the long-term answer.
3. **Later, if needed:** the survey and grant search for the borehole, or the Grauberg pipeline.

This gives members a real choice between a demand-and-storage route, a Grauberg route and a borehole route, not just two expensive options.

## Things I assumed or don't know

- I have assumed the 55% occupancy and 125 L figure apply to the whole period of the outage.
- I don't know how low the Grauberg spring gets, how much storage your site allows, or whether the glacier stream is nearby. All costs above are my rough estimates, not quotes.
- I'd check them first with the hydrologist and the Grauberg family.
