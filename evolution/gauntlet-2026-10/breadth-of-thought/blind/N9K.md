You have far more than two options, and a few of them look stronger than the two the committee is excited about. First, some arithmetic that changes how to read those two.

**What the numbers say**

- Your 12 m³ tank covers about 2 days at full occupancy, or about 3.5 days at the 55% you see from mid-August. It cannot ride out a 19-day gap.
- Helicopter water works out at about €1,375 per m³ (€1,100 for 0.8 m³). At 55% occupancy you use roughly 3.3 m³ a day, so that is about €4,500 a day. Covering a 19-day gap like last August's would cost about €85,000 if it all fell in the 55% period, and more if any of it fell earlier. That is above your €75,000 reserves, so flying water in is a stopgap for a few days, not a plan for a recurring gap.
- The borehole is €140,000 against €75,000 in reserves, so you can't fund it without a large outside contribution. It also has a 40% chance of finding nothing usable, and the money is spent either way.
- Your real asset is the June overflow. You have more water than you can use in June and too little in late August. That is a storage and demand problem as much as a supply problem.
- You use about 125 litres per guest per day. That is high for a hut at this altitude, and the 2016 flush toilets and showers are the likely reason. I'm assuming that, since I don't have a breakdown.

**Options**

1. **Cut demand (cheapest, fastest).** Meter the toilets, showers, kitchen and laundry separately this season so you know where the water goes. Then choose from:
   - Shower tokens or time limits.
   - Closing the showers during shortages.
   - Low-flush or waterless urinals.
   - Dry or composting toilets in the toilet block as a fallback mode.

   If you could get to about 70 litres per guest, use would be about 3.4 m³ at full occupancy and about 1.8 m³ at 55%. That makes every other option smaller and cheaper. Guests did ask for the flush toilets and showers, so this has a comfort and booking cost, and you would need to communicate it well.

2. **Store the June surplus.** Add a large insulated or buried tank, or several, fed by the overflow that now floods the path. Fill it in June and July and draw it down in late summer. At demand-cut levels of about 2 m³ a day, 25 to 30 m³ of extra storage covers roughly two weeks. Costs depend on site and on how the tank is delivered. If tanks have to be flown in, get quotes before the AGM. This is the option that most directly fits your seasonal pattern.

3. **Pipe or pump water from the Grauberg spring.** The alp is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower, so water would have to be pumped up. Lifting 3 m³ a day by 190 m takes only a couple of kWh a day in theory, and your spare solar capacity should handle that easily. The pipe route, its cost, the pumping lift and friction losses, and the right to take the water all need a proper survey. The family's goodwill and a written agreement matter more than the engineering. Because their spring has never failed, this is the only option that adds a source you know is reliable. Ask them what volume they could spare in late summer, and whether they'd want something in return such as a fee, a share of the infrastructure, or guest traffic.

4. **Carry or fly water from Grauberg instead of the hut's spring.** If a pipe is too costly or can't be permitted, a short hop from the alp may cost less per rotation than your current quote. Get a quote for that specific route. This is still a helicopter-priced option, so it only helps with shorter gaps.

5. **Improve the existing source.** Have your hydrologist check whether the spring capture can be moved, deepened or extended to a lower or second outlet, and whether other seeps or streams exist above the hut. Also check whether meltwater from the snowfield can be diverted into storage in early summer. This is cheap to investigate and could add several days of supply.

6. **Rainwater and greywater.** Collect roof runoff for flushing, and treat shower and basin water for reuse in the toilets. This is a partial measure. Flushing is likely a large share of use, so reusing greywater there could free up a meaningful volume, but it adds treatment equipment and upkeep that a hut warden has to run.

7. **Adjust the season and bookings.** Shorten the season (for example, close from late August or early September in dry years), cap beds in late summer, or offer a reduced-water mode with guests informed at booking. This costs revenue, but a planned partial closure is cheaper and kinder to guests than the refunds and cancelled groups you had last year. Since occupancy already falls to 55% from mid-August, the revenue lost may be smaller than it looks.

8. **Helicopter as a backstop only.** Keep it for the first few days of a shortage while other measures take effect, and budget for it. At demand-cut levels (about 1.8 m³ a day) it costs about €2,500 a day, so a full 19-day gap would be about €47,000.

9. **Borehole, but later or cheaper.** Defer it. Revisit it if the other measures fall short, if you can get a cheaper quote, or if funding appears (alpine club federations, regional or national mountain-hut grants, or sponsors). Ask whether the geologist can give a clearer estimate from a survey before any drilling money is committed.

**How I'd put it to the members**

The strongest package is options 1, 2 and 3 together, with 8 as the backstop and 5 as a quick check. Together they use the June surplus, reduce what you need, and add a reliable source, all for less than the borehole and without a 40% chance of losing the money. You can phase them: meter and cut demand this season, get quotes for storage and the Grauberg pipe over the winter, and build what you can afford before next July.

If members prefer one choice by priority:

- **Lowest cost and quickest:** demand reduction, plus the helicopter for emergencies.
- **Best fit for the seasonal pattern:** large storage fed by the June overflow.
- **Most reliable supply:** the Grauberg pipe, once the agreement and the survey are in place.
- **Best if funding appears:** the borehole, accepting the 40% risk.

**What to find out before November**

- How much water each use takes (metering, or a rough audit).
- Quotes for tank storage and delivery.
- A survey and quote for the Grauberg pipe, and a first conversation with the family.
- Whether a shorter helicopter hop from the alp is cheaper.
- What your hydrologist thinks about the spring capture and any other water above the hut.
- Which grants or club funds could contribute to any of this.

One caution: the committee is describing the reserves as a strong position. They cover one of these projects or a short helicopter spell, not a full recurrence of last August plus a capital project, so I'd present the plan as a sequence rather than a single large spend.
