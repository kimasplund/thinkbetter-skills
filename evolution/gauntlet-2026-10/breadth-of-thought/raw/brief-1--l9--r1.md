You have far more than two options, and the numbers suggest the two on the table are the weakest. Below is a menu you could put to members, with rough sums. All costs other than your quotes are my order-of-magnitude assumptions and need real quotes.

**First, the numbers that frame everything**

- 6 m³/day for 48 beds is about 125 L per guest per day. Most alpine huts with dry toilets and no showers run at 15 to 30 L. Your 2016 upgrade (flush toilets and showers) is almost certainly what turned a manageable demand into a fragile one.
- After mid-August you're at about 55% occupancy, roughly 26 guests. At your current per-guest use that's about 3.3 m³/day, so the shortage falls when demand is already lower.
- Helicopter water: 6 m³/day is 7.5 rotations, about €8,250 per day. Even at 55% occupancy (4 rotations, about €4,500 per day), a repeat of last August's 19 dry days costs roughly €85,000, more than your reserves. A 12 m³ tank is only two days at full load.
- Borehole: €140,000 for a 60% chance means an expected cost of about €233,000 per working well. It also exceeds your €75,000 reserves, so it needs funding you haven't described. Even if it works, its yield could fall too, since it may draw on the same shrinking snowfield system.

**The options**

1. **Cut demand (cheapest, fastest, and it makes every other option smaller).**
   - Go back to dry or composting toilets, at least in a drought mode. Flushing is probably the largest single use.
   - Fit waterless urinals, push-taps, and low-flow fittings. Make showers token-based, scheduled, or available only when the tank is above a set level.
   - A target of 40 L per guest per day means 1.9 m³/day at full house and about 1 m³/day at 55%. A 19-day gap would then need about 20 m³ in late season instead of about 60 m³.
   - Cost: low (a few thousand to tens of thousands of euros). The trade-off is guest comfort, and you removed these facilities because guests complained, so frame it well.

2. **Store the June surplus.**
   - The spring floods the path in late June and the overflow is wasted. A bigger tank or a buried cistern, say 40 to 60 m³, would capture it. This is the option that matches the actual problem, which is timing rather than total volume.
   - Caveat: the spring may be low by the time the store is needed, but it can be filled all through July and early August. Check the real hydrograph, because storage only helps if the spring runs above demand for long enough to fill it.
   - Cost: maybe €30,000 to €80,000 depending on site, frost protection and permits. Combine with option 1 and you may need much less.

3. **Rainwater and meltwater harvesting.** Collect from the roof and other hard surfaces into the same storage, with treatment for drinking water. This is a useful supplement but not a full answer, because late-summer rainfall is unreliable. Cost is low to moderate.

4. **Greywater recycling.** Treat shower and sink water and reuse it for flushing. With spare solar capacity you can power it. It can cut net demand substantially, but it adds complexity, maintenance and a risk of failure at altitude, and it needs a warden or volunteers who can run it. Moderate to high cost.

5. **Take water from Grauberg.**
   - Their spring has never failed and they're friendly. The issue is that it's 190 m lower, so you'd be pumping uphill over 1.1 km. Your spare solar could drive that pump.
   - Variants: (a) a permanent pipeline and pump station, probably expensive and needing easements and permits; (b) a seasonal or emergency arrangement where you buy water and carry it in tanks by vehicle or sling load; (c) a shared agreement, with you paying for their infrastructure in return for a drought allocation.
   - First check how much they can spare, whether their spring also dwindles in a dry year, and what they would want. A written agreement matters, since goodwill alone is not reliable. Cost ranges from low (an agreement) to high (a pipeline).

6. **Extend the spring source.** Have your hydrologist check whether a better capture, a second catchment or a collection gallery at the spring would improve late-season yield. Cheap to investigate, uncertain gain.

7. **Run the season to match the water.**
   - Cap numbers or close earlier, for example to about 31 August or mid-September, when the glacier route is crevassed and guides stop booking anyway.
   - Make closure a planned, advertised decision with a water-level trigger. Last year's refunds and cancelled groups are the cost you are trying to avoid, so a planned shorter season with no refunds beats an unplanned closure. The cost is lost revenue, which in late season is limited.

8. **Helicopter as a backstop only.** Keep it as insurance, not a strategy. Set a budget (say 4 to 6 rotations a season, about €5,000 to €7,000), and agree trigger levels in advance. Consider a reserve earmarked for it.

9. **Staged borehole.** Don't commit €140,000 up front. Commission a hydrogeological survey and maybe a cheaper test drilling first, and seek grants (regional, national, alpine club federation funding). Treat it as a later option if the cheaper measures fail.

**Suggested packages for the AGM**

- **A. Frugal:** 1 + 7 + 8. Lowest cost, accepts some guest-comfort loss and some risk.
- **B. Resilient (my likely pick):** 1 + 2 + 3, with 8 as backup. It addresses timing, reduces demand and fits your reserves.
- **C. Partnership:** 1 + 5 + 8. It depends on the Grauberg family and a pump/pipeline design.
- **D. Big capital:** staged borehole (9) plus 1, only if funding is found.
- **E. Status quo plus helicopter:** include this for honesty, with the €85,000 worst-case figure next to it.

**What I'd do before November**

- Get the spring's daily flow record for the past five years if one exists, and pull out the dry spells.
- Meter your actual water use by purpose (toilets, showers, kitchen) for a few weeks.
- Ask Grauberg informally about capacity and terms.
- Get two quotes for storage, and ask the hydrologist what yield to expect in a dry year.

Assumptions: I've assumed no existing water-use meters, current tank frost protection, and that grants may be available. If any of these is wrong, the ranking of options changes, but demand reduction plus storage should still come first.
