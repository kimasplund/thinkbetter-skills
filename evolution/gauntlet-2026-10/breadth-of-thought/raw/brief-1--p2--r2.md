There are well over two choices, and several of them look better than either proposal on your numbers. Below is the full set, with the arithmetic behind it. The figures I had to estimate are flagged.

## What the numbers say about the two proposals

**Flying water in.** At full occupancy you use 6 m³/day, which is 7.5 rotations a day, or about €8,250 a day. Your 12 m³ tank lasts only 2 days at that rate. Covering a 19-day outage at full occupancy would cost roughly €157,000. At the 55% late-season occupancy (about 3.3 m³/day) it is still about €4,500 a day, or roughly €86,000 for 19 days. Either figure is more than your whole reserve. Helicopter water only works as a small top-up, not as the way you cover a shortage.

**Borehole.** €140,000 is nearly twice your reserves. With a 60% chance of success, the expected cost of getting one working borehole is about €233,000. A 40% chance of spending €140,000 and getting nothing is a hard thing to put to members unless the money comes from outside the club.

The better question is how to make the hut need much less water, or how to bring in water that costs far less than flying it. Both are possible.

## The options

**1. Cut demand: dry toilets back in, showers metered or limited**
Flush toilets and showers are almost certainly the largest share of your 6 m³/day, and they date from 2016. Drinking, cooking and washing-up for 48 guests is a small fraction of that. I'm assuming, without data, that toilets and showers account for roughly half. Metered showers, low-flow fittings, and either composting/dry toilets or a switch to dry toilets in shortage weeks could plausibly bring use down to about 3 m³/day.
- Strengths: cheap, quick, works whatever the spring does, and reduces the load on every other option.
- Weaknesses: guests complained about the old toilets, so expect pushback. A partial version (low-flush plus metered showers) gives a smaller saving.
- Best as: the base layer of any plan.

**2. Store the June surplus: much more tank capacity**
The spring floods the path in late June and fails in August, so the problem is timing as much as supply. Your 12 m³ tank is full before opening and cannot hold the surplus. If demand is cut to about 3 m³/day (option 1), a 19-day outage needs roughly 57 m³. That means about 45 m³ of additional storage, such as several insulated or buried tanks. I haven't priced it, so get quotes, but it is very likely well under the borehole cost. Tanks can also be flown in.
- Strengths: attacks the actual mismatch between supply and demand, and needs no new water source.
- Weaknesses: it only helps if the spring refills the tanks at some point during the season. If a dry year starts early, the stored volume may be partly used before the outage. Needs hygiene and frost management.
- Best as: the second layer, paired with option 1.

**3. A shortage mode instead of closing**
Last August you closed for 11 days and refunded bookings and cancelled guided groups. The alternative is a published "water-short" mode: drinking and cooking water only, portable or dry toilets, no showers, perhaps reduced bed numbers. Drinking and cooking is perhaps 10 L per person per day. For 48 guests that is about 0.5 m³/day, roughly one helicopter rotation a day (about €1,100) or less if you cap numbers, instead of €8,250 for normal operation.
- Strengths: the helicopter becomes affordable. You keep the bookings, the guided groups and the income.
- Weaknesses: needs clear booking terms so guests know what they are buying, and some will cancel. Comfort drops for a couple of weeks.
- Best as: the safety net that makes the helicopter proposal sensible.

**4. Buy or share water from the Grauberg alp**
Their spring has never failed, they are 1.1 km away, and the family is friendly. Their spring is lower than the hut, so you would need a pipeline and a pump. Lifting 6 m³ by 190 m takes only a few kWh a day, which your spare solar capacity can supply, so the energy is not the obstacle. The obstacles are the pipeline cost, land and path rights, frost protection, and whether the farm's own needs and dairy rules leave room to sell you water.
- Strengths: a reliable second source with no drilling risk, and potentially a lasting relationship with the farm.
- Weaknesses: pipeline cost is unknown and could be large. It depends on one family and on landowner and water permits. A pipe on a mountain route is also exposed to rockfall and frost.
- A cheaper version: ask the farm for a supply agreement for emergencies and fly water from there. Shorter hops should cost less per rotation than your current flights. Ask your helicopter operator.
- Best as: the longer-term option if the cheaper layers prove insufficient. A conversation with the farm costs nothing and should happen before November.

**5. Greywater recycling**
Treat shower and washing water and reuse it for toilet flushing. In a hut with flush toilets and showers this could reduce fresh-water use by perhaps a quarter to a third.
- Strengths: keeps the comfort level, and uses solar power you already have.
- Weaknesses: more equipment to maintain at altitude by a volunteer warden, treatment and discharge rules, and installation cost.
- Best as: a middle path if members will not give up flush toilets.

**6. Collect rain and extra meltwater**
Roof catchment and a second intake on other nearby streams or snowmelt can add water on top of the main spring. The roof will not supply much on its own, but in combination with storage it adds volume cheaply. Ask your hydrologist whether a second source is feasible in the same catchment, and whether the spring's own intake can be improved to capture more of what it yields in late summer.
- Strengths: low cost, and it helps option 2 fill.
- Weaknesses: modest volumes, and it depends on weather.
- Best as: a top-up.

**7. A smaller, staged borehole decision**
Instead of voting on the full €140,000, ask the geologist what a cheaper first stage (a survey, or a test hole) would cost and how much it changes the 60%. Look for external funding (the national alpine club, regional and mountain-hut grants). Defer the drilling decision until the cheaper options have been tried.
- Strengths: keeps the borehole possible without staking the reserve on a coin flip.
- Weaknesses: delays an answer, and the grant route is uncertain.

**8. Adjust the business side: booking terms, season and pricing**
Water-conditions wording in the booking terms, a water surcharge or shower token fee, or a lower bed cap from mid-August. Occupancy is already about 55% from then, so the late season is already the lighter period. These do not create water but they cut the cost of a shortage and fix the refund exposure.

## Options I would drop

- **Making snow into water with the solar array.** It takes roughly 90 kWh per m³ and relies on snow being there in late summer, which is exactly when the snowfield is shrinking.
- **Closing earlier or shortening the season.** The shortage is in the middle of the season, and this loses income for no extra water.
- **Fog or dew collection, or trucking water.** Not realistic at 2,640 m with foot-only access.

## How to choose

- **Lowest cost and quickest:** options 1, 3 and 8.
- **Keep guest comfort as it is:** options 5 and 2, then 4.
- **Most secure supply long-term:** option 4, and possibly 7.
- **Use the least reserve money:** options 1, 3, 6 and 8.

## What I would put to the AGM

Rather than two proposals, offer a package with a staged decision:

1. **Now:** cut demand (option 1), publish a shortage mode (option 3), and add booking terms (option 8). This makes the helicopter affordable as a supplement.
2. **Over winter:** price extra storage (option 2) and talk to the Grauberg family (option 4). Get the helicopter quote for a shorter route.
3. **Only afterwards:** decide on pipeline, greywater or a borehole, using the real quotes, with external funding sought first.

The comparison for members is that the helicopter as sole plan could cost €86,000 to €157,000 in a bad year, and the borehole is a 40% chance of losing €140,000. Several of the options above cost a fraction of either and address the shortfall directly.
