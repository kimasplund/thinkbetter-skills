There are more than two options. Several are cheaper than the field, and a few of them combine well. I've assumed you are an English parish or town council, that the 360 spaces are unreserved and separate from the 380 reserved plots, and that all figures are rough and need checking by your bereavement officer and solicitor.

## The numbers behind the brief

- 25 years at 95 burials a year is about 2,375 burials. After the 360 spaces you still need roughly 2,000 more.
- A typical planning figure is 1,000 to 1,500 single graves per hectare, including paths and access. So the 25-year need is about 2 to 3 hectares, not 14. The whole field would last well over a century at your current rate.
- At £1.15m, the field costs about £82,000 per hectare. That is usually several times the price of ordinary farmland, so the price probably includes hope value for development. An independent valuation would help your negotiating position.
- After using the whole £280,000 reserve, you would borrow about £870,000. At roughly 5% over 40 years, that is about £50,000 a year in repayments, or about £8 to £9 per household if you have around 6,000 households. Parish borrowing also needs government consent.
- Cremation can't fix this. If it rose from 70% to 75% of funerals, burials would fall to about 79 a year and the 360 spaces would last about 4.6 years. At 80% they would last about 5.7 years. It helps at the margin but doesn't change the answer.
- Lead time matters. A new burial area needs planning permission, a groundwater survey, drainage, access and layout, which typically takes 2 to 3 years. With about four years of space left, the council needs to decide during 2027.
- One thing to check: 95 burials is 30% of funerals, which implies about 315 funerals a year. That is a lot for a town of 14,000. If many come from outside Wickham Lees, that affects who should pay and who might share the cost.

## The realistic options

**1. Buy the whole field (£1.15m)**
- It would solve the problem for generations and give the council full control.
- The catch is cost: it takes all your reserves and adds about £50,000 a year of debt. It buys about six times the land you need.
- It gets much better if you can recover money from the surplus, as in option 2.

**2. Buy the field and pass on the surplus**
- Buy it all, then resell, lease or transfer about 10 to 12 hectares. Possible takers include the farmer on a leaseback, the district council, a land trust, allotments, or community woodland.
- This cuts the net cost sharply but depends on resale value. If the price includes hope value, you may not recover what you paid for the surplus.
- Test this with the farmer first. "Only as one lot" is a negotiating position, and a third party buying the rest may satisfy it. Ask about an option agreement as well.

**3. Use Ferney Bank (3 hectares, already council-owned)**
- On the planning figure, 3 hectares is enough for about 25 years or more, with no purchase cost.
- Check these first: soil and groundwater, planning use, access and distance from town, and whether the land is held as open space or under covenants. Taking land out of recreational use may need public notice and consultation.
- This is probably the strongest single option, but only if the survey comes back clean. A former golf course is often well suited to a natural burial area.

**4. Get more from the land you already have**
- Survey the north section. It is "full" because of the grave records, but old family graves may have unused depth or space between plots, and a ground-penetrating radar survey can show where. Gains may be modest, but they cost little and the surveys are cheap.
- Dig all new graves to double depth, which gives two burials per plot. This roughly doubles what your 360 spaces can hold for new sales, if the ground allows. Check the water table.
- Put cremated remains in small plots and in existing graves where the right-holders consent.
- Together these might add a few years, not 25.

**5. Recover the 380 reserved graves in the east section**
- These were sold in the 1960s and 70s on 75-year rights, so the oldest rights lapse from about 2035 and the rest by the early 2050s. Check each deed and the 1977 cemeteries order, including renewal and refund terms.
- Many holders will be dead and the heirs hard to trace. Writing to holders, advertising, and offering a refund or surrender could return some earlier.
- This gives real capacity in the 2030s and 2040s but does nothing for the next four years. It is a supporting option, not a rescue.

**6. Reuse old graves**
- The north section is a century old and the original rights have expired. But outside London, local authorities generally have no power to reuse graves, and the Church of England has its own separate procedure.
- Take legal advice before putting it in the paper. It is not a realistic route within four years, but you could note it as a long-term lobbying issue.

**7. Partner with Ashby Combe**
- Their extension has had hardly any burials since 2017, so there may be spare capacity three miles away.
- An agreement could cover reserved spaces, a resident fee, or a joint burial committee. This could be the cheapest option of all.
- Find out why their extension is underused. It could be location, waterlogging, local preference or a different catchment, and any of those could affect your residents too. Local people may also resist burying outside the town.

**8. Share the cost with the other parishes you serve**
- If many of the 95 burials come from outside Wickham Lees, a joint committee funded by those parishes, or a non-resident fee surcharge, could pay for the new site.
- A higher fee for non-residents can also reduce demand a little.

**9. Hand it to someone else**
- A private natural burial operator, a charitable trust, or the district council could provide the new capacity.
- If the farmer sells to anyone, this could happen without you. It reduces the council's financial risk but also its control over price and location.

**10. Plan a managed end to new burials**
- Sorrel Lane would close to new burials in about four years, or later with options 4 and 5, and you would keep maintaining it. Residents would use Ashby Combe or other cemeteries.
- This is the cheapest option for the council but the hardest for residents, particularly for families wanting a local burial. It is worth presenting as a real option so that the cost of the alternatives is visible.

**11. Compulsory purchase of part of the field**
- A council can in principle seek compulsory purchase for burial land, but it needs ministerial consent and is rarely used.
- It isn't a plan, but the possibility may help negotiations about splitting the lot.

Raising fees helps with money but not with space. At 95 burials, £1,450 per right brings in about £138,000 a year, so a fee review and a ring-fenced fund for new burial land is worth proposing alongside any of the above.

## How they fit together

Three workable packages for the council to choose between:

- **Cheapest and fastest:** Ferney Bank (3), plus double-depth graves and the north section survey (4), plus reclaiming reserved graves as they lapse (5). This is the lowest-cost route with a good chance of covering 25 years.
- **Most secure:** buy the field and pass on the surplus (2), with Ferney Bank held in reserve if the survey is positive. This costs the most and carries the most risk if resale falls short.
- **Lowest cost, least control:** a deal with Ashby Combe (7) plus cost sharing with other parishes (8), keeping Sorrel Lane for existing rights and ashes.

## What to find out before January

1. A soil and groundwater survey, and the planning, covenant and open-space position, for Ferney Bank.
2. An independent valuation of the field, and whether the farmer would take a split sale or an option.
3. How many spaces the north section survey and double-depth graves would add.
4. A list of the 380 reserved graves by year of grant, with the number of holders traceable.
5. What is happening at Ashby Combe, why it is underused, and whether they would take Wickham Lees residents.
6. Where the 95 burials a year come from.
7. The council's borrowing position and what consent it would need.

My own view is that "buy the field or don't" is a false choice. Test Ferney Bank first because it is cheapest, run the other low-cost steps alongside, and go back to the farmer with a request for a smaller lot or an option agreement. If the survey rules Ferney Bank out, the field or a joint solution becomes the main option, and you will have the evidence to support it.
