Both committee proposals are weaker than they look, but you have more than two options, and some of them are cheap. Your reserves are also thinner against this problem than they seem.

**Why the two proposals struggle**

- **Helicopter.** At 0.8 m³ per rotation, full occupancy (6 m³/day) needs about 7.5 flights, roughly €8,250 a day. At 55% occupancy (about 3.3 m³/day) it is about 4 flights, or €4,500 a day. Last August's 19 days would have cost about €85,000 at 55% occupancy, and more at higher occupancy. That is more than your whole reserve, and the pattern is getting worse. It works as a few-day emergency measure, not a plan.
- **Borehole.** €140,000 is almost double your reserves, and there is a 40% chance of paying and getting no usable water. A dry hole is very likely still billed.

**Other options**

| Option | What it does | Rough scale and caveats |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cut demand | Your 6 m³/day is about 125 litres per guest, which is high for a hut. Flush toilets and showers (added 2016) are probably the main cause. Coin or timed showers, low-flush or vacuum toilets, tap aerators and greywater reuse for flushing could plausibly halve it. | Cheapest by far. If you reach about 60 L per guest, a 55%-occupancy day uses about 1.6 m³, so a 19-day outage needs about 30 m³. I'm assuming a per-guest figure like this, so measure your real use first. |
| 2. Dry-spell mode | When the spring fails, switch off the showers and flush toilets and use composting or dry toilets in part of the hut. Keep a few of the old dry toilets, or add a small composting block. | Cuts use further for the weeks that matter. It fits a late-summer plan, since occupancy is already lower then. |
| 3. Store the June surplus | Your problem is timing. The spring floods the path in June and fails in August. A large insulated or buried cistern of 30 to 60 m³ would capture that overflow. | This is probably the best structural fix. It needs a quote and a freeze-protection design at 2,640 m. It also needs water-quality handling for long storage, such as chlorination or UV. It combines well with options 1 and 2. |
| 4. Pipe or pump from Grauberg | Their spring is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower, so water has to be lifted. The lift is about 1.5 kWh per 3 m³, which your spare solar can cover. | Needs the family's agreement, a flow study to confirm their spring can spare it, a seasonal pipe, and possibly a shared-cost or supply contract. Pipe and pump costs are unknown, so get a quote. I'd treat it as a strong backup. |
| 5. Improve the existing spring | Have the hydrologist look for a second capture point or a deeper intake, and check for leaks or losses in the pipe. | Low cost. A small survey could also sharpen the borehole's 60% odds before you spend €140,000. |
| 6. Roof rainwater and snowmelt | Collect rain and melt from the roof into storage. Use spare solar to melt snow in a heated tank early or late in the season. | A supplement only. Rain in the dry weeks is unreliable. |
| 7. Cheaper, staged hydrogeology | A geophysical survey first, then a cheaper shallow well or a smaller-diameter borehole if one makes sense. | Only worth doing if the survey result is strongly positive. |
| 8. Operating and financial cushions | Business-interruption insurance, a firm cancellation and refund policy, and a rule to shift or compress the season when the spring fails, for example by closing early on the glacier route when it is crevassed. | These protect your cash flow. They do not create water. |
| 9. Pre-agreed helicopter top-up | Keep helicopter flights as a bridge for a few days only, with a budget cap. | Fine as a last layer, not as the plan. |

**What I'd put in front of the AGM**

Present a layered plan instead of one big purchase:

1. Measure actual water use, then install demand-saving fixtures and a dry-spell mode (options 1 and 2).
2. Get a quote on a large storage cistern to catch the June overflow (option 3).
3. Open talks with the Grauberg family and commission a short flow check (option 4).
4. Ask the hydrologist about improving the spring intake (option 5). Defer the borehole unless a cheap survey improves the odds.
5. Keep a capped helicopter budget for short gaps only.

Together, options 1 to 3 could bridge a 19-day outage without drilling anything or flying in a single rotation. I'd ask for quotes on the cistern and the Grauberg line before November so members can compare real numbers.

**Assumptions to check**

- Per-guest water use of about 125 L is derived from your 6 m³ at 48 beds.
- Outages could get longer than 19 days, so size storage for 25 to 30 days if the budget allows.
- Costs for the cistern, pipeline and fixtures are not known yet.
