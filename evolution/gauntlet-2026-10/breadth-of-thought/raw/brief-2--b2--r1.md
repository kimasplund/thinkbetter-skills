There are a lot more than two options, and two of the most promising are already yours: the Ferney Bank site and the reserved graves in the east section. Buying the whole field looks like the most expensive way to solve a problem that needs roughly 2 to 3 hectares.

## What you actually need to solve

- At 95 burials a year, 25 years is about 2,400 graves. As a rule of thumb that is 2 to 3 hectares of usable ground once you allow for paths, parking and water-protection buffers. The field is 14 ha, so it is about five or six times what the 25-year question needs. It would last well over a century.
- £1.15m for 14 ha is about £82,000 a ha. Ask the District Valuer or an independent land agent how that compares with farmland values. The price may include hope value for development that a cemetery can never realise.
- Your reserve covers £280k, so the gap is £870k. As an illustration, borrowing that over 40 years at about 5% costs roughly £51,000 a year. I'm assuming about 6,000 households, which makes that about £8 to £9 per household per year, before any income from reselling surplus land. Check the real rate and your tax base.
- Don't plan on cremation rising. If it moved from 70% to 75% of funerals, burials would fall to about 79 a year, and 360 spaces would last about 4.6 years instead of 3.8. That is under a year of extra runway.
- The "four years" may be too pessimistic. See options 1 and 2.

## The options

**A. Get more out of Sorrel Lane**

1. **Reclaim the reserved graves in the east section.** About 380 plots were sold in advance in the 1960s and 70s and are still empty. I assume the 360 count excludes them, because they are already sold. If so, they are a second stock of space as large as the first. Two things work in your favour.
   - Burials into reserved graves that the owners' families do use are burials that don't draw on the 360 new spaces.
   - A 75-year right sold in 1965 runs out in 2040, and one sold in 1975 in 2050. Nearly all of them expire inside your 25-year window.

   You can also write to the registered owners or their heirs now. Ask whether they still want the grave, and offer to buy it back or take a voluntary surrender. Many owners will be elderly or dead, and heirs are often unaware of the grave. The legal steps for expiry, notice and resale are set by the Local Authorities' Cemeteries Order 1977 and your own regulations, so have your solicitor confirm them. Even a partial recovery could add years.

2. **Find out how fast the new spaces are really being used.** Ask the bereavement officer how many of the 95 burials a year go into new graves, how many into reserved graves, and how many into existing family graves. If 15 a year are going into existing graves, the 360 last closer to 4.5 years. You can then do three things.
   - Dig new graves to double depth where the ground allows, so each space serves two or three burials.
   - Promote family and reopened graves.
   - Tighten the layout of what remains of the east section.

3. **Use the north section differently.** It is full of graves, but the long grass may still hold pockets of unused ground, margins or old paths. A ground survey would tell you cheaply. Cremated remains need very little space. A cremated-remains area or a memorial wall there would take ashes demand off the grave stock, and the Friends of Sorrel Lane would probably welcome it.

4. **Policy levers.** Check what share of burials are non-residents and what they pay. Compare £1,450 for 75 years with neighbouring councils. This will not solve the problem, but a non-resident premium cuts demand a little and raises money towards new land.

5. **Reuse of old graves.** Outside London, reusing graves whose rights have lapsed needs a clear legal route, and the answer differs for consecrated and unconsecrated ground. It will also meet heritage and public resistance. Keep it on the list as a long shot, not a plan.

**B. Use land the council already owns**

6. **Ferney Bank (3 ha, closed pitch-and-putt).** At about 2,000 to 3,000 graves after buffers, this could cover most or all of the 25 years by itself, and combined with options 1 to 3 it is likely enough. It costs nothing to acquire. The checks decide whether it works.
   - Title, covenants, and any grant conditions on the old leisure use.
   - Whether it counts as public open space, which brings advertising and consultation duties if its use changes.
   - Planning permission.
   - Environment Agency groundwater and drainage constraints. Start with the free groundwater maps, then commission a hydrogeological survey.
   - Access, parking, and how far it is from Sorrel Lane. A site across town is a very different proposition from one nearby.

   Laying out access, drainage, paths and landscaping is a real cost, but probably far below buying the field. Get a surveyor's estimate before quoting any figure to council. Allow two to three years for planning, surveys and groundworks, so work has to start well before the 360 spaces run out. If the council had other plans for Ferney Bank, such as sale for capital, note that burial use gives up that value.

**C. Use someone else's capacity**

7. **A capacity agreement with Ashby Combe.** Their extension, three miles away, has barely been used since 2017. Options range from simply directing overflow there, to paying them to reserve space for Wickham Lees residents, to a joint burial committee. Ask their clerk why use has been so low. It could be a small village with few deaths, or something less welcome like poor ground or an unpopular site. Ask about non-resident fees too, because those are usually higher. Your residents would travel three miles and you'd depend on another council's decisions. It works best as a stopgap or backstop.

8. **Other burial grounds nearby.** Ask at the county association about parish churchyards with room, private or charity natural burial grounds, and neighbouring councils. A natural or woodland burial operator might also lease land from you at Ferney Bank and run the service.

**D. Acquire land**

9. **Buy the whole field outright.** Its advantages are real: it is next door, so there is one site, one set of staff and no change for mourners, and it settles the question for a century. Against that, you'd borrow heavily to buy about six times the land you need, and you'd still have to lay it out.

10. **Buy the field but not keep all of it.** The farmer insists on one lot, but you could still limit what the council holds.
    - Keep 3 to 4 ha and let or sell the rest, perhaps leasing it back to the farmer for grazing in the meantime.
    - Buy with a partner such as the district council, a wildlife trust or a neighbouring parish that takes the surplus.
    - Take a time-limited option or right of first refusal on the field for a small fee, so you aren't forced to decide in January and can't be outbid in the meantime.

    Ask the farmer why it must be one lot. If it is about leaving the farm viable or about tax, one of these may meet his need. Resale value of the surplus is uncertain, so treat it as an offset, not a given.

11. **Look for a smaller parcel elsewhere.** Candidates include glebe or church land, district council land, an unused corner of a school or recreation ground, or a different farmer's 3 ha. A modest, focused search takes little effort and would give you a price to compare with the field.

12. **Compulsory purchase of part of the field.** Tight rules apply, ministerial consent and a long lead time among them, and it is slow, adversarial and costly. Its main value is as a lever in negotiation, and your solicitor should say whether it is open to a town council.

**E. Stop providing new burials**

13. **Managed wind-down.** As I understand it, running a burial ground is a power for a town council, not a duty, so you could plan to close Sorrel Lane to new graves once it is full and let people use other providers. That would save the capital and avoid the risk, but residents would pay more, travel further, and lose the only local cemetery. Confirm the legal position, and present it to council as the baseline everything else is measured against.

## What I'd take to council in January

- **Lead with a staged plan** rather than a single decision.
  - First, firm up the real capacity: audit the 95 burials, trace the reserved graves, and survey the north section.
  - Second, commission a feasibility study on Ferney Bank.
  - Third, open talks with Ashby Combe as a backstop.
- **Keep the field as insurance, not the lead.** Ask the farmer for a time-limited option, test whether he'd accept a split, and get a valuation. If Ferney Bank fails its checks, the field becomes the main route, and by then you'll know its true price.
- **Show the cost per grave.** Buying 14 ha to bury 2,400 people is about £480 per grave in land cost alone, set against a £1,450 fee. Burial fees bring in roughly £138,000 a year gross. That makes borrowing affordable in principle, but I don't know your running costs, so check it.

## What to check before January

1. Bereavement officer: the split of the 95 between new graves, reserved graves and reopened graves, plus the space per grave in the east layout.
2. Registers: the reserved-grave owners, their purchase and expiry dates, and your solicitor's view on how to reclaim them.
3. Ferney Bank: title, covenants, grant conditions, distance, and the groundwater screening.
4. Ashby Combe: the clerk on capacity, fees, and why use is low.
5. Farmer and valuer: why one lot, and what the land is worth.
6. Finance: your borrowing rate, the tax base, and your actual burial running costs.

I've assumed England and a town council with the usual burial powers, so confirm the legal points above with your solicitor.
