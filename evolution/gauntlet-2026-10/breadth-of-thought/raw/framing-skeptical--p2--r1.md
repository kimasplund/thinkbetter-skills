Yes, there are other options, and I think the committee is framing this wrongly. Both proposals try to increase supply. The numbers suggest the cheaper levers are on the demand side and in storage, with supply from the Grauberg alp as a serious third route.

## What your numbers say

- Full occupancy uses 6 m³/day, which is about 125 litres per bed per night. That is high for a hut at this altitude. Flush toilets and showers (added in 2016) are very likely the bulk of it.
- From mid-August you run at about 55%, so about 3.3 m³/day at today's habits. The failures hit exactly the weeks when you are already half empty.
- Helicopter water costs about €1,375 per m³ (€1,100 for 0.8 m³). Covering a 19-day failure at 55% occupancy would take about 63 m³, or roughly €86,000. That is more than your reserves, in one bad year, and the spring is expected to fail more often. As a standing plan the helicopter option does not work at current consumption.
- Your 12 m³ tank holds two days at full use. The spring floods the path in June, so you have a large surplus at the wrong time of year.

So the problem is not that no answer exists. It is that the hut is sized for a water supply it no longer has, in the weeks when it needs it least.

## The realistic options

**1. Cut demand in the dry weeks (fastest, cheapest, within your control)**
- Measure first: fit sub-meters on the showers, toilet block and kitchen for a few weeks this summer. I'm assuming flushing and showers are well over half the total, but you should confirm it.
- Water-saving mode from a fixed date, for example 10 August: showers off or limited to a few paid minutes, urinals waterless or on the lowest flush, drinking and cooking water only from the kitchen.
- If a hut at this altitude can run at 30 to 40 litres per guest, 55% occupancy (about 26 guests) needs roughly 1 m³/day. A 19-day outage then needs about 19 m³ in total, and your existing tank covers 12 of it.
- Cost: low, mostly plumbing fittings and guest communication. Downside: guests who expect showers will grumble, and you reverse part of the 2016 upgrade for part of the season.
- Going further, composting or dry toilets for the late season only would cut flush demand to near zero. This is a bigger step and a harder sell.

**2. Store the June surplus**
- Add tank capacity filled from the June overflow and from July flow, to carry you through a late-summer outage.
- Sizing depends on option 1. With conservation you need roughly 15 to 20 m³ extra. Without it you would need 50 m³ or more, which gets expensive and hard to site.
- Cost: I can't price this from here. Get quotes for buried or insulated tanks, including the helicopter lifts to place them. Check also that the spring's July flow actually fills them while the hut is operating.
- It works with every other option and turns the June flood from a nuisance into an asset.

**3. Take water from the Grauberg alp**
- The alp is 190 m lower and 1.1 km away, with a spring that has never failed. Pumping up that height to deliver about 1 to 3 m³/day takes only a few kWh a day, which your spare solar capacity can easily supply.
- The unknowns are real. You need the farm family's agreement, a legal right to the water (check their water-rights status and whether the spring can spare the volume in a dry year), a route for a buried pipeline across 1.1 km of terrain, and a price. I would guess tens of thousands of euros, but that is a guess, so get a quote.
- A cheaper version is a seasonal arrangement for emergency supply only, with a small pump and a temporary line, rather than a permanent main.
- Why it is worth asking: it is the only option that secures supply rather than coping with a shortfall, and it may cost well under the borehole. A friendly neighbour is an asset, so speak to them before the AGM.

**4. Catch more water on site**
- Roof rainwater collection into the new tanks, mainly for flushing and washing, which keeps drinking water for the spring or the main tank.
- Check other small springs, seeps or meltwater streams near the hut. A second small source may be cheaper than a drilling contract. Your hydrologist can say which are reliable late in the season.
- Yield is uncertain, so treat it as a top-up, not a main supply.

**5. Change the late-season offer instead of closing**
- Rather than refunding everything, publish a "reduced water" status for late August and September: a lower bed cap, no showers, and guests carry some drinking water. Guided groups can be told this at booking.
- Set a rule in advance, such as spring flow below a stated threshold, so closures and refunds are planned, not a scramble.
- Revenue is lower than in a normal week but far better than 11 closed days and two cancelled groups.

**6. The helicopter, but with a budget and a trigger**
- Keep it as a backstop, not the plan. If demand is cut to about 1 m³/day and the tank is full, a 19-day outage needs perhaps 7 m³ from outside, about nine rotations or roughly €10,000. Affordable, and a one-off insurance cost.
- Combine water flights with your regular supply rotations where you can.

**7. The borehole**
- At €140,000 with about a 60% chance of usable water, the expected cost of getting a working well is roughly €230,000. It also exceeds your €75,000 reserves, and groundwater near a shrinking snowfield may decline for the same reason the spring does.
- It is not impossible, but it should be the last resort. If members want it kept alive, ask the geologist whether a cheaper survey would improve the 60% before anyone commits money. Club-level or regional funding for hut infrastructure is worth asking about too.

## Which to choose

| If your priority is | Lean towards |
|---|---|
| Cheapest and quickest, ready for next July | 1 + 5 + a small 6 backstop |
| Resilience for the next 20 years | 3 (Grauberg) with 2 and 1 |
| Keeping 2016 comforts all season | 3, or 2 sized large |
| Not spending reserves on a gamble | Anything except 7 |

## What I would put to the AGM

1. This summer: fit meters, adopt a water-saving mode from mid-August, and set a published trigger for reduced service. Total cost is small.
2. By spring: get written quotes for extra storage (option 2) and for a Grauberg supply line (option 3), and confirm water rights and volumes with the family.
3. Keep a helicopter budget of about €10,000 as insurance, not as the strategy.
4. Park the borehole unless the Grauberg and storage routes both turn out to be impractical.

## Assumptions to check

I assumed the 6 m³/day figure is mostly toilets and showers, that the spring flows well enough in July to fill extra storage, and that the Grauberg spring has spare volume. If your sub-metering shows guests really use most of the water for cooking and drinking, the case for demand cuts weakens and the Grauberg route becomes more important. The first meter readings will settle that, so they are the one thing I would do before November if you can.
