**Short answer:** the sick club is a good starting analogy but the wrong model to copy as it stands. Your draft would work for one of the three risks you have, partly work for a second, and can't work for the third. I'd keep the pooling idea, change the trigger and the eligibility rules, and split the fund by what kind of risk it covers.

(I don't know your jurisdiction or what a "bad month" currently looks like in euros, so I've made a few assumptions below. I can't check whether anyone in subtitling has done this. Other freelance groups have, and I'd look at them.)

## Why the sick club doesn't transfer cleanly

A dockers' sick club works because of four things:

1. **Who gets sick is random.** At any moment, a small and fairly steady fraction of members is off.
2. **Illness is independent across members.** Your neighbour's flu doesn't give you one.
3. **A doctor's note is outside the member's control.** The member can't dial it up or down.
4. **Membership was effectively compulsory.** Everyone on the dock was in, so the healthy couldn't opt out.

Your draft breaks all four:

- **Your "bad month" is partly predictable.** 31 of 46 members have their two lowest months in January and August. That isn't insurable risk, it's a known cash-flow pattern. It needs savings or smoothing, not pooling. On top of that, your trigger compares each month with a trailing 12-month average that includes the seasonal dips, so it is only mildly distorted. The real problem is that the same people will claim in the same two months, which leads to the next point.
- **Claims are correlated.** In January and August most members are low at once, so the pool is paying out when most members are drawing on it. Insurers call this correlated risk, and pooling handles it badly.
- **The trigger is self-reported and controllable.** Members invoice their clients themselves, and can decline desk offers without penalty. A member can defer an invoice to next month, or turn down a job in a thin month, and the fund tops them up. I'm not suggesting anyone would do this deliberately. But a rule that rewards it will, over time, produce it. This is moral hazard, and the dockers' doctor's note didn't have it.
- **Joining is voluntary and open at any time.** People who expect a thin patch join, and people who expect a good year don't. That is adverse selection, and it will push the pool toward claimants.
- **A 4% levy isn't an annual-loss budget.** If all 46 join, 4% of €1.9M is about €76,000 a year. The average member invoices about €41,000 a year, so about €3,400 a month, and 60% of that is about €2,070. The most the fund can top up one member in one month is about €1,380. Seven or eight members at that maximum in one month would take about €10,000, around 13% of a full year's contributions.

## The Halcyra problem is a different kind of risk

Halcyra sends about half your work by value, and from April it will trial machine-made first drafts with post-editing at a lower per-minute rate. That is not a bad month, it is a possible change in the price of the work. Rough scale: the desk handles about 70% of jobs, so if jobs and value roughly line up, desk work is on the order of €1.3M a year and Halcyra's share is something like €650,000. If that falls by even a fifth through volume or rate, the loss is about €130,000 a year. That is well above the entire annual pool of €76,000, and it lands on the same people.

No hardship fund of this size can insure that, and a rule that tries will drain the pool in a few months. Worse, your trailing-average trigger would reset downward, so after a year the "60% of average" floor would quietly follow the decline down. A structural shift is not insurable. It needs a different response: renegotiating, diversifying clients, training in post-editing, and pricing the new work well.

## What else this resembles, and what other fields do

- **Unemployment and income insurance run by unions.** Ghent-style systems, where unions administer the fund, are the closest thing. They work because they pay on something verifiable (loss of a job, hours), have waiting periods and caps, and are large enough to pool.
- **Schemes for performers and other intermittent workers.** Entertainment funds and similar schemes tend to test eligibility on verifiable work history (hours or contracts in a period), not on a drop in self-reported income. This is the nearest field to yours, and worth a read.
- **Crop and farm insurance.** Farmers face the same mix: predictable seasons, weather shocks that hit everyone at once, and moral hazard. The usual answers are individual savings or deposit accounts for the predictable swings, insurance for the shocks, and triggers based on an index (such as total rainfall) that no single farmer controls.
- **Friendly societies and mutual aid.** They pay modest, capped, discretionary benefits and rely on a waiting period before first claim.
- **Reserve funds.** Many co-ops simply build a reserve first and pay out later, rather than promising a formula from day one.

The common lesson is to match each tool to its risk: savings for what's predictable, pooling for what's random and independent, and something else entirely for structural change.

## How I'd set it up

**1. Split it into three layers.**

- **Seasonal smoothing (January and August).** Don't pool it. Offer an optional individual smoothing account: members put aside a set share in good months and draw it in the two weak ones. The co-op can administer it. It costs the pool nothing and solves the predictable part.
- **Hardship pool (the thing you actually asked for).** For genuine unexpected shortfalls: illness, losing a client, a family emergency.
- **Structural transition (Halcyra).** A separate budget line, not paid from the hardship pool. Use it for training in post-editing, rate negotiation support, and finding new clients. Decide now, before April, what it's for.

**2. Change the trigger.**

- Compare against the **same period last year or a seasonally adjusted baseline**, not a trailing average, so predictable dips don't count as hardship.
- Measure over a **rolling quarter** instead of one month, so deferring an invoice doesn't move the result.
- Require **evidence**: client names and invoice dates, checked against the desk's records where the work came through the desk.
- Add an **availability test**. A member who declined desk offers worth X in the period has that amount counted as income. Declining is still allowed, but it doesn't increase the payout.
- Exclude **Halcyra-driven declines** from the hardship formula, or handle them through the transition budget.

**3. Make the payout a share, not a full top-up.**

- Pay, for example, 50 to 70% of the gap below the threshold, so members keep a reason to chase work.
- Cap it: no more than three months in any 12, and a ceiling per month.
- If claims in a month exceed what the pool can pay, **pay pro rata**. The rule must say so up front.

**4. Fix joining.**

- A **waiting period** (say 6 months from joining, 3 months of contributions paid) before first claim.
- Open joining in **one or two windows a year**, not at any time, or make it compulsory for desk members. A compulsory scheme is the dockers' model, and it is the best protection against adverse selection. Whether 46 freelancers will vote for it is a political question.
- No joining after you know your own bad quarter is coming.

**5. Build the reserve before you promise anything.**

- Set a **target reserve** (for example a number equal to three months of expected claims, which you can estimate from last year's monthly figures). Don't pay any hardship claims until it is reached, or pay at a reduced rate until then.
- Run the numbers on last year's monthly data first, **as if** the fund had existed, and see how many months it would have paid out and how big the draws would have been. Do this separately for seasonal and non-seasonal shortfalls.
- Review after 12 months and change the rules then, in public.

**6. Governance.**

- The two desk members allocate work, which affects members' incomes, so they shouldn't also decide claims. Use a small committee of three members who aren't on the desk, plus the treasurer for the money.
- Publish the rules, the claims rate and the reserve each quarter, without names.

**7. Legal and tax.** A fund that promises a payout to members under a formula can be treated as insurance in some countries, with regulation to match. A discretionary fund that the committee may pay from can fall outside that. I'd take advice before launch. Also check how payouts are taxed, and how they interact with state benefits that members may be claiming.

## What I would do first

1. Run last year's actual monthly figures through the draft and through the revised version. That tells you whether the pool would have lasted, and what the January and August draws would look like.
2. Decide whether it is compulsory for desk members.
3. Before April, put a separate plan and budget in place for Halcyra. If you can only do one thing, do that, because it matters more to your members' incomes than anything the hardship fund can do.
