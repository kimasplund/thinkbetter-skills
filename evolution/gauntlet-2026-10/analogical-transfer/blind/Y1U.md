**Short answer:** No. A fire-style firebreak is the wrong model here, and as designed it could make things worse. I'd recommend a different plan, and I'd still use the 1 December decision date.

**Why the firebreak model fails**

A firebreak works because fire spreads by contiguous adjacency, so a gap of bare ground stops it. Ochre wilt doesn't spread that way. Three facts from the lab break the analogy.

1. **The vector flies.** The beetle travels up to 3 km. A 400 m ring is well inside its range, so the beetle crosses it easily. A barrier only works against something that has to cross it on foot.
2. **The infection is already outside the ring.** Of 20 symptom-free beech just outside the visible patch, 6 (30%) were infected. Symptoms take 18 to 24 months to appear, so the 140 symptomatic trees show where the disease was about two years ago, not where it is now. Infection is probably well beyond the visible stands, and likely beyond 400 m. The firebreak would be drawn around the wrong boundary. (The sample is small, with an uncertainty of roughly 12% to 54%, but even the low end is a serious infection rate.)
3. **Felling feeds the vector.** The beetle breeds in freshly cut or dying beech. Clear-felling a ring this winter produces a large amount of ideal breeding material. Stacked timber stays at the roadside until late summer, so it will be there through the whole May to July flight season. You would be raising the beetle population right at the front you are trying to defend.

There is a second problem. Tallis Ford firewood is cut from the surrounding woods and about 9,000 campers take leftover logs home. Infested logs could carry the beetle and fungus to other regions. This is a long-distance pathway that no ring can address, and it is a bigger risk than the local spread.

**What this is really like**

It is closer to a **vector-borne epidemic with a long latent period and a human-assisted transport route** than to a fire. The best analogies:

- **Dutch elm disease and Sudden Oak Death/ash dieback management.** Elm bark beetles carry the fungus. The programmes that worked, for example in some UK and North American cities, relied on prompt **sanitation**: finding and removing infected trees quickly and disposing of the wood so beetles couldn't breed in it. Blanket felling of healthy trees did not work.
- **Human disease outbreaks (e.g. foot-and-mouth, cholera).** Success came from **tracing, targeted removal of sources, and controlling movement**, not from a physical cordon alone. Latent periods mean you have to treat the apparently healthy near contacts as potentially infected.
- **Emerald ash borer and Asian longhorn beetle.** Quarantines on **firewood and timber movement** were central, because people spread the pest further than the insect does. Eradication succeeded mainly where infestations were small and found early, using intensive survey and removal.

The common lessons are: control the vector's breeding sites, control human movement of wood, survey intensively to find the true extent, and act on the biological timeline (flight season) rather than the administrative one.

**What I would do**

1. **Don't approve the ring felling.** Tell Ruairi this is not because his instinct for decisive action is wrong, but because the mechanism differs. Fire needs fuel continuity. Here the dispersal is by air and by vehicle.
2. **Delimit the outbreak first (now to February).** Survey and sample well beyond the visible patch, in concentric bands out to at least 3 km, prioritising beech near Tallis Ford and along roads. Use the lab to test symptom-free trees. Ask the lab how fast results can come back. You need the real extent before choosing a treatment boundary.
3. **Do sanitation felling, but time it and handle it carefully.** Remove symptomatic and confirmed-infected trees (the 140 plus those the sampling confirms). Fell in winter, which is the right season because beetles are inactive. Then deal with the wood **before May**: chip, burn on site, debark, or move it to a processing site under licence. Do not leave it stacked at the roadside until late summer. If the haulage contract can't be changed, that is the thing to renegotiate or work around, and it matters more than the felling itself.
4. **Stop the firewood route immediately.** Suspend firewood sales cut from the surrounding woods this summer, or source certified, treated, or non-beech wood from outside the area. Put up signs and messaging asking campers not to move wood. Treat this as the highest-priority pathway.
5. **Consider monitoring and trapping.** Ask the lab whether pheromone or trap-tree monitoring exists for this beetle, to see where it is flying in May to July.
6. **Notify and get expert help.** Report to the national plant-health authority if you haven't already (a notifiable disease may carry legal duties and possible funding), and get a plant-health or forestry pathologist involved. Nobody in your district has handled this before, so borrow experience.
7. **Plan for a longer game.** With 30% infection outside the patch, eradication may not be realistic. Be ready to switch to containment and slowing spread, protecting high-value stands and the campsite's safety (dying beech drop limbs), and replanting with other species.

**Decision for 1 December**

Rather than "ring fell or not", decide on: (a) funding and commissioning the delimitation survey, (b) a revised timetable for sanitation felling with wood disposal before May, (c) the firewood restriction at Tallis Ford, and (d) notifying the authority. Those can all start in January, so the contractors' timetable still holds, but for different work.

**Assumptions:** I've assumed ochre wilt behaves like other beetle-vectored wilt diseases, that no statutory control measures have been issued yet, and that the lab's 6 of 20 figure is representative. Please check these with the plant-health lab, since they change the details but probably not the direction.
