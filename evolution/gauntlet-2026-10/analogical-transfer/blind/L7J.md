**Short answer:** No, the fire firebreak is the wrong model, and as specified it could make things worse. Ochre wilt is closer to a vector-borne epidemic in agriculture or public health than to a fire front. I'd replace the ring with a "find, remove, and sanitise" programme, and I wouldn't commit to the ring by 1 December.

**Why the firebreak fails here**

A fire spreads by contiguous fuel and radiant heat. A gap in the fuel stops it. This disease doesn't spread that way, for four reasons.

1. **The vector flies.** The beetle travels up to 3 km. A 400 m ring is about an eighth of that distance, so it is not a barrier to the beetle. Fires throw embers too, but fire crews plan for that. Here the flight range is the main way the disease moves.
2. **Infection is already outside the ring.** Of 20 symptom-free beech sampled just outside the visible patch, 6 were infected, which is about 30%. With a 95% interval of roughly 12% to 54%, the true rate is uncertain, but it is clearly not near zero. Symptoms take 18 to 24 months to appear, so the 140 symptomatic trees show where the disease was around two years ago. The real front is probably well beyond them, and the 400 m ring is an arbitrary line drawn around old information.
3. **Felling feeds the beetle.** The beetle breeds in freshly cut or dying beech. If you clear-fell a large ring in winter and leave the timber stacked at the roadside until late summer, you create a large amount of breeding material. It will be there for the whole May to July flight season. In effect the plan builds a beetle nursery next to the outbreak.
4. **People move the wood.** About 9,000 campers a year take leftover firewood home. Infested logs travelling that way can start new outbreaks far beyond any ring. Long-distance jumps like this are the classic way tree pests and diseases escape local containment.

**What it's really like**

It is like a slow-incubation, vector-borne epidemic with a hidden infected population. Three comparisons are useful.

- **Dutch elm disease and sudden oak death, and plant pathogens generally.** The programmes that worked, or slowed spread, combined early detection, prompt removal of infected trees, and sanitation of the breeding material. Removing symptomatic trees without dealing with the wood tends to fail. Wide cut ring-fences have a poor record against flying vectors.
- **Human disease with a long incubation period.** You can't contain it using visible cases only, because the visible cases are old. You need surveillance that finds infections before symptoms appear, plus contact tracing, which here means sampling and mapping trees around known cases.
- **Movement controls, as for livestock disease and plant quarantine.** Where people carry the agent, the intervention is control of movement. Firewood rules are the equivalent.

The wildfire experience is still useful. Incident command, mapping, contractor management and tight deadlines all transfer. What doesn't transfer is the mechanism of spread.

**What I'd do**

*Before 1 December (decision window)*
- Ask the lab to expand sampling around the three stands, in rings or transects out to about 1 to 3 km, with numbers sufficient to estimate infection by distance. Ask how fast results can come back, and whether symptom-free trees can be tested before January.
- Ask the lab and the national plant-health authority two questions. What is the legal notification and control framework for a finding like this? What do they recommend for beetle control? I'm assuming there is a statutory duty to report and possibly to act, but I don't know your jurisdiction.
- Map beech density, roads and timber stacks within 3 km, and the Tallis Ford firewood supply chain.
- Reframe the decision to Ruairi. The objective is not "make a gap". It is "remove infected and breeding material before the beetle flies in May, and don't create new breeding sites".

*Winter works (January to March)*
- Remove symptomatic trees and trees testing positive, and any trees the lab judges to be infected or at high risk. Target by evidence, not by a fixed radius. This is likely a much smaller number of trees than a 400 m clear-fell.
- Deal with the cut wood before the flight season (by end of April at the latest). Options are chipping, burning, debarking, or hauling to a processing site. Use whichever is permitted and practical. Don't leave beech stacked at the roadside through May to July. If the haulage contract runs only in late summer, renegotiate it or arrange an exception. Cost and timing here are the key operational issue.
- Stumps and fresh wounds are also breeding or entry points, so ask the lab how to treat them.

*Before and during summer*
- Stop Tallis Ford firewood being cut from beech in the affected area. Source firewood from elsewhere or from non-host species, and tell campers clearly not to take wood home. Signage, handout at check-in and a "burn it here" rule are cheap and are likely to matter a lot.
- Put up beetle traps if the lab advises, both to monitor and to see where the beetle is.
- Run aerial or ground surveys through the summer, so new symptoms are found quickly.

*Ongoing*
- Treat this as a multi-year programme. With 18 to 24 months of latency, you won't know whether the winter works worked until the second or third summer. Plan staffing and budget accordingly.
- Keep the fire-style discipline: clear command, a single map, a decision log and agreed trigger points for escalating.

**If you still need some area-based clearance**

If the extended sampling shows infection concentrated within a certain distance, a targeted removal zone based on that data could be justified. It should only go ahead if the felled wood is handled before May. Without that, I would rather cut fewer trees and deal with them properly than cut more and leave them as beetle habitat.

**What to say to Ruairi**

His instinct is right in one respect: act decisively and early. The error is the analogy. Fire needs fuel continuity and the disease needs a vector and a pathway. So the plan should cut the vector's breeding sites and the human pathway, and remove infected trees, rather than create a fuel gap.

**Assumptions**
- The 6 of 20 figure is representative of trees near the patch. If the sample was biased, the picture shifts, but the main conclusion doesn't change.
- Legal obligations, chemical and treatment options and beetle ecology details come from the lab and the authorities. I've not assumed them.
