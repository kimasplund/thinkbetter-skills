**Short answer:** the sick club is the right ancestor for the mutual-pool idea, but it is the wrong model to copy as it stands. It worked because of three conditions your draft doesn't have. Your two biggest facts, the January/August pattern and Halcyra, are not the kind of risk a pool like this can carry. I'd split the fund into layers and redesign the trigger.

## Why the sick club worked, and where you differ

A dockers' sick club worked because:

1. **The event was verifiable and not chosen.** A doctor certified the illness. Nobody could make themselves ill to claim. Your trigger is "my invoiced total was low", and the member controls that figure. They control when they invoice, and they can decline desk offers at no cost.
2. **Nearly everyone was in it.** Dockers were in a closed shop, so healthy people paid too. Yours is voluntary and open at any time. Whoever expects a thin patch joins, and whoever expects a good year doesn't bother. The pool ends up with the worst risks.
3. **Illnesses were roughly independent.** Ten dockers off sick this week didn't mean ten more would be next week. Your members' bad months are strongly correlated: 31 of 46 have their lows in the same two months, and one client sits behind about half the work by value.

One part of the dockers' world is closer to you than the sick club: the **hiring hall**. Casual dock labour was allocated through a hall, much as your desk allocates work. The unions' out-of-work benefit came with rules tied to the hall's own records, such as whether you had presented yourself for work and refused none. Your desk holds the same kind of record, and the fund should use it.

## Other things this resembles

- **Unemployment insurance.** The relevant features are waiting periods before you can claim, caps on duration, and a test that you didn't refuse suitable work. Your "decline without penalty" rule would be a problem under any of these schemes.
- **Crop and index insurance for farmers.** This is the closest match to your January/August pattern. Insurers don't cover the predictable seasonal low, because it isn't a risk. It is a known cost, and farmers handle it with savings. They insure against the drought, which is the unpredictable, correlated shock. Where payouts depend on a measured index rather than the farmer's own reported loss, the moral-hazard problem largely disappears.
- **Intermittent-work schemes (for example France's scheme for performing-arts freelancers).** They qualify people on a minimum amount of work done over a longer window, not on a single month. That smooths out timing games.
- **Trade-displacement history (typesetters, mill towns).** Income-support pools did not save these trades when a technology cut the price of the work. The schemes that helped paid for retraining and moving into the new work. That is the Halcyra precedent, and it is a different instrument from a hardship fund.

## What your numbers say

I'm working from €1.9M across 46 members, which is about €41k a year or about €3,440 a month each. I'm also assuming the "half our work by value" from Halcyra refers to desk work, so roughly a third of total income. Correct me if not.

- **Income to the pool:** 4% of €1.9M is about €76,000 a year, or about €6,300 a month, if everyone joins. Voluntary uptake will be lower.
- **A top-up to 60% of an average member's income** is about €2,060. A single member with a month of zero invoicing costs the pool about as much as 15 members pay in that month. The year's whole inflow covers about 37 such zero-income member-months.
- **January and August:** if even a third of the 31 seasonal-low members dip under 60% in those months, the claims arrive together, in the months when contributions are smallest. The pool would be drained by the thing everyone already knows is coming.
- **Halcyra from April:** a rate cut doesn't trip your trigger unless it cuts a member's month below 60% of their average, which takes a drop of over 40%. So the fund would mostly not pay out on it. Where it does, it would pay many people at once. Over time the trailing 12-month average falls to the new level, so the trigger moves down with it and the fund quietly stops protecting those members. That is the right outcome for a structural change, but nobody will have decided it. Members who join expecting protection from "AI" will be wrong.

## Weaknesses in the draft rules, and the fix for each

| Problem in the draft | What happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One-month test on self-reported invoicing | Delaying an invoice into a bad month earns a top-up. A member at €0 collects about €2,060, then invoices double next month. | Test a rolling 3-month total against 60% of their trailing average. Date by invoice date. Cap top-ups at 3 months in any 12. |
| Declining desk offers is free | A member can refuse work and claim. | Eligibility requires that the member declined no more than a set share (say a third) of desk offers in the period. The desk already has these records. |
| Open any time, voluntary | Adverse selection. People join when work is thin. | Open enrolment twice a year. A 3-month wait before claims. A 12-month history before the trigger applies. Better still, make participation the default for new co-op members, with an opt-out. |
| Predictable lows in January and August | The fund pays out against a known pattern. | Handle seasonality outside the pool (see layer 1 below), and measure the trigger against a seasonally adjusted baseline. |
| No limit on total payouts | A bad quarter can exceed the pool, and then it is first come, first served. | Pool-level rules: pay pro rata if claims exceed, say, a quarter of the balance in a month. A reserve target of several months of expected claims before the fund pays more than small amounts. |
| The two desk members run it | They allocate the work and also judge who is in hardship. That is a conflict of interest. | A separate treasurer or trustee panel applies fixed rules with no discretion. The general meeting sets the rules. |
| Payouts counted as income | They would raise the trailing average and feed back into the trigger. | Exclude payouts from invoiced income when calculating the average. |

## How I'd set it up

**Layer 1: smoothing for the predictable (seasonal) dips.** A named personal reserve within the co-op. Part of each member's contribution (say 2 of the 4 points) is held in their own sub-account and paid out in January and August as an even top-up. It is their own money, so there is no pooling, no moral hazard, and no cross-subsidy. Since the lows are known, this is the correct tool.

**Layer 2: the mutual pool for unpredictable shortfalls.** The remaining contribution goes into a shared pool. Triggers and limits as in the table. Start modestly: a lower percentage than 60% at first, such as 50%, rising once the reserve has built up. Show members the arithmetic above so they understand that the pool is not a salary guarantee.

**Layer 3: a separate transition budget for Halcyra-type change.** Put a fixed share of contributions (or a one-off levy) into training and co-op-led work on post-editing: paid learning time, a style and quality guide, and a negotiated rate for post-editing work taken through the desk. Say plainly in the rules that a client-wide rate or method change is outside the hardship fund. Otherwise the fund will be asked to cover something it can't.

**Related concern: concentration.** One client at about half of desk work is a larger risk to the co-op than any one member's bad month. Part of the fund's value is giving you time to diversify the client base. Consider a second fund trigger for the co-op as a whole, reviewed by the general meeting, if one client's volume falls by a set amount.

## Before you launch

1. Test the rules against last year's monthly data member by member. Count how many top-ups the draft rules would have paid in each month, and compare the total with the pool. This takes an afternoon and will show whether January and August break it.
2. Check the legal position. Depending on your country, a pool that pays out on a defined event may count as insurance and need registration or an exemption. Co-ops of this kind usually can operate as mutual benefit funds, but you need to confirm it.
3. Start with a pilot year and a review at 12 months, and write down in advance what would make you change the rules.

## Where I'm unsure

The sick-club comparison is sound in spirit (mutual aid among people who share a trade), but it breaks on verification, participation and correlation, and I'd treat those three as the design problems. I'm fairly confident in the layered structure. I'm less sure of the exact percentages and caps, because I haven't seen your monthly figures. Run the back-test before fixing numbers. My confidence in the overall approach is about 75%.
