**Short answer:** The sick club is the right starting model for one of your three risks and the wrong model for the other two. Keep the pooling idea, but change the trigger, the entry rules and what the fund promises.

## Where the sick club fits, and where it doesn't

A dockers' sick club works because of four conditions:

1. Illness hits members at random and mostly independently of each other.
2. It is observable: a doctor signs a note.
3. Membership is effectively compulsory, because everyone on the dock is in.
4. Members can't easily choose to be ill.

Your fund meets none of these cleanly.

- **Your bad months are correlated.** 31 of 46 members have their two lowest months in January and August. In those months many members claim at once, so there is little pooling. A fund that pays out in the same two months for most of its members is a savings scheme with extra steps. It also starts each January with whatever it has built up, so the timing matters more than the annual total.
- **The big risk isn't a bad month.** Halcyra supplies about half your work by value, and from April it will trial machine drafts with post-editing at a lower rate. That is a structural, one-client shock that hits everyone together. A sick club can't cover it. With a rolling 12-month baseline, the fund would pay out while the baseline catches up, then stop, and it would have spent the reserve on a permanent shift. Say plainly in the rules that the fund covers temporary dips and does not cover a lasting change in the market.
- **Income isn't observable the way illness is.** Members invoice their own clients, can turn down desk offers freely, and can shift invoice dates. That creates moral hazard: someone can decline work or delay invoicing at month-end and still qualify.
- **Voluntary entry at any time invites adverse selection.** People join when they see a thin patch coming. The fund then gets the members most likely to claim. This is the same problem that has hurt voluntary flood and health cover.

## What else it resembles

- **Unemployment and income insurance for self-employed and seasonal workers.** Voluntary schemes tend to suffer adverse selection unless they have waiting periods or are compulsory. Schemes for performing-arts freelancers in some European countries use qualifying-period rules and look-back averages. I'm going from general knowledge here, so check the specifics before citing any of them.
- **Farm income stabilisation.** Farmers face lumpy income and often correlated shocks, such as a bad year for everyone. Schemes typically use a multi-year reference margin, matched contributions and a cap, and they treat catastrophic or correlated losses separately, often with an outside backer. The lesson is that a pool can't cover shocks that hit everyone at once without a reinsurer or a large reserve.
- **Mutual aid and friendly societies.** These work for small, observable, independent events and fail when claims cluster.
- **Individual buffer accounts**, similar to unemployment savings accounts. Each member funds their own account, with a pooled layer on top for deep shortfalls. This handles seasonality well, because everyone's January dip is just smoothing from their own August balance.

So your fund is really two things stuck together: a seasonal smoothing problem, which is best done with buffer accounts, and a rare-deep-shortfall insurance problem, which is what pooling is good at.

## Rough numbers

If all 46 join, 4% of €1.9M is about **€76k a year**, or about €6.3k a month. The average member invoices roughly €41k a year (about €3.4k a month), so the 60% floor is about €2.06k for the average member.

An illustration with assumed figures: if the 31 seasonal members dip to 50% of their average in January and August, each would be topped up by roughly €340 in each of those months, so about €21k across the two months for them alone. That is manageable once the fund has built up, but the fund starts empty, and the bigger risk is a single member falling to near zero for months. One member at zero for three months costs about €6k, so a handful of such cases would use most of a year's contributions. Run the real monthly data through this properly before you launch.

## How I'd set it up

1. **Define the baseline by calendar month.** Compare each month against the same month in previous years (or a seasonally adjusted average), so a normal January doesn't trigger a payout. Alternatively, use a rolling 3-month total against the trailing 12-month average to look for sustained dips rather than single months.
2. **Pay on shortfall below the line, with caps.** Cap the top-up per month (for example at 30% of the member's baseline), cap the months per year (for example 3), and cap total payouts per year as a share of the fund.
3. **Control entry.**
   - Open enrolment once or twice a year.
   - Require a waiting period (for example 6 months of contributions before the first claim).
   - Set a minimum contribution history.
   - Consider a default opt-in for new members, so that healthy members join too.
4. **Deal with moral hazard through the desk.** You already log offers. Require that a claimant accepted a reasonable share of suitable desk offers, or logged unavailability, in the claim period. Verify invoicing with bank statements or an accountant's check, and apply a rule on invoice dates so that postponing invoicing doesn't count.
5. **Run a reserve target.** Hold, for example, 6 to 9 months of expected claims before payouts reach the cap. Adjust contributions up or down within a band depending on the reserve. Say in the rules that payouts reduce pro rata if the fund runs short, so it can never owe more than it holds.
6. **Handle Halcyra separately.** Keep the hardship fund for temporary dips. Spend part of the early contributions, or a separate small pot, on post-editing training, rate negotiation and finding other clients. That is what actually protects members from Halcyra's change. Consider a rule that if the whole co-op's income falls past a threshold, the fund pauses top-ups and the membership meets to decide what to do.
7. **Check the legal side.** A scheme that pays members on defined events may count as insurance under your national rules, with tax and VAT consequences. Get an hour of advice from an accountant or lawyer before launch.
8. **Review after 12 months.** Compare actual claims, seasonal patterns and participation with your assumptions, and change the rules by a vote of the members.

## Assumptions

I assumed all figures are annual or monthly gross invoiced income in euros, that the co-op can legally hold and distribute a pool, and that "nobody in subtitling has done this" is true as far as you know. I haven't seen your monthly figures, so the illustration above is rough. The decisions to make first are the baseline definition (point 1) and the entry rules (point 3), since they drive most of the cost.
