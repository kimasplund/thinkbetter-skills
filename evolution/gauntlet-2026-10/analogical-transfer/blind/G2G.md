The sick club is a useful starting point, but it is only half the right model. It fits one of the three things going on in your numbers, and the draft rules as written would probably fail on the other two.

## Why the sick club worked, and where you differ

A dockers' sick club works because of four conditions:

- Illness is rare for any one person and mostly independent between people, so the pool is rarely hit by many claims at once.
- A doctor or workmates can verify it, so it is hard to fake.
- Members can't easily cause it or time it.
- Membership came with the job, so people couldn't wait until they were ill to join.

Your draft breaks each of these:

- **Claims arrive together.** 31 of 46 members have their lowest months in January and August. In those months two-thirds of the pool claims at once. Seasonal dips are also predictable, so they are a cash-flow timing problem and not an insurable risk. A pool that pays everyone for a dip everyone knows is coming mostly hands your own contributions back.
- **The trigger can be gamed.** Members invoice for themselves and may decline desk offers without penalty. A member can hold back an invoice until next month, or turn down an offer in a slow month, and the fund pays the difference. Nobody is cheating in the strict sense. The rules just reward it.
- **Joining is open at any time.** People will join after a bad run and may leave after drawing. Voluntary, open-door insurance attracts the people most likely to claim. With only 46 people, a few such members can sink it.
- **One client dominates.** Halcyra supplies about half your work by value. A sick club's risk is spread over many independent causes. Yours is concentrated in one client's decision.

## Three needs, and what each is like

You are really trying to cover three different things, and each has a different precedent.

**1. Predictable seasonal troughs (January, August).**
- This is like a farm's winter or a school-term worker's summer. The usual answer is smoothing: a reserve that members pay in during good months and draw on in the troughs.
- It can be a pooled advance that members repay as they earn, or a personal reserve that the co-op holds. It is a savings and lending scheme, not insurance, and it should cost the pool close to nothing.

**2. Unpredictable bad stretches (a lost client, illness, a dry quarter).**
- This is the insurance part. The closest precedents are farm income-stabilisation schemes and the French scheme for intermittent entertainment workers.
- Farm income-stabilisation schemes, such as Canada's AgriStability, pay when income falls below a set share of a multi-year reference level. Many use an average that drops the best and worst years, so a member can't move the baseline by timing one month.
- The French scheme shows that a freelance cultural workforce can be covered. It also shows that without eligibility rules and controls, costs run away. Check the details of both before you cite them to members.
- Union-run unemployment funds in Belgium and the Nordic countries (the "Ghent" systems) stay solvent while voluntary because they get outside support and have strict eligibility. Voluntary enrolment alone doesn't hold up.

**3. A structural change, which is what Halcyra's trial may turn out to be.**
- Machine-drafted work with post-editing at a lower rate is a permanent change in what the work pays. Insurance is the wrong tool for that.
- The nearest precedents are short-time work schemes. They bridge a temporary demand slump and are deliberately not used for lasting decline. Retraining and transition funds are the tools for lasting decline.
- A fund that props up income at the old level through a permanent shift will drain and then collapse. That is a worse outcome for members than having no fund.

## What the numbers say

- 4% of €1.9M is about €76,000 a year if all 46 join. Spread over 12 months, that is roughly €6,300 a month.
- Halcyra is about half of your work by value, or roughly €950,000. If the trial cut the rate on even a fifth of that, the income lost would be about €190,000 a year. That is more than twice what the fund collects, and it would recur every year.
- The 60%-of-trailing-average trigger does not cover that case. Someone whose income falls by a third and stays there never drops below 60%, so they are never paid. Their trailing average also slides down slowly, which means the fund pays out while the average catches up and then stops.
- The people you lost after two or three thin months probably had months at 65 to 80% of normal, not 40%. A 60% trigger may never have paid them. Test this against your own data.
- For the seasonal dips, take an illustrative case: January and August at about half the monthly average. A top-up to 60% would cost roughly 10% of one month's income per member in each of those months. That is manageable, but then 31 people draw twice a year, and the fund is mostly returning their own money.

## How I would set it up

**Separate the layers.**
- Run a seasonal smoothing facility (an advance or personal reserve, repaid within the year) apart from the hardship pool. The hardship pool should not pay for January and August.

**Measure shortfall against a protected baseline.**
- Compare the last 3 months with the same 3 months in each of the past two or three years, dropping the highest and lowest. That removes seasonality and makes one-month invoice timing pointless.
- Use a graduated payment, for example 50% of the gap between actual income and 75% of baseline. Avoid a hard cliff at 60%.
- Set a maximum payment per member and per year.

**Close the gaming routes.**
- Require invoices or accountant-signed income statements for the period.
- Make a condition of the fund (not a penalty for declining) that a claimant has accepted a reasonable share of desk offers that matched their profile in the claim period. Members stay free to say no, but then they don't claim.
- Because the desk sees 70% of the work, it can verify most of that. Two elected members will find this uncomfortable, so have the treasurer or an independent checker assess claims.

**Fix enrolment.**
- Hold enrolment windows once or twice a year, with a 6 to 12 month wait before first claim and a minimum commitment of a year or two.
- Alternatively, make participation the default, with an opt-out only at the window.
- Contributions should also be set by the pool's expected claims. Tune 4% after the back-test below.

**Protect the pool.**
- Hold a reserve target (at least one year of expected claims) before paying anything beyond a small cap.
- Pay pro rata if claims exceed the pool, and state that in the rules up front.
- Fixed rules are better than discretionary payments, which cause disputes in a small co-op.

**Handle Halcyra separately.**
- Set aside part of the contributions for a transition fund covering post-editing training and tooling, and for collective rate negotiation.
- Agree before April what the fund does if the trial goes badly. A fund is most likely to be misused in the first quarter after a shock.
- Ask Halcyra for volumes and a timeline for the back-catalogue trial, so you can size the exposure.

## Before you adopt anything

Run the draft and the revised rules over your last three years of monthly figures for all 46 members. Check three things:

1. How many payments each rule would have triggered, and in which months.
2. Whether the people who left would have been paid.
3. What happens to the pool if Halcyra's work falls by 20% from April.

If the pool is empty by autumn under the Halcyra case, that tells you the fund can only cover temporary dips, and you should say so to members before they sign up.

I'm assuming the Halcyra back catalogue is a meaningful share of their work and that individual monthly data is available to you as treasurer. If either is wrong, the sizing above changes, but the structural points stay the same.
