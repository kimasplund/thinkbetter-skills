I'm district manager for the Glasmoor Forest Service: 18,000 hectares of woodland, about a third of it beech. In August our survey crews found ochre wilt, a fungal disease that kills beech within a few years, in three stands on the eastern side near the Tallis Ford campsite. About 140 trees show symptoms.

Nobody here has dealt with a tree disease outbreak before. We do have decades of wildfire experience, and our fire officer, Ruairi Banfield, wants to handle it the way we'd handle a fire: this winter, clear-fell every beech in a 400 m ring around the three stands, a firebreak to starve it of fuel. He wants a decision by 1 December so the contractors can start in January.

What the plant-health lab has told us:

- The fungus is carried from tree to tree by a bark beetle that flies from May to July and can travel up to 3 km.
- The beetle breeds in freshly cut or dying beech wood.
- Trees show symptoms 18 to 24 months after infection. The lab sampled 20 symptom-free beech just outside the visible patch, and 6 were infected.

Other facts: felled timber normally stays stacked at the roadside until the haulage contract runs in late summer. Tallis Ford sells firewood cut from the surrounding woods to about 9,000 campers each summer, and many of them take their leftover logs home.

Is the firebreak the right model? What is this really like, how have other fields contained things that spread, and what would you do?
