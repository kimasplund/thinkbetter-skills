**Short answer:** the gym is the wrong model in its pure form, and your own numbers and licensing terms show why. Several closer analogues exist. I'd put a capped, off-peak version in front of the board, not an unlimited all-performance pass.

## Why the gym model doesn't fit

A gym makes money because most members rarely turn up, and an extra visit costs the gym almost nothing. You have the opposite on both counts.

1. **Your best customers would trade down.** About 1,800 people came to four or more productions and bought roughly a third of your single tickets. I'm assuming your seats sold are about 88,400 (520 seats x 250 performances x 68%). Less about 7,700 subscriber seats, that leaves roughly 80,700 single and other tickets, so a third is about 27,000 tickets. That is about 15 tickets per person, which presumably includes companions, or about €500 a year each at €34. At €180 a year (€15 x 12), if all of them joined, you would lose roughly €590,000 net. Even if only a third switched, it's about €195,000. Unlimited pricing gives the most to the people who already pay you the most.
2. **Subscribers get nothing new.** Season Seven households pay €182 for seven seats (€26 each). Unlimited at €180 is cheaper for the same money, so expect most of the 1,100 to move across and give up about the same revenue for more attendance.
3. **Peak nights are already full.** Friday and Saturday sold out on 80% of dates. Every member seat on those nights displaces a full-price sale. The spare capacity is Tuesday to Thursday, where occupancy is 49% (about 265 empty seats a night).
4. **Licensing makes unlimited close to impossible.** Any admission issued at under half face value counts as complimentary, and complimentary admissions are capped at 4% of the house. That is about 20 seats per performance, or about 5,000 a year.
   - With a €34 average ticket, half is €17. A member who sees a show four times a month is paying about €3.75 per visit, so every one of those admissions would count as complimentary.
   - The cap blocks the model at about 20 member seats a night, however many members join.
   - Complimentary tickets also no-show at 14%, against 3% for paid ones.
   - Rights holders get 10% of gross box office per performance. You need to know how membership income is allocated to each performance, because that decides what you owe. Get this answered in writing before anything else.

## What it actually resembles

- **Cinema subscriptions (MoviePass, AMC A-List, Cineworld Unlimited).** MoviePass collapsed because it paid full price per ticket while charging a flat fee. Cinema chains that survived own the venues, cap usage, and exclude premium shows. The lesson is to cap visits and never let the fee fall below your per-seat obligations. You have a per-performance royalty, which is the MoviePass problem in miniature.
- **Airline or hotel standby and off-peak pricing, and last-minute rush or day-seat schemes.** These sell only seats that would otherwise be empty, close to the date, at a lower price. That matches your Tuesday to Thursday gap.
- **Ski season passes (such as Epic or Ikon).** These are priced as a loyalty product. Regulars pay up front, blackout dates protect peak days, and passes sell early, which brings in cash before the season starts. Your equivalent is blackout dates on Friday and Saturday.
- **Museum and arts memberships.** Members pay for access, status and priority booking, not just a discount. Many give very little per-visit value on purpose.
- **Gyms and software subscriptions.** Reasonable for the cash-flow smoothing and cancel-any-time convenience, but they depend on low usage, which you shouldn't plan on.

## What the other fields teach

1. Cap or exclude the premium inventory, or it gets drained by your best customers.
2. The price must cover the per-use cost, which for you is royalties and the complimentary-ticket rule.
3. Protect your existing loyalty tiers. Redesign subscriptions and the membership together.
4. Treat the first year as a test. Expect heavy use from those who join early.
5. Measure whether members bring new people, not only whether they sign up.

## How I would set it up

1. **Off-peak membership, not unlimited.** Valid Tuesday to Thursday only, with Friday, Saturday and opening nights excluded. Something like €15 to €20 a month for a fixed number of admissions (for example two or three a month), with no unlimited use.
2. **Price every admission at or above half face value.** At €34 that means €17 or more per visit, so the pass stays inside the 4% complimentary cap and doesn't trigger the complimentary rules. For example, €15 a month for one visit is a €15 admission, which is under €17, so €20 a month for one visit or €36 for two would work. Check the arithmetic against your real average ticket price by production. Confirm with rights holders how membership income is allocated.
3. **Release seats late.** Members book from a few days ahead, or on the day, into unsold seats, not two weeks ahead. That keeps earlier booking for full-price buyers and subscribers.
4. **Add a deliberate reason to join that isn't volume.** For example, priority on late seats, a guest discount, and a members' event.
5. **Fold or tier the existing products.** Keep Season Seven, perhaps with a top-up option, and give the 1,800 frequent attenders a clear offer (a loyalty tier or credit book) so they don't simply drop to the cheapest product.
6. **Pilot, then decide.** Cap the first intake (say 300 to 500 members), run it for one season, and track four things: seats filled Tuesday to Thursday, the share of members who were previously buyers, revenue per seat, and attendance of no-shows.
7. **Board numbers to bring.** Cannibalisation scenarios (10%, 33% and 100% of the 1,800 switching), complimentary-cap usage per performance, and a royalty cost estimate.

## Assumptions

- I used your average ticket price (€34) across all performances, and assumed the 4% cap applies to the whole house per performance and is partly used already by press and other complimentary tickets. If it is, you have less than 20 seats a night to work with.
- I have not seen your contracts, so the half-price and cap reading comes from your summary. Confirm it with your licensing contact before the board meeting.
- The 15 tickets per person figure is an estimate and probably includes guests, so the real cannibalisation per person may be lower, but the direction is the same.

Overall, Maren's instinct to smooth income and reach people who don't pay €34 is sound. A capped Tuesday to Thursday membership delivers it without giving away your best customers or breaking the licensing rules.
