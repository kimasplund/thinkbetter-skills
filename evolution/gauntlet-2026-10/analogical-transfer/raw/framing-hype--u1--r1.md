Short answer: the gym model is the wrong match, and the figures you gave me show why. A membership can still work for you, but not as "unlimited for €15". Here is how I'd walk the board through it.

## Why the gym comparison fails

A gym is profitable because most members rarely turn up, and the building is never full at the moments that matter. Your situation is the opposite on both counts.

- **Your best customers are your heaviest users.** The 1,800 people who attended four or more productions bought about a third of your single tickets. Single tickets are roughly 80,000 a year (88,400 attendances at 68% of 130,000 seats, minus 7,700 subscriber seats). A third of that is about 27,000 tickets, around 15 each, or roughly €510 a head at €34. Unlimited access for €180 a year is the cheapest option for exactly the people who already pay you the most.
- **Your subscribers would also move.** Season Seven costs €182 for one seat at seven shows. Thrale Unlimited costs €180 for any number of shows. No sensible subscriber stays put.
- **Your scarce seats are the ones members would want.** Friday and Saturday sold out on 80% of dates. Every member in a peak seat is a €34 sale lost.

A rough worst case: if the 1,800 frequent attenders and the 1,100 subscribers all join, you collect about €522,000 where you now collect roughly €1.1 million from them (about €914,000 in single tickets plus €200,000 in subscriptions). That is a gap of about €590,000, or around 3,300 additional paying members before you break even, and each new member has to fit into seats without displacing anyone. Treat these as estimates, because I've assumed the frequent attenders buy for themselves and that the €34 average applies to them.

## The licensing terms are the real constraint

This is the part that makes "unlimited" unworkable as proposed.

- Any admission sold at under half its face value (under €17 at the average price) counts as complimentary.
- Complimentary admissions are capped at 4% of the house, so about 20 seats per performance, or about 5,000 a year across 250 performances.
- At €180 a year, a member who comes 11 or more times is paying under €17 a visit. Every frequent attender would fall below the line, and 2,900 converts attending 10 to 15 times each would generate 30,000 or more such admissions against a cap of about 5,000.

I'm assuming the test is applied per admission using the effective price. Please confirm that with your licensing contacts before the board meeting, because it determines the whole design. Also ask how membership income will be allocated to performances for the 10% payment. At full allocation that is about €18 per member per year.

There is a second cost. Complimentary admissions no-show at 14%, against 3% for paid tickets. If members behave like comp holders, you will have more empty seats that someone else could have filled.

## What other fields have learned

- **Unlimited cinema passes (MoviePass and AMC's A-List).** MoviePass paid theatres full price for every ticket on a flat fee and collapsed. AMC capped the number of visits, excluded some formats and survived for years. The lesson is to cap usage per member and never let one side of the deal be open-ended.
- **Airline unlimited passes (American Airlines' lifetime pass, JetBlue's all-you-can-fly pass).** The heaviest users cost far more than they paid, and the airlines ended the offers. Unlimited use by self-selected enthusiasts means the people who sign up are the ones who lose you money.
- **Ski season passes.** Resorts sell commitment early at a discount, use blackout dates on the busiest days, and fill empty midweek capacity. Their real revenue comes from buying earlier and visiting less than the price implies, which is also the logic your Tuesday to Thursday figures suggest.
- **Gyms.** Their income depends on non-attendance, and many members join in a burst and drop off. That is the one thing a theatre cannot count on. Your members will come, because the product is good.

## What I would put to the board

Replace "unlimited" with a pass built around your empty seats.

1. **Aim at Tuesday to Thursday.** At 49% occupancy you have around 265 empty seats on an average midweek night. That is where a member costs you almost nothing.
2. **Keep every admission at or above €17.** For example, €15 a month (€180) includes 10 off-peak admissions, which is €18 each, and further visits cost €17 or more. That is a discount club rather than "all you can see", but it stays inside the licence terms.
3. **Offer day-of standby for the feel of unlimited.** Release unsold midweek seats to members 24 hours ahead or on the day, within the 20-seat comp allowance. Booking two weeks ahead is the worst choice for no-shows, because people forget. Late booking also keeps the empty-seat risk small.
4. **Use the comp allowance sparingly.** It is the only legal room for sub-€17 admissions. Check how much your press, donor and guest lists already use before promising any of it to members.
5. **Keep Friday and Saturday at or near full price** for members, or exclude them.
6. **Protect the subscription.** Price the pass so it does not undercut Season Seven for a loyal household, or offer subscribers a one-way upgrade, so they don't leave for a cheaper product.
7. **Start small.** Cap the first year at about 500 founding members, measure who joins and how often they come, and widen only when you see real numbers.

The strongest case for the board is that a membership can help, with a design fitted to your seats and your licences, and that the version Maren has proposed would likely cost more than it brings in.

## What would change my view

- If the rights holders agree a different test for membership admissions.
- If most of your 1,800 frequent attenders are actually buying for several people, which would shrink the loss.
- If surveys show the pass genuinely brings in people who would never pay €34.

I'm fairly confident about the direction (about 80%) and much less about the exact euro figures, which depend on the assumptions above.
