**Short answer:** the gym is the wrong model, and as written Thrale Unlimited fails on your own numbers. Halvard is right about the plan and wrong about the category. A membership that fills Tuesday to Thursday could work. One that gives open access to every performance cannot.

## Why the gym fails here

A gym makes money from three things you don't have.

1. **Spare capacity every hour.** Your Friday and Saturday evenings sell out on 80% of dates. Members booking two weeks ahead would take seats that now sell at €34, and you'd be swapping €34 for about €15 a month on your best nights.
2. **Members who pay and don't come.** At a gym the unused membership is the profit. A theatre member sees the shows, so there is no breakage. And because they can cancel at any time, a member can see what they want and leave. "Steady monthly income" is weaker than what you have now, because your subscribers pay €182 up front.
3. **Near-zero cost per visit.** Yours isn't zero. This is where the plan breaks.

## The licensing rules sink the flat fee

Any admission issued at under half face value (below €17) counts as complimentary. A member paying €15 a month who comes once a month is already under €17 per visit, so effectively every member admission is complimentary.

Complimentary admissions are capped at 4% of the house, which is 20 seats a performance. Over 250 performances that is about 5,000 admissions a season, and your existing comps use part of it.

| | |
|---|---|
| Member admissions allowed per season (at most) | ~5,000 |
| If the average member comes 6 times | ~830 members |
| Revenue at €180 each | ~€150k, about 5% of box office (roughly 88,000 tickets × €34 ≈ €3.0m) |

Your 1,100 subscribers alone use 7,700 seats a year, so if they moved over, the cap would be broken by itself. They would move, because €15 a month (€180 a year) undercuts the €182 subscription for something strictly better.

Two more costs:
- You pay rights holders 10% per performance. Whether they take it on the face value of those seats or on an allocated share of the fee is unknown, and you need it in writing.
- Comps no-show at 14%, against 3% for paid tickets. Free cancellation and "book it, decide later" will push members towards the 14% end.

## The cannibalisation problem

The 1,800 people who came to four or more productions bought about a third of your single tickets, roughly 27,000 tickets. That is 15 tickets each, about €500 a year, which implies they often buy for companions. A member pays €180.

If only 10% of that group joined, you would lose roughly 180 × (€510 − €180) ≈ €59k. Discount clubs attract your heaviest users first. Whether they bring in people who would never pay €34 is the one thing nobody has measured. Maren's belief may be right, but it isn't evidence, and it is the thing the whole case rests on.

## What this actually resembles

- **Cinema unlimited cards (Cineworld, UGC) and MoviePass.** Cinema cards work because the card is priced above the typical visit and the studio is paid per admission by contract. MoviePass paid full price per ticket while charging a flat fee, and collapsed. Your comp rule and rights fee make you the MoviePass case unless the terms change.
- **Airline yield management and standby.** The cheap seat exists to fill the empty one. It is protected from the full-fare buyer by day, notice period and restrictions. That is the right tool for your 265 empty midweek seats.
- **Ski season passes (Epic-style).** Blackout dates on the peak days are what let the pass be cheap. Yours would have blackouts on Friday and Saturday.
- **Museum memberships.** They work because of donor motive and zero marginal cost. Neither transfers.
- **All-you-can-eat.** It works only when the average guest eats less than the price implies, and the heavy eaters self-select first. That is the pattern you would see here.

I can't confirm a theatre that has run an unlimited scheme successfully. Check that before the meeting. "Nobody has thought of it" is a weaker explanation than the one above: the comp rule and the rights terms make it hard.

## How I'd set it up

1. **Ask the licensors first.** Ask how membership admissions count for the rights fee and whether they are complimentary. This costs a few emails, and all the economics depend on the answer.
2. **Price each visit at €17.50 or more**, so every admission counts as paid and stays outside the 4% cap. Offer a monthly fee (say €9) plus member seats at €17.50, rather than free entry.
3. **Restrict to Tuesday to Thursday**, with Friday and Saturday seats released to members only as standby when unsold, a day or two out.
4. **Keep Season Seven distinct.** At €9 a month plus €17.50, a seven-show subscriber pays about €230, above €182, so they stay put. Heavy single-ticket buyers will still find it attractive.
5. **Limit booking and cancellation.** Allow free cancellation up to a cut-off, with a strike rule for no-shows.
6. **Pilot it.** Run 300 founding members for one production cycle and match them to your customer records. Define in advance what failure looks like, for example fewer than half of members being new to you, or midweek paid sales falling.

A cheaper test of Maren's claim: sell a €17.50 midweek seat to everyone, no membership, and see who arrives.

## For the board

- The opportunity is the ~265 empty midweek seats, worth more than a gym-style pitch.
