**Short answer:** No, not as proposed. A fuel break stops something that moves across contiguous fuel. Ochre wilt moves by a flying vector, it is already outside the visible patch, and the work itself would create beetle breeding material. Wildfire experience is useful here, but the right parts of it are different from the firebreak.

## Why the firebreak fails on your own numbers

1. **The ring is too small.** The beetle flies up to 3 km. A 400 m ring covers a fraction of one flight. It would be like cutting a 400 m break against a fire that throws embers 3 km.
2. **The fire has already jumped.** 6 of 20 symptom-free beech just outside the patch were infected. With a sample of 20 that means anywhere from roughly one in eight to over half, but it is clearly not "a few stragglers". Symptoms take 18 to 24 months to show, so the 140 symptomatic trees show where the disease was about two years ago, not where it is now. Several beetle seasons have probably passed since then. A line drawn around the visible patch is likely to have infected trees on both sides of it.
3. **Felling feeds the vector.** The beetle breeds in freshly cut or dying beech. A clear-fell of everything within 400 m, with the timber stacked at the roadside until late summer, puts a very large amount of ideal breeding wood in place before the May to July flight. A firebreak removes fuel. This operation adds it.
4. **There is a human-carried route.** About 9,000 campers a year buy firewood cut locally, and many take leftover logs home. If any of that wood is beech, or comes from infested stands, it carries beetles or fungus to other regions. This matters most for a first outbreak, because it is how local outbreaks become distant ones.

## What this is really like

It is a **disease with a long hidden incubation period and a flying carrier, plus a risk of long-distance spread by people moving wood**. Three other fields have handled that.

| Source | What worked there | What carries over |
|---|---|---|
| **Dutch elm disease control** (elm bark beetle, spread by breeding in dying or cut elm) | Prompt sanitation: find infected trees, remove them, and debark, chip or burn the wood before the beetles fly. Cities that left cut elm lying around saw outbreaks continue. | Remove the breeding material or treat it before the flight season. Timing and wood handling matter more than the width of any cleared zone. |
| **Emerald ash borer and other invasive-pest programs** | Mapping the infested area with delimitation surveys, and quarantine or restrictions on moving firewood and logs. Long-distance jumps by human transport were what broke containment. | Treat wood movement as the main route past any perimeter. Control the firewood. |
| **Animal and human outbreaks with latent cases** (foot-and-mouth, contact tracing) | Culling or isolating ring zones works only after you know who is already infected. Where there is a long silent incubation, tracing and testing come before large-scale removal, along with movement controls. | Find out where infection really is before deciding where to cut. Your lab result is the first step in this. |

The fire analogy still has a good part. Firefighters deal with spotting: embers landing ahead of the front. You do not rely on a fuel break against spotting. You scout ahead of the front, find the spot fires early and put them out before they join up. That is also the right approach to ochre wilt. The edge of the disease is not the visible patch. It is wherever beetles have already landed.

## What I would do

**Before 1 December**
- Commission a delimiting survey now, if the lab can process samples. Prioritise beech between the infected stands and about 3 km out, especially downwind or along the valley in the direction the beetles will most likely move. Aim for a map of where infection has been found, not a fixed radius. If winter sampling is difficult, ask the lab what is feasible. Do not assume the answer.
- Ask the lab and the national plant-health authority what the legal duties are (notification, statutory control zones) and whether they will help fund or direct a response. A first outbreak almost certainly triggers reporting.
- Stop cutting and selling beech firewood from the Tallis Ford area. Tell campers not to take logs home. Supply firewood from elsewhere, or non-beech, and keep leftover wood on site. This is cheap and can be done now. It also needs no decision from Ruairi's timetable.

**What the 1 December decision should be**
Approve a **sanitation felling** instead of a ring clear-fell: symptomatic trees plus any sampled-positive beech nearby, and nothing wider unless the survey supports it. Make the decision conditional on a wood-handling plan.
- Fell in winter, when the beetles are not flying.
- Remove, debark, chip or burn the wood **before 1 May**. No stacks of beech at the roadside over the summer. Ask your contractors whether the haulage contract can be renegotiated or replaced with on-site chipping. If it cannot, the plan is not ready, and that is a reason to wait or to fell less.
- Leave healthy trees alone unless the survey shows infection. Killing healthy beech beyond the infection edge is not needed and creates more breeding material.

**After that**
- Treat summer 2026-27 as a surveillance season: patrol, flag new symptoms, and run the same cycle of detect, fell and clear each winter.
- Plan for a longer campaign. With a two-year incubation, you will not know whether it worked for at least two years.

## Where I am unsure

- I do not know the beech density near Tallis Ford, the terrain, or whether the beetle's true spread rate matches the 3 km maximum. The delimiting survey will answer these better than I can.
- 20 samples is small. The 30% figure is a warning, not an estimate to build a budget on.
- Some clearing near the stands may turn out to be right. But it should be sized by the survey and done with the wood removed before the flight, not drawn as a ring on a map.

Ruairi's instinct is correct that the first response should be fast and organised. The change I would make is the target: not a barrier around the disease, but finding it, removing infected wood before the beetles fly, and closing off the firewood route.
