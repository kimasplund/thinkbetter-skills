**Short answer:** the gym is the wrong model as proposed, mainly because of your own licensing terms. A cheaper-to-run cousin of it, an off-peak membership, looks sound. Here is the reasoning, the analogies, and a setup.

## 1. Why "unlimited for €15" breaks on your numbers

**The complimentary cap is the binding constraint.** An admission under half its face value counts as complimentary. At an average face value of €34, half is €17. A member who pays €15 a month and sees even two shows is paying about €7.50 an admission, and that is complimentary. Complimentary admissions are capped at 4% of the house, which is about 20 seats of 520 per performance. Your existing comps use part of that allowance.

- The most the scheme can ever carry is about 20 × 250 = 5,000 member admissions a season, minus current comps.
- Divide that across members. With 2,000 members, each gets 2.5 visits a year. With 400 members, each gets about 12.
- An "any performance, as often as you like" promise cannot be kept at any scale that matters. Either members hit a hidden wall, or you breach the licences.

**The royalty is unclear.** Rights holders get 10% of gross box office. Membership money may or may not count as box office. Expect rights holders to say it does, and to want a per-admission allocation. Raise this with them early.

**Cannibalisation is the main financial risk.** Make rough assumptions:

- Your 1,800 frequent attendees bought about a third of single tickets. Say about 80,000 single tickets were sold (88,400 attendances at 68% occupancy, less 7,700 subscription seats). That puts them at about 27,000 tickets and roughly €900k.
- Each of them averages about 15 tickets, about €500 a year. If all 1,800 joined at €180, you would give up about €570k to gain nothing from them. Adverse selection makes this worse, because heavy users join first.
- The 1,100 subscribers pay €182 for seven shows. A €180 unlimited plan undercuts that, so expect subscribers to switch.

**The gym logic runs backwards here.** Gyms make money because most members barely use the product. Theatre seats are perishable and demand is concentrated. Your Friday and Saturday evenings sell out on 80% of dates, so a member in a Saturday seat displaces a €34 buyer. Tuesday to Thursday averages 49%, so there you have about 265 empty seats a night that earn nothing today. Online booking up to two weeks ahead also lets members take seats that would have sold at full price.

**"Cancel any time" is also a weakness.** People plan theatre visits around the productions. Monthly members will join for one show and cancel, so the income is steadier on paper than in practice.

## 2. What it is actually like

| Analogy | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Cinema unlimited passes | MoviePass paid cinemas full price per visit and collapsed. Chain schemes with usage limits, tiers and a fixed monthly price have survived. Lesson: the cost per use must be capped. |
| ClassPass and airline or hotel off-peak deals | Sell the perishable, unsold inventory only. Cap how many slots one venue offers through the scheme. The provider is paid per use. |
| Ski season passes | Priced early, with blackout dates, and tiered by flexibility. The cheaper the pass, the fewer peak days. |
| Museum and orchestra memberships | The value is priority, belonging and a fair-price perk, not a free good. The fee itself is a donation-like income. |
| Rush, standby and young-person schemes | Cheap tickets released shortly before curtain fill empty seats without damaging full-price sales. |
| Your own subscription | It already works as the committed-buyer product. A new scheme must sit alongside it and not undercut it. |

Common lessons:

1. Sell empty inventory, not peak inventory.
2. Cap use per person, per performance and per period.
3. Price per use must stay above the marginal cost, and here above the half-price line.
4. Make the scheme visibly different from the product that pays your bills.

## 3. How I would set it up

**Thrale Midweek Membership, as a 12-month pilot.**

- **What it is.** €15 a month for members, with a 3-month minimum commitment and an annual option. The membership buys access to a member price on Tuesday to Thursday performances only. It is not free entry.
- **Member price.** Set each admission at 55% of the listed face value for that seat, or at least €17 on a €34 seat. That keeps it above the half-price test, so it is not complimentary, it does not use the 4% cap, and it clearly counts as box office for royalty purposes. Confirm with your licensors that "under half" means strictly under and that the fee is not itself treated as a discount.
- **Booking window.** Members can book from 72 hours before the performance, subject to availability, so they do not displace advance full-price buyers. Close the scheme on any performance already above about 70% sold.
- **Sold-out evenings.** Exclude Friday and Saturday completely.
- **Limit.** Cap membership at about 600 founding members for the first year, and cap member seats per performance at about 80.
- **Subscribers.** Keep Season Seven as it is. Give subscribers the member price for extra midweek shows at no fee. This rewards your best audience and avoids switching.
- **Frequent attendees.** The 1,800 high-frequency buyers are your best conversion target for subscriptions. Offer them the subscription on the strength of their history, and keep the membership aimed at new and lapsed people.
- **Comps.** Keep ordinary complimentary tickets within the 4% cap as they are. The 14% no-show rate on comps against 3% on paid tickets suggests paid member seats will behave far better. Track this.

**Rough upside.** Your empty seats come to about 41,600 a season (130,000 capacity less 88,400 attendances). If members fill 5,000 of them at €17, that is about €85k, plus about €100k of fees from 600 members at €15 × 12. Subtract the 10% royalty and the cost of lost subscription switching. This is modest but mostly new money, and the downside is small.

## 4. Before the board meets

1. Ask rights holders, in writing, how membership fees and member-price tickets are treated. Ask counsel to confirm the half-face-value and cap rules.
2. Pull the sales data. Count Tuesday to Thursday performances and their occupancy distribution, and how many of the 1,800 would be at risk.
3. Check whether the 27,000 ticket and €570k figures above hold, since they rely on my assumptions.
4. Set kill criteria: for example, subscription numbers falling more than 5%, or midweek occupancy gain below target after two productions.

Assumptions I made: face value is the listed seat price (average €34), the 4% cap applies per performance as stated, and about a third of your single tickets came from the 1,800 frequent attendees. If any is wrong, the numbers move but the conclusion probably does not.
