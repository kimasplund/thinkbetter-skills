# Is a fire-style firebreak right for ochre wilt?

**Short answer: no, not as proposed.** A 400 m clear-fell ring would probably fail to stop the disease, and it could make things worse. A better model is a human or livestock disease outbreak: find the infected, remove the sources, and control the vector and the movement of material. Fire is the wrong model for the three reasons below.

## Why the fire model breaks

A firebreak works because fire spreads by contiguous adjacency. It burns what it touches, so a gap of bare ground starves it. Ochre wilt differs on each point that matters.

1. **It jumps.** The beetle flies up to 3 km. A 400 m ring is well inside one flight. Fire's closest equivalent is spotting by embers, and foresters know a firebreak does little against a spotting fire.
2. **It is already past the line.** 6 of 20 symptom-free trees just outside the visible patch were infected, about 30%. The sample is small, so the true figure could plausibly lie anywhere from roughly 12% to 54%. Either way, a large share of apparently healthy trees nearby already carry the fungus. Trees show symptoms only 18 to 24 months after infection, so what your crews can see is a picture of infections from one to two years ago. The real edge of the outbreak is unknown and is probably well beyond the 140 symptomatic trees and beyond 400 m. A ring drawn around the visible patch encloses only part of the infection.
3. **Cutting creates fuel.** The beetle breeds in freshly cut or dying beech. A winter clear-fell leaves stacks of fresh beech at the roadside until late summer, which covers the whole flight season from May to July. You would be building a beetle nursery next to the outbreak. In fire terms, you would be piling fuel beside the fire and calling it a break.

There is also a human pathway. Tallis Ford sells firewood cut from the surrounding woods to about 9,000 campers a year, and many take leftover logs home. If infested or beetle-bearing wood is in that supply, the camp is a long-distance spreading mechanism. A thousand kilometres is as easy as 3 km. This pathway is not in the fire model at all, and it may be the highest-consequence risk on your list.

## What this is really like

It resembles an infectious disease with an insect vector, a long latent period and a human-mediated transport route. Three fields have handled that structure:

- **Human and livestock epidemics (foot-and-mouth, cholera).** Contact tracing and a surveillance zone come first, so you learn where the disease is before deciding where to cut. Culling is targeted at infected and dangerously exposed animals, not applied by a fixed radius. Movement controls are central. Cholera control in London in the 1850s began by mapping cases to find the source, not by walling off a district.
- **Malaria and other vector-borne disease.** Control is aimed at the vector's breeding sites and flight season, not only the sick. The beetle's breeding material is the lever: remove or treat it, and the vector cannot multiply.
- **Dutch elm disease and sudden oak death management.** These are the nearest forestry cases, with bark beetles carrying the fungus. Programmes that worked, such as sanitation felling, were fast, prompt in removing infected and breeding material, and paired with monitoring. Programmes that failed often relied on a fixed buffer or left cut wood where beetles could use it. I am recalling these from general knowledge, so ask the plant-health lab for the actual outcomes and best-practice guidance for ochre wilt specifically.

The common principles are: know the true extent before acting, remove the sources and the breeding sites, control movement of carriers (here wood and people), and monitor continuously. Radius-based barriers are used rarely, and only after the extent is known.

## What I would do

**1. Do not approve the ring on 1 December.** Replace it with a decision on a defined first phase. The cost of delay is small if the first phase begins now, since the disease works on an 18 to 24 month cycle and the beetle is inactive until May.

**2. Find the real extent (now to February).**
- Ask the lab for more sampling: symptom-free beech at graded distances, say 100 m, 500 m, 1 km and 3 km from the patch, in all directions, not only outward from the visible stands. Larger samples are needed than 20. Ask for a sampling design that tells you how infection falls with distance.
- Look for beetle activity and symptoms in other stands, especially near Tallis Ford and along roads.
- Map the beech by stand, because a third of 18,000 ha is about 6,000 ha and you cannot treat it all.

**3. Remove the known sources and do it so the beetle cannot use the wood.**
- Fell the symptomatic and confirmed-infected trees this winter. Do not leave the wood in stacks over the summer. Options are to chip, burn on site or under permit, bury, debark and treat, or remove it to a processing site before May. Ask the lab which methods they accept. The key rule is that no fresh or dying beech remains accessible to the beetle from May to July. If any felling is done in this window, the timber must be dealt with before the flight season starts, not at the end of summer. That also means changing the haulage contract, which is your largest practical obstacle. Start that conversation now.
- Treat any felling you do in the surrounding area as part of the same programme, so that no routine felling creates breeding material unnoticed.

**4. Close the firewood route immediately.**
- Stop selling or distributing firewood cut from the surrounding woods at Tallis Ford for next summer, or restrict it to wood from verified uninfected sources or heat-treated wood. Put up signs telling campers not to take logs home, and say why.
- Ask the lab whether the fungus survives in firewood, and whether beetles do. Be careful with that answer, because the dependable rule in plant health is to presume risk until shown otherwise.
- This is cheap, quick and reaches the most distant risk. It could be the first action you take.

**5. Monitor the beetle in season.**
- Put traps along the east side and near Tallis Ford from May, with the lab's advice on which type to use, and check them. Re-survey stands in late summer and each year after, bearing in mind that a stand that looks clean now may not be.

**6. Decide on any wider cutting after the extent is known.**
- If sampling shows infection concentrated near the patch, a larger sanitation cut of infected and heavily exposed trees might be justified, with all wood removed or treated before May. If infection is widespread, containment in the east may no longer be realistic, and the aim shifts to protecting high-value or isolated beech stands and slowing the spread. Take that judgement to the lab and the national plant-health authority. Check whether you have a legal duty to notify them, since many countries require notification of this kind of pest.

## What to say to Ruairi

His instinct is right in an important way. Act early and decisively, use a pre-planned response and pick a clear line. The fire experience is a real asset for logistics, contractors and emergency decision-making. The model is wrong because fire is stopped by distance and fuel breaks, while this disease moves through a flying vector, a long hidden incubation and human transport. The task is closer to tracing and quarantine than to building a barrier. He could lead the operations side, including crews, wood handling and the campsite controls, with the lab leading the epidemiology.

## Where I am less sure

- I do not know what the lab thinks of the 400 m figure, or how the fungus behaves in felled wood or firewood. The lab's answers could change the details.
- The 6 of 20 figure is a single small sample. It tells you the problem is bigger than the visible patch. It does not tell you how much bigger.
- I have assumed no one else, such as a national agency, has authority over the response. If they do, this plan goes through them.
- Other practical factors, such as terrain, budget and public access to the woods, may change the order of work, though not the main logic.

My confidence that the 400 m ring should not be approved as the main response is high, about 85%. My confidence in the details of the alternative is lower, around 60%, until the lab has weighed in.
