**Short answer:** the gym is the wrong model for Thrale Unlimited as pitched. Its recurring income and cancel-any-time sign-up do carry over. Its economics don't, and your licensing terms make a literal "unlimited" unworkable. Other fields have faced your situation, and they suggest a narrower design that you can pilot.

## What the problem is, stripped of theatre

You have a perishable inventory of 130,000 seats a year (520 x 250). You sell 68% of them, and the empty ones are concentrated on Tuesday to Thursday. The peak nights sell out. You want a flat fee that fills the empty seats and attracts new people, without teaching your best customers to pay a fifth of what they pay now. A third party takes a cut of every admission, and it limits how cheaply you can issue one.

## Why the gym fits poorly

A gym makes its money from members who pay and don't come. It also has far more members than capacity, because only a fraction show up at once. Theatre breaks both conditions.

- **Who joins.** At €15 a month, a member breaks even against single tickets after about 5.3 visits a year (€180 / €34). People who go to the theatre often will join. People who rarely go won't. The 1,800 people who bought four or more productions last season are the obvious buyers.
- **The cost of that.** Those 1,800 bought about a third of your single tickets. On roughly 80,000 non-subscriber admissions, that's about 26,000 tickets, or around 14 each (worth about €480 a head). That figure probably includes guests, so check it. If they all moved to €180 a year, you would give up about €300 each, around €540,000. That is roughly 18% of a box office of about €3.0 million (88,400 admissions x €34). You would need roughly 3,000 entirely new members just to break even.
- **Peak seats are the product.** A gym's marginal member costs almost nothing. A member on a Saturday takes a seat you would otherwise sell for €34, and Friday and Saturday sell out on 80% of dates. A two-week booking window lets members take those seats first.

## Closer relatives, and what they teach

**Cinema unlimited cards.** This is the nearest cousin. Cinemas also have a high fixed cost, a perishable seat and low average occupancy.
- Where these cards worked (the French and UK chains, AMC's A-List), they were priced at roughly two admissions' worth a month. They were tied to a 12-month commitment, had caps on use, or both.
- MoviePass is the cautionary tale. It charged under $10 a month and paid theatres full price per visit. Heavy users drove the losses, and it collapsed.
- What carries over: tie the term to a year, cap usage or timing, and do not pay out or promise more per heavy user than the fee brings in.

**Ski season passes.** Resorts sell passes before the season, non-refundable and paid up front. Peak days are blacked out on the cheaper tiers, and the price steps up for fewer restrictions.
- What carries over: sell before the first production opens, so the cash arrives early. Make peak-night access a more expensive tier, not a free extra.

**Airline yield management and standby.** An unsold seat is worth nothing once the curtain goes up. Airlines sell discounted seats in limited buckets, and the discount never touches the flights they expect to fill.
- What carries over: the member allocation should be a fixed number of seats per performance, released against forecast demand. It should not be an open entitlement. Last-minute release of unsold seats (rush or standby) is a well-tested way to fill the Tuesday-to-Thursday gap without discounting Saturday.

**Museum memberships.** Visitors pay for unlimited entry, but most visit only a couple of times a year, and many join partly to support the institution.
- What carries over: a production is seen once, perhaps twice, so you shouldn't expect gym-style repeat visits. The risk lies with your existing heavy attenders, not with a new member who comes every night. Perks such as a bring-a-friend price, priority booking and a members' evening add value that doesn't cost you a seat.

## The constraint that matters most: your licensing terms

Your own numbers settle this. An admission issued at under half its face value (under €17) is complimentary. Complimentary admissions are capped at 4% of the house, or 20 seats per performance (520 x 0.04 = 20.8), and the cap is shared with press and any other complimentary tickets.

- At €15 a month, a member who sees more than about ten performances a year pays under €17 per visit. If the fee is spread across visits, every admission beyond the tenth counts as complimentary.
- On that reading, the cap gives you at most about 5,000 member admissions a year across all 250 performances. That's less than a fifth of what your 1,800 heaviest attenders use now. "Unlimited" is therefore unavailable.
- Complimentary admissions also no-show at 14%, against 3% on paid ones. That matters for the Friday and Saturday seats you can least afford to leave empty.
- I'm reading "issued at" as the fee divided across visits. That reading is my assumption. Have a lawyer and the rights holders confirm it, because it decides the design. If the rights holders agree to a fixed membership allocation per performance, the cap may not bite at all. The 10% rights share on €15 a month is only €1.50, so a negotiated allocation is affordable.

## How I'd set it up

1. **Don't launch "unlimited". Pilot an off-peak pass.** Roughly €15 a month on a 12-month term, billed monthly. It covers Tuesday to Thursday, plus unsold Friday and Saturday seats released 24 to 48 hours ahead. It gives no access to sold-out peak nights.
2. **Hold it under the cap.** Allocate up to about 15 member seats per performance, leaving the rest of the 20 for press and other complimentary tickets. If you assume 100 to 150 Tuesday-to-Thursday performances (please confirm), that is about 1,500 to 2,200 admissions. Tuesday to Thursday averages 49% occupancy, so around 265 seats a night are empty and the pass doesn't displace paying customers there. Cap membership numbers so that demand stays below the seats on offer. Revenue on this scale is modest, perhaps tens of thousands of euros, and the point is to learn.
3. **Protect the heavy buyers.** Don't let the pass undercut Season Seven (€182 for seven productions, about €26 a show). Give the 1,800 frequent buyers a better offer on the existing structure: a discount on extra tickets, or an upgraded subscription. Take care that Season Seven holders don't simply switch to the pass for the same money with more flexibility.
4. **Guests pay.** A member's guest pays a ticket, perhaps at a modest friends' price above €17. That is where new revenue comes from.
5. **Fix no-shows.** Release unclaimed member seats a couple of hours before curtain, and give a strike or fee for repeated no-shows, as class-booking services do.
6. **Pre-agree the board's decision rule.** For example: scale if at least 60% of members were not buyers in the previous two seasons, Tuesday-to-Thursday occupancy rises by a set number of points, and no more than a small share of the 1,800 frequent buyers move over.

## Before the board meets (five weeks)

- **Week 1:** ask counsel and the rights holders about the complimentary definition and a membership allocation. Everything depends on this.
- **Weeks 1 to 2:** pull per-person ticket data for the 1,800 (how many tickets, which nights, how many guests), complimentary use per performance, and the exact count of Tuesday-to-Thursday performances.
- **Week 3:** model three scenarios (10%, 30% and 60% of the 1,800 converting) against a plausible new-member count.
- **Weeks 4 to 5:** write the board paper around the pilot and the decision rule above.

## How sure I am

I'm fairly sure the gym model overstates the upside and that the complimentary cap is a real barrier. I'm less sure of the dollar figures, which rest on my assumptions about guests, the number of Tuesday-to-Thursday performances and how the licensing terms are read. Those facts are all checkable inside your five weeks. I haven't verified the details of individual cinema or museum schemes, so treat them as pointers, not as precedents to cite.
