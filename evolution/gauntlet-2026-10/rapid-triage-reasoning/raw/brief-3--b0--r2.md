**Do not let the tanker take this milk, and do not tip it down the drain to the beck. Hold the tank, sample it, and get a lab result first.**

**Why not load it and trust the dilution**

- Cow 274's two milkings come to about 20 litres in 11,600. That is roughly 1 part in 575.
- The legal limit for penicillin in milk is about 4 parts per billion. Milk from a cow with tubes in can carry thousands of ppb, and sometimes far more, in the first milkings after treatment.
- Even 5,000 ppb diluted 575 times is about 9 ppb. That is over the limit, and the plant's test detects at about that level.
- So your wife may be right, but she may not. I can't tell which from here, and neither can you without a test.
- The two sides of the bet are very uneven. If you hold the milk and it's clean, you lose a day or two. If you ship it and it fails, you pay for about 26,000 litres from four farms plus a penalty. That is well over twice the value of your tank, and the other three farms will know it was yours.

**Do now, before 07:15**

1. **Call the driver and the co-op office.** Tell them there is a possible antibiotic contamination in your tank and that you are not loading today. This costs you nothing and keeps the driver on schedule. Ask whether the driver or the co-op can test your tank on the spot, as some do. If they can and it's negative, that changes the plan.
2. **If you have a beta-lactam rapid test kit on the farm, run it now.** Agitate the tank for at least 5 minutes first so the sample is representative. A kit result takes about 10 minutes. A clear negative is good evidence, but you should still get the lab result before shipping. Don't load on a kit result alone unless the co-op accepts it.
3. **Take two samples from the agitated tank.** Label them with the date, time and "possible penicillin, cow 274". Keep one chilled and retained. Drop the other at the Penmorrow depot by 10:00. Their screen reports by mid-afternoon, and that result is also what your insurer needs.
4. **Leave the milk where it is.** Don't dump anything yet. If you dump before there is a lab result, you lose the insurance claim, and you may throw away €5,000 of milk that was fine.

**Why the milk can wait**

The tank is cooled and can hold until the result comes back. You lose some keeping quality, but a day is manageable. You'll know by mid-afternoon, which is before the 16:00 milking.

**The 16:00 milking is a problem either way**

11,600 + 2,900 is 14,500 litres, which is 1,500 over your 13,000 capacity. The options are:

- **If the lab result is clear:** ask the co-op for an extra collection that afternoon or evening to empty the tank before 16:00.
- **If it isn't back, or it's positive:** milk into the old 6,000-litre tank, kept completely separate from the suspect milk. Clean and sanitise it first. It has to be cooled, so check that it has a working cooler. If it doesn't, ask the co-op or a neighbour for cooling or collection help.
- **Do not mix the afternoon milk into the suspect tank.** That would put clean milk at risk too.

**If the result is positive**

- Don't use the beck. Milk with antibiotic in it is a pollutant, and the cooler failure two years ago was clean milk. Ask the co-op or the environment regulator how to dispose of it, usually to a slurry store at a controlled rate.
- Keep the lab report, the retained sample and Callum's account in writing. Your insurer needs these for the claim.

**Before the next milking**

- Put cow 274 on the dump line and mark her with a new band or leg tape, not just a leg band. The course runs until tomorrow morning, and then the vet's withdrawal period starts. Ask your vet when her milk can go back in the tank.
- Ask Callum whether any other cow lost a band or is on treatment. Check your treatment records against the dump line.

**If the lab comes back clean**

You ship and lose nothing but a day. The tank is the cheapest part of this to protect.
