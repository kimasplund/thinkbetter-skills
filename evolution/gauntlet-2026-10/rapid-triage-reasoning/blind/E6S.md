Do not let the tanker take this milk, and do not dump it yet. Hold it, get it tested, and use the old tank for 16:00. Here is the order I'd do things in.

**Why not just load it**

Your wife's sum is 20 litres in 11,600, which is about 0.17%. That doesn't dilute to nothing. Milk from a cow on penicillin tubes can carry thousands of times the legal limit, and the plant's antibiotic test picks up a few parts per billion. A 600-fold dilution can still leave the tank above the limit. I can't tell you it would fail, but I can't tell you it would pass either. If it fails, you pay for roughly 26,000 litres from four farms plus the penalty, against about €5,000 for your own tank. That is not a risk worth taking to save 40 minutes.

**Now, before 07:15**

1. Ring the co-op and the tanker driver now. Tell them you have a possible antibiotic contamination and want the pickup held. Co-ops handle this regularly, and they may have a procedure such as a sample first or a separate disposal collection. Ask them directly. The driver doesn't need to wait ten minutes. He just doesn't load from your tank, and he can move on to his other farms.
2. Don't touch the outlet. Run the agitator for several minutes, then take two clean, labelled samples (date, time, farm, "possible penicillin contamination"). Keep one cold as a back-up. Photograph the tank level and the parlour software record showing cow 274's two milkings.
3. If you have a beta-lactam rapid test kit on the farm (Delvotest, SNAP or similar), run it now. It only takes a few minutes. A clear fail makes the decision easy. A pass is encouraging but not proof, so still send the sample to the lab.
4. Get a sample to the Penmorrow depot by 10:00. The result comes by mid-afternoon, and you need that result for the insurance claim. A negative result means you can ship the milk at the next collection. A positive result means you have a documented discard for the insurer.

**Why not dump it**

- Tipping milk into the beck is a pollution incident. Milk is very damaging to a watercourse because of its oxygen demand, and this milk would also contain antibiotic. You could be prosecuted and fined. Draining the cooler failure milk two years ago was not a good precedent.
- Dumping it also destroys your insurance claim, because the policy needs a lab result to support it.
- If the result is positive, ask the co-op or your insurer how to dispose of it legally. Options are usually a licensed collection, spreading under the rules, or a slurry store. They will tell you which applies.

**The capacity problem at 16:00**

The tank holds 11,600 litres and its capacity is 13,000, so one more milking of about 2,900 litres brings it to 14,500. That doesn't fit. Two options:

- Use the old 6,000-litre tank for the 16:00 milking (clean and sanitise it this morning, and cool the milk as best you can). Then if the held milk fails, the fresh milk is separate and still sellable. If the held milk passes, the tanker takes everything at the next collection.
- Ask the co-op whether they can do an extra or early collection once the result is in.

Keep that fresh milk separate, and only combine it with the held milk once the result is back.

**Going forward**

- Keep cow 274 on the dump line for the full course plus the withdrawal period. Put a new leg band or tag on her today and mark her clearly in the parlour software.
- Brief Callum, and add a rule that any cow with a lost band is checked against the treatment list before she is milked into the tank.

I've assumed that the co-op accepts the sample route and that the old tank is usable. If either is wrong, the co-op can tell you what they'd rather you did, so make the call first.
