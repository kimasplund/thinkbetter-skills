## Why the deal doesn't work as offered

**1. Your importer cap rules it out.**
- Roasting loses 15%, so your current 21 t of roasted coffee already uses about 24.7 t of green coffee.
- Hollis's 18 t needs about 21.2 t of green. Together that is about 46 t against a 26 t cap.
- The cap allows about 22 t of roasted Alto Merida in total. You have roughly 1 t of headroom, so Hollis's volume would have to come out of your café and web customers.
- If your 21 t is a green figure rather than roasted, the answer is the same. You still can't fit another 21 t of green under 26 t.

**2. The roasting capacity isn't there either.**
- A 5 kg batch every 20 minutes, 8 am to 6 pm on weekdays, is at most about 30 batches a day. That is roughly 150 kg of green a day, or about 37 t a year in theory. Once you allow for warm-up, cleaning, cooling and holidays, 30 t is more realistic.
- You are already using most of that. Adding 21 t of green takes you to about 46 t, which is well over the limit.
- The 15 kg roaster would fix this and the lease hours wouldn't matter. But with a seven-month lead time, an order placed today arrives around May. The deal starts in February.

**3. It breaches your café agreements.** You promised the cafés you wouldn't sell the Alto Merida blend through supermarkets in your county. Hollis has six stores there, and a private-label bag of the same blend almost certainly counts. Those 40 cafés are your core business and your reference customers. Putting them at risk for a supermarket contract that could be lost at the first renewal is a poor trade. Have a solicitor read the exact wording, because that clause decides what you can offer Hollis.

**4. The contract roaster option has its own problem.** Your organic certificate covers your unit only. Any contract roaster would need its own organic certification, and you would have to add them as a subcontractor with your certifier before any Hollis coffee goes out under an organic label. Check that lead time first. You would also still hit the cap, because outsourcing the roasting does nothing about the green coffee supply.

## What I'd do before December

Treat the Hollis offer as a negotiation and don't sign it yet. Faye is right that the buyer's departure is a real deadline. The answer is to get a revised, signable offer in place before then, not to sign something you can't fulfil.

1. **Ask for a different start and a ramp.** Propose a start around September or October, once a new roaster is installed, with volumes stepping up over the first year. Offer a small trial range in the meantime if Hollis wants shelf presence earlier.
2. **Solve the green coffee supply before you promise volume.** Ask your importer now whether the cap can be raised, or lifted for a specific private-label line. If it can't, Hollis could be offered a separate blend from a different origin. That sits outside the Alto Merida cap and probably outside the café promise too, though that depends on the clause wording. Hollis may still prefer their own blend to yours, since it's their label.
3. **Fix the café clause.** The options are to ask the cafés for a written waiver (they may agree if you offer them something in return, such as a price lock or an exclusive blend for them), to exclude the six county stores, or to use a different blend as above. Don't sign before you've picked one.
4. **Only commit to the roaster once the contract is firm.** A £68,000 purchase with a seven-month lead is a bet on Hollis. Make the signing conditional on that, or negotiate a minimum volume commitment from Hollis that justifies the spend.
5. **Protect yourself on terms.** If Hollis ends up taking more than about 40% of your output, you would depend heavily on one buyer. Look at the exit and notice terms and at payment days, and keep your café and web business whole.
6. **Check the "doubling profit" figure.** It was calculated at Hollis's price. Rerun it with the real cost of the extra roaster, certification work, any extra green coffee, and the margin you lose if some café or web volume has to be dropped.

## Best way to grow over the next three years

All of the Alto Merida options share one ceiling, which is about 22 t of roasted coffee a year until 2028. That changes how to rank them.

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Web subscriptions** | **Best first move.** Retail sales earn far more per kilo than wholesale or private label, so you can grow profit without needing more green coffee. It also needs no new machine, no lease change and no breach of the café agreements. |
| **A second, uncapped coffee** | Use a different origin or a new blend for growth in cafés and online. The cap and the café clause apply to Alto Merida only, so this is the cleanest way to add volume. |
| **Hollis, restructured** | Worth pursuing if the supply and café problems are solved, on a different blend, in the right stores, with a roaster purchase tied to it. As offered, no. |
| **Contract roaster** | Only a stopgap, and only if the roaster is organic certified. It doesn't fix the green coffee cap. |
| **Varnland distributor** | Alto Merida volume would again compete for the capped coffee. It could suit a different blend, but the export paperwork, organic certification for export and longer payment terms are big distractions at your size. |
| **Opening your own café** | Last. It needs capital and management time, competes with your own wholesale customers and does nothing about the capacity or supply limits. |

## The three-year shape

- **Now to December:** talk to the importer about the cap, have the café clause reviewed, and take a revised Hollis proposal back to them.
- **Next 6 months:** push web subscriptions, and develop a second coffee that isn't covered by the cap.
- **Once Hollis is on workable terms:** order the 15 kg roaster, with the start date set after it arrives.
- **Before 2028:** renegotiate the importer contract, since that is when the cap lapses and your options widen.
