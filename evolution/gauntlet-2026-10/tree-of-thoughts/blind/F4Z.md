**Short answer: don't sign the Hollis deal as written. You can't legally or physically deliver it. Counter before December with a smaller, different-blend version, and grow the rest through margin rather than Alto Merida volume.**

## Why the deal as offered doesn't work

I've assumed the 21 tonnes is roasted coffee you sell. If it's green input, the conclusion is the same.

**1. The importer cap blocks it.**
- 21 t roasted at 15% weight loss needs about 24.7 t of green coffee. That's already close to your 26 t cap.
- Hollis's 18 t needs about 21.2 t of green on top, so you'd need roughly 46 t of Alto Merida against a 26 t limit.
- Under the cap you have about 1.3 t of green spare, which is roughly 1.1 t of roasted coffee. Even if you stopped supplying cafés entirely, the cap would only cover about 22 t roasted in total.

**2. Your café agreements forbid it.**
- You promised not to sell the Alto Merida blend through supermarkets in the county. Six Hollis stores are in your county.
- Signing would put you in breach with your core customers. Check the exact wording with a solicitor, but don't assume it can be worked around.

**3. Your roasting hours can't cover it.**
- 46 t of green is about 9,200 batches a year. On roughly 250 weekdays that's 37 batches a day, or about 12 hours of roasting in a day when the lease allows 10.
- Today you do about 20 batches a day, around 6.5 hours, so you're comfortable. The extra volume isn't possible on this machine.

**4. The bigger roaster and the contract roaster don't fix the first two problems.**
- The £68,000 machine has a seven-month lead time. Ordered today, it arrives about May, after the February start. You'd also need to confirm the unit, power and lease allow it, and that the organic certificate covers it.
- A contract roaster removes the roasting-hours problem. It does nothing about the importer cap or the café clause, because the blend is still Alto Merida. It also needs its own organic certification, and you lose some margin and control.

So the "doubling profit" figure isn't achievable at that volume and blend. Faye's deadline is real, but a signature that puts you in breach with two parties is worse than losing the buyer.

## What I'd do instead

**Before December, counter Hollis with a version you can deliver.**
- **A different organic blend, not Alto Merida.** It sits outside both the importer cap and the café clause, and you'd need a new importer or a separate contract for it. Hollis may accept a Kittiwake-branded or private-label alternative, so ask.
- **A staged volume.** For example 4 to 6 t in year one, rising only once capacity exists. 4 to 6 t of roasted coffee is about 5 to 7 t of green, or roughly 1,000 to 1,400 batches. That's likely workable on the current machine with some Saturday-free scheduling, but check your actual batch turnaround.
- **Contract terms that protect you.** A start date tied to when you can actually supply, a minimum purchase commitment, a price review each year, and an exit if they drop the range. Don't let one buyer who is leaving in December set the terms of a three-year relationship.
- **Order the bigger roaster only once Hollis commits to volume** that justifies £68,000. Get the lease, power and organic certificate confirmed first.

If Hollis insists on Alto Merida, the honest answer is no, or only outside the county with the café clause and the importer cap both addressed in writing first. Asking your importer for a higher cap, and your cafés to release the clause, are both possible but slow, and neither is likely to be settled by December.

## The other paths, given those limits

Until 2028 your Alto Merida can only grow by about 5%. So growth has to come from higher margin per kilo or from a second product.

| Path | Fit |
|---|---|
| **Web subscriptions** | Best first move. Retail margin per kilo is far higher than wholesale, and it uses the spare 1 t or so of Alto Merida well. Moving some café volume to subscriptions raises profit without raising tonnage. |
| **Hollis, reshaped as above** | Good, because it adds volume from a new origin without touching the cap or the clause. Worth doing at a smaller scale. |
| **Contract roaster** | Useful only for the second blend, and only if it's organically certified. Keep it as a fallback for overflow rather than the plan. |
| **Varnland distributor** | Not blocked by the café clause, but it still draws on the capped Alto Merida. Only worthwhile with the second blend or a higher cap. |
| **Own café** | Competes for cash with the roaster purchase and for your time. It's the least connected to the constraints you actually have, so I'd leave it until the others are running. |

**A three-year shape that fits your constraints:**
1. **Now to December:** negotiate with Hollis on a second-origin blend, and ask your importer what a higher cap or a second origin would cost.
2. **2027:** grow subscriptions, supply Hollis in stages, and decide on the 15 kg roaster once Hollis volume is committed in writing.
3. **2028 onward:** when the cap expires, renegotiate the importer contract and the café clause together, and then consider Varnland or a café.

## What would change this advice

- If your cafés would release the supermarket clause in exchange for something, such as price or exclusivity of a different blend, the county problem goes away. The importer cap would still need to be fixed.
- If the importer would lift the cap for a fee, Alto Merida at larger volume becomes possible, but you'd still need the roaster or a certified contract roaster.
- If your real roasting speed is slower than 20 minutes per batch once you count loading and cooling, the roasting-hours problem is worse than I've shown.

Please take the clause wording to a solicitor and the cap wording to your importer before replying to Hollis. I'd be reasonably confident in the conclusion, though the exact numbers depend on whether 21 t is roasted or green weight.
