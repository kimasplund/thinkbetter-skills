**Short answer:** Neither side of the split is right as the proposals stand. Your best chance of still being here in ten years is to build around the upper mountain, which is the only part that reliably has snow. The bike park is the better of the two options your members are arguing over, but only if the Trust agrees to change the lease first. Full snowmaking on the lower runs should come off the table.

**1. Snowmaking fails on water, before cost or climate**

- 9 ha × 4,500 m³ is about 40,500 m³ a season. Your licence allows 40,000 m³.
- The beginner-area guns already used 31,000 m³ last winter, so about 9,000 m³ is left. At the consultant's own rate, that covers roughly 2 ha, not 9.
- Covering the whole lower area would need a larger abstraction licence. That is a regulatory risk you don't control, and it is not a given.
- Even with water, 650–900 m gave 18 skiable days. Snowmaking only works in cold enough hours, and those hours are scarcest at the elevations where you most need them. €2.1M (about €1.05M net of the fund) is a large bet on a marginal site.

Ask the consultant to re-run her figures against the licence and to show the number of producible days at 650–900 m in recent warm winters. A small snowmaking extension within the 9,000 m³ headroom could still make sense, for example to secure a link run or a beginner and school area. It can't be the strategy.

**2. The bike park is attractive but blocked by the lease**

- Your lease with the Grazing Commons Trust limits you to winter sports between 1 December and 15 April. As written, you can't run a summer bike park on the upper slopes.
- The lease has about nine years left (ten years from 2025). That is below the 15 years the tourism fund needs for assets on leased land, so the 50% grant would not apply to the bike park either. Your €850k would be fully self-funded unless the lease is extended.
- The consultant's "double annual revenue" claim needs testing. Ask what visitor numbers, spend per visit and season length it rests on. Bike parks have long ramp-up periods and real running costs: trail maintenance, insurance, staffing and lift operating costs.

On the other hand, the upper mountain has 71 skiable days against 18, and a bike park shares the chairlift you need to keep anyway. It diversifies you into months when you earn nothing today. Those are real strengths, so the lease problem is worth solving.

**3. Make the lease the first action**

Before the November vote, open talks with the Trust. You want two things:

- A variation permitting summer, non-winter-sports use, with sensible conditions on grazing, trail routing and access.
- An extension that leaves at least 15 years remaining. That would make the upper-slope capital eligible for the 50% grant, which would cut the bike park to roughly €425k net.

If the Trust refuses either, the bike park is dead and you should know that before spending anything. Make the board's approval conditional on those answers. Check the fund's rules on whether a signed extension counts when the application is made.

**4. The dry slope and tubing lane is the underrated option**

- It sits on land you own, so it qualifies for the 50% grant: €1.4M becomes about €700k net.
- It runs in summer and in poor-snow winters without depending on the Trust or on water.
- Its weakness is revenue. Dry slopes and tubing are lower-yield and often seasonal or weather-driven, and I can't see a demand estimate for them. Treat it as a second-phase option to test, not a rescue.

**5. The upper-only operation is the foundation, not a retreat**

The €600k Summit chairlift refit is needed under almost every plan that works, because the bike park also depends on that lift. Giving up the lower runs saves operating cost on land that had 18 days. The shuttle from the car park is the weak point: it adds friction for families, so cost it and plan the arrival experience properly. The lower land is valuable for other uses (tubing, dry slope, parking, trails, base facilities) rather than as skiing terrain.

**6. Selling to Ardane is the worst fit for the next few years**

- If land is sold within ten years of the 2019 purchase, the €1.2M buyout grant has to be repaid. That clause runs until 2029, which makes any sale before then a €1.2M handicap on the price. Get advice on whether a sale of shares or assets would also trigger it.
- It ends community ownership, which is presumably your reason for existing.
- Keep the relationship warm. A sale becomes a sensible fallback after 2029 if the plan fails, and an informal approach doesn't need an answer now.

**Recommended direction**

1. Commit to the upper mountain as the core business and budget the €600k chairlift refit. Check the lift's condition and what the refit really covers.
2. Open lease negotiations immediately, seeking summer use and a term of at least 15 years remaining.
3. Make the bike park conditional on those results. If they come through, proceed with a funded bike park, probably phased (start with a smaller set of trails, learn demand, then expand).
4. Drop full lower-slope snowmaking. Consider a small scheme within the 9,000 m³ headroom only if it demonstrably saves days.
5. Test demand for the dry slope and tubing on the lower land, and apply for the 50% grant if it stacks up.
6. Don't sell before 2029 unless the other routes fail.

**What could change this advice**

- If the Trust refuses summer use or an extension, the plan becomes upper-only skiing plus lower-land tubing or dry slope, with a hard look at whether skiing alone is viable. A sale would then be worth discussing.
- If the water regulator would grant a larger licence and the consultant can show enough cold hours at 650–900 m, snowmaking looks different, but that needs evidence you don't have yet.
- I've assumed the figures you gave are accurate and that the grant works as you describe. I also haven't seen the operating costs or cash position behind each option. Your finances may limit how many of these you can afford at once, so get the board to ask for a funded two-to-three year cash plan alongside the November vote.
