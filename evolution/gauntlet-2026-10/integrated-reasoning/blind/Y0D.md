My recommendation is not to buy any of the three kilns by Friday. Your own numbers suggest the kiln isn't what is causing the crazing, and crazing is the problem you most need to fix.

**Why the evidence points away from the kiln**

- The timing doesn't fit. Your kiln elements were replaced in January and rejects stayed near 2% until May. The one thing that changed in between is the clay body, which you switched in April. Crazing shows up weeks after delivery, so a May jump fits April's new body.
- The test kiln is two years old and a different machine, and it shows about 8% since May. A new kiln should have fixed that, but a different kiln gives nearly the same rate. Crazing follows the pieces, not the kiln they were fired in.
- Crazing that appears after dishwasher cycles is usually a thermal expansion mismatch between glaze and body. The glaze is under tension and cracks as the piece takes up moisture and heats and cools. A cheaper body usually has a different expansion and porosity. Your satin white was tuned to the old body, so it may no longer fit the new one.

I'd put this at about 85% likely. The remaining 15% covers things I can't see from here, such as a changed glaze material batch or a firing-schedule drift. Even then, a new kiln is only a fix if the cause is the firing, and the test kiln result argues against that.

**What this means for each kiln**

- **Kiln B (€14,900):** Controlled cooling can modestly affect crazing, but it is the least likely of the three to solve this, because the test kiln already fires on a different schedule and still crazes. It also adds no capacity. Its price is under €15,000, so Ines could approve it alone, but that doesn't make it the right purchase.
- **Kiln C (€22,100):** Gas and reduction firing is a new product line, not a fix. Reduction changes how glazes behave, so it would add a second glaze-fit problem while you're already fighting one. Two restaurants asking about reduction glazes is interest, not orders. I'd revisit it once crazing is solved and Tomasz can show real demand.
- **Kiln A (€18,400):** This is the only option that addresses what you have actually lost, which is capacity. The two refused accounts are real. But it needs all three partners to agree, it has a six-week lead time, and buying it now won't touch the 9% reject rate. If you buy it without fixing the body and glaze mismatch, a bigger chamber will simply produce more pieces that craze.

**Why the Friday deadline shouldn't drive this**

An expiring quote is a sales tool. Ask the vendor for a two-to-three week extension. If they refuse, you lose a price, not a business. Spending €18,400 on a hunch, with a reject problem that the purchase won't change, is the larger risk. I'd also note that a vendor who says the price disappears on Friday is the pitch you said you distrust.

**What to do instead, starting this week**

1. Fire the same glaze on the old clay body and the new one, in both kilns, side by side. If crazing follows the body in both kilns, you have your answer. This costs a few firings.
2. Ask your clay supplier for the thermal expansion figure of the new body and compare it with the old one. Also check whether the body is more porous when fired.
3. If the body is the cause, you have two ways out. Adjust the glaze to fit the new body, usually by lowering its expansion, for example with more silica or less sodium and potassium flux. Or go back to the old supplier and accept the higher price. Compare that price against what free remakes now cost you.
4. Run a boiling-water or steam-and-cool test on fired samples. Crazing shows up in hours instead of weeks, so you can check fixes without waiting for restaurant complaints.

**The capacity argument for the partners**

At 2,400 pieces a week, the jump from 2% to 9% rejects means roughly 170 more pieces a week going to free remakes, which is about 7% of your output. Fixing the crazing recovers some of the capacity you thought you needed a new kiln for. It won't cover everything, so Kiln A may still be right later. But you will decide on real numbers: how many pieces a week you can make at 2% rejects, and whether demand still exceeds that.

**How to put it to Ines and Tomasz**

- To Ines: the test kiln is the evidence. Controlled cooling on Kiln B is the wrong tool for a mismatch between body and glaze.
- To Tomasz: reduction glazes are worth pursuing after the glaze is stable. Right now the satin white is failing on the pieces already in restaurants.
- To yourself: your lean toward A is sound as a capacity decision. Make it once the crazing is fixed and you know your true capacity, with everyone's agreement, not under a Friday deadline.
