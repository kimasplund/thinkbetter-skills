## Why the kiln is probably not the cause (about 80% confident)

- **The timing points at the clay change, not the kiln.** Rejects were about 2% for a year. The clay body changed in April and rejects jumped in May. The kiln elements were replaced in January, and nothing went wrong for about four months afterwards.
- **Your test kiln is the strongest evidence.** It is a different, two-year-old kiln, and it crazes at about 8%, close to the main kiln's 9%. If the 14-year-old kiln were the problem, the test kiln should look much better. A new kiln would not change what the clay and glaze do together.
- **Crazing is a fit problem between glaze and clay body.** The glaze shrinks more than the body as it cools, so it cracks. Your glaze recipe is unchanged, but the body underneath it is different. The same recipe on a new body can have a different fit. The cheaper body is the obvious suspect. Delayed failure after dishwashers is typical of this. Repeated heat, moisture and detergent expose marginal fit, and some bodies also take up moisture and swell slowly.

The main alternatives, roughly by likelihood:

| Cause | Rough likelihood | What would show it |
|---|---|---|
| New clay body's expansion or porosity no longer matches the satin white glaze | ~65% | Old-body pieces in the same firing stay clean, new-body pieces craze |
| New body is under-matured at your firing schedule (more porous) | ~15% | Higher water absorption; improves with a hotter or longer soak |
| Cooling rate or kiln behaviour | ~10% | Would not explain the test kiln matching |
| Glaze batch or application thickness drift that happened to coincide | ~10% | Craze correlates with thicker glaze or a specific glaze batch |

## What to do this week (cheap, and it decides the question)

1. **Run a side-by-side test.** Take the old-body clay if any is left (or a small batch from the old supplier) and the new body. Glaze both with the same satin white and fire them in the same load. If only the new body crazes, that settles it.
2. **Test for crazing quickly.** Put fired test pieces through a few dishwasher cycles, or use a boiling-then-cold-water test or an autoclave test. You will know in days rather than weeks.
3. **Ask the new supplier** for the body's thermal expansion figure and recommended firing range, and compare them with the old body.
4. **Adjust the glaze to suit the new body.** Typical changes are a glaze with a lower expansion (more silica, less sodium or potassium flux) or a small change to the firing schedule. Try several variations in the test kiln first.
5. **Do the arithmetic on the clay saving.** At 9% rejects with free remakes, plus the cost of the two to three weeks of exposure at the restaurants, the cheaper clay may be costing more than it saves. Going back to the old supplier is a real option.

If the fit is corrected, you should return to about 2%. That recovers roughly 7% of 2,400 pieces a week, about 170 pieces a week of remakes that currently use up capacity. That is a good part of the reason you are at capacity.
