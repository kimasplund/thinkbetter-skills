## Why the kiln is probably not the problem

Crazing in pieces that fail weeks later, after dishwashers, is usually a glaze-to-body fit problem. The glaze shrinks more than the clay body as it cools, or the body takes up moisture and expands. Your own facts point that way:

| Clue | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Rejects went from 2% to 9% starting in May | Something changed in April or May |
| You switched clay body in April and left the glaze unchanged | The glaze may no longer fit the new body |
| The 2-year-old test kiln shows about 8% since May | A different kiln, not just your old one, has the same problem |
| The main kiln got new elements in January | The timing doesn't match, since the problem began about 4 months later |

I'd put roughly 85% on the clay body and glaze mismatch as the main cause. The new body may have a different thermal expansion. It may also mature at a different temperature and end up more porous in your firing schedule. The remaining 15% is mostly a cooling-rate effect that both kilns share, such as the same schedule or the same loading habits. That's the one way a kiln with controlled cooling (B) would help, but a new kiln wouldn't fix it automatically, because you'd still need to tune the schedule.

None of the three kilns fixes a glaze that doesn't fit the clay. If you buy one now and the crazing continues, you'll have spent €15-22k and still be remaking 9% of your output.

## What fixing the crazing is worth in capacity

At 2,400 pieces a week, going from 9% back to 2% frees about 7% of your output, roughly 170 pieces a week. That is about the size of two typical accounts, since 2,400 pieces across 40 restaurants averages about 60 pieces each. In other words, fixing the glaze fit may remove much of the capacity squeeze for free. It also stops the unpaid remakes and the damage to restaurant relationships. This assumes your 2,400 figure counts good, shipped pieces, so check it against your own numbers.

So whether you need A depends on how much room you have after the fix. If, once rejects are back near 2%, you still can't take the accounts you're turning away, A is a sound buy. If you can, you may not need to spend €18,400.

## What to do this week

1. **Ask the kiln supplier for an extension** on the quote, even two to four weeks, or a refundable hold on the price. A six-week lead time means Friday matters less than it looks. A quote that expires usually gets renewed, perhaps at a slightly higher price. Weigh that small risk against spending €18,400 on the wrong premise.
2. **Run a glaze-fit test now.** Fire the same glaze on the new clay body and on any remaining old-body pieces, in both kilns. Then test them with a hot-water or steam cycle, or a dishwasher run if you can arrange one. You'd have a clear answer in a few days. If the old body doesn't craze and the new one does in both kilns, that confirms the body and glaze are the cause.
3. **Adjust the glaze or the body.** Typical fixes are changing the glaze recipe to lower its expansion, for example by adjusting silica or flux, or going back to the old clay supplier if the savings don't justify the rejects. Ask the new supplier for the expansion data on their body and which glazes they recommend.
4. **Tally how much volume you turned down** and what the two accounts were worth. That, not the crazing, decides whether A pays back.
