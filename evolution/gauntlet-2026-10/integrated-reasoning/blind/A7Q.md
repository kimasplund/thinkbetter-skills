**Short answer: don't buy any of the three kilns by Friday. Your own numbers suggest the kiln isn't the cause of the crazing.**

**Why the kiln is probably not the problem**

- Crazing went from about 2% to 9% starting in May. In April you changed clay body suppliers, and the glaze recipe stayed the same. The timing fits the clay change.
- Your two-year-old test kiln, which is a different kiln with different elements, shows about 8% crazing over the same period. If the 14-year-old kiln were the cause, the test kiln should be much better. It isn't, so the problem follows the clay and glaze rather than the kiln.
- The kiln's elements were replaced in January. The rise started in May, so that doesn't fit either.

Crazing means the glaze shrinks more than the clay body as the piece cools, a mismatch called glaze fit. A new clay body has a different thermal expansion. A glaze that fitted the old body for six years can easily fail to fit the new one. Crazing that only shows up after weeks of dishwasher cycles is typical of a marginal fit, because moisture and heat cycling make the cracks appear.

A new kiln would not fix this. Kiln B's controlled cooling can shift the result slightly, but it won't compensate for a body and glaze that don't match. I'd treat the 9% as a materials problem until a test shows otherwise.

**What it's costing you**

At 2,400 pieces a week, 9% is about 215 rejects a week, against about 48 at 2%. That is roughly 165 extra remakes a week. Those remakes eat the capacity that made you turn away two accounts. Fixing crazing is likely worth more extra output than any of the kilns would give you, and it costs far less. It also protects your relationships with the 40 restaurants you already have.

- **A** is the only option that adds capacity. Capacity is your real constraint, but you would be adding it while 9% of output is being remade. Fix the rejects first and you may find you already have more room than you think. Its six-week lead time and Friday expiry are a sales deadline. Ask the supplier for an extension. Quotes are often extended, especially for a purchase this size.
- **B** is the cheapest, and I'd call it a reasonable later upgrade, mainly for firing control. But it sits just under the €15,000 line, so Ines could approve it alone. Don't let that line decide it. A decision this size is worth having all three partners agree on.
- **C** is the weakest case right now. Two restaurants asking about reduction glazes is thin demand against a €22,100 spend, a different fuel, and new skills. Reduction firing would also change how your glazes look and behave, which compounds the problem you already have.

**What I'd do this week**

1. Ask the kiln supplier for a 30-day extension on Kiln A's quote, in writing. If they refuse, letting it lapse costs you little, since the evidence says the kiln isn't the fix.
2. Call your clay supplier and ask for the body's thermal expansion data, and compare it with the old body. Ask for any changes in formulation.
3. Run a quick test. Fire the same glaze on the old clay body (if you have leftover stock or can buy a small amount) and on the new body in the same kiln, then put the pieces through repeated dishwasher or thermal-shock cycles. If only the new body crazes, you have your answer.
4. If the new body is the cause, adjust the glaze to fit it. Usual routes are adding silica or reducing the high-expansion fluxes, which a glaze technician or your glaze supplier can advise on. Or go back to the old body, accepting its higher price. Compare the price difference with the cost of roughly 165 extra remakes a week.
5. Once the reject rate is back to about 2%, you'll know your true capacity and can decide on a kiln with real numbers.

**What to take to Ines and Tomasz**

"The test kiln shows the same crazing as the main kiln, and it started when we changed clay. Buying a kiln won't fix that. We should ask for a quote extension, test the clay and glaze fit, and decide on a kiln after that." This avoids picking a winner between your three positions. Each of you may still be right about the longer term. A may be right for space, B for control, and C if reduction demand grows. The order matters, though: fix the glaze fit first, then buy.

**Assumptions and limits**

I'm assuming the test kiln's 8% is on the same new clay and the same glaze, and that the crazing is true crazing rather than another defect such as a firing fault. I haven't seen the glaze recipe, firing schedules or the clay's specifications. A test that confirms the clay is the cause would settle this. If it came back clean, with old and new body behaving the same, I'd revisit the kiln question, and A would then be the likeliest choice for the extra capacity.
