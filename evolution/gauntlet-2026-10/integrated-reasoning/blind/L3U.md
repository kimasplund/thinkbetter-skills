Short answer: don't buy any of the three kilns to fix the crazing, and don't let Friday's deadline decide anything. Your own numbers say the kiln is probably not the cause.

**Why the kiln is unlikely to be the problem**

- Your small test kiln is two years old and has crazed at about 8% since May. Your main kiln is 14 years old and is at 9%. Two very different kilns are producing nearly the same result, so the common factor is the ware, not the firing equipment.
- The timing fits the clay change. You switched clay bodies in April and the rate jumped from about 2% to 9% from May, after six years of the same glaze. Nothing else you listed changed at that point. The January element replacement came months earlier and doesn't line up.
- The delayed failure also fits. Crazing that shows up two or three weeks later, after dishwasher cycles, is the typical sign of a glaze that sits under tension on its body (a thermal expansion mismatch). Moisture uptake and repeated heating and cooling make it worse over time. A new, cheaper body can easily have a different expansion and moisture behavior than the old one, even with an identical glaze recipe.

I'd put this at roughly 75-80% likely to be a body-and-glaze fit problem. The remaining chance is mostly that firing schedule or cooling contributes at the margin. I can't rule that out from here, but it wouldn't explain why both kilns behave the same.

**What the kilns would and wouldn't do**

| | A (€18,400) | B (€14,900) | C (€22,100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixes crazing? | No | Maybe slightly (controlled cooling) | No, and it changes the firing atmosphere, so your satin white would need re-testing |
| Adds capacity? | Yes, about 30% | No | Unclear, depends on the chamber size, which isn't in the quote |
| Approval needed | All three partners | Ines alone (under €15,000) | All three partners |
| Main risk | Buying more capacity while a 9% reject rate eats it | Spending money on cooling control that may not address the cause | Largest outlay, serves two restaurants' interest, and doesn't address either problem |

Capacity and crazing are two separate problems, and the kiln quotes only bear on the first. At a 9% reject rate, a meaningful share of your output is being remade for free, so some of your "at capacity" problem is rework. Cutting rejects from 9% back to about 2% frees roughly 7% of your weekly output, around 170 pieces, without any new equipment. That may not be enough to take on the accounts you turned down, but it is real.

**What I'd do instead, starting this week**

1. Run a controlled test. Fire the same glaze on the old clay body and on the new one, in the same load and kiln. If the old body doesn't craze, you have your answer. If you no longer have any of the old clay, ask the old supplier for a small sample.
2. Ask the new clay supplier for the body's thermal expansion data and ask how it differs from the old body.
3. Test the standard fixes on the new body: adjust the glaze toward lower expansion (more silica, or less of the high-expansion fluxes), or fire a little longer or hotter for a better glaze-body interface. Then run test pieces through the dishwasher repeatedly, since that is where the failures show up. Your local ceramics supplier or a glaze consultant can help with this, and it costs far less than a kiln.
4. If the new body can't be made to fit, the price saving from the cheaper clay is probably smaller than the cost of remaking 9% of your output. Compare the two numbers directly.

**About Friday**

Letting the quote lapse costs you little. Kiln A is the only one with a real deadline, and a six-week lead time means that even if you signed today, it wouldn't help your crazing for more than a month. Ask the vendor for a two- or three-week extension; vendors usually agree when a sale is plausible. If they won't, you lose a quote, not a crisis. Buying A because of the date, while the cause of the rejects is still unknown, is the one outcome I'd actively argue against.

**If you want something to bring to Ines and Tomasz**

- To Ines: the evidence points at the clay switch, not the kiln, and her preferred kiln B only helps if cooling is part of the problem, which the test kiln result argues against. Under €15,000 it is her call, but I'd ask her to hold off until the body test is done.
- To Tomasz: reduction glazes are a legitimate product question, but they are a separate decision from fixing rejects, and a gas kiln is the most expensive way to find out whether two restaurants will actually order. Ask both restaurants how many pieces they would buy, and maybe test a reduction glaze on a trial basis first.
- To both: the one-week clay-body test is cheap and its result changes which kiln, if any, makes sense. If the test shows the clay is the cause and you fix it, revisit capacity afterward, and A is then a reasonable choice for the reason you already gave. Because it exceeds €15,000, it needs all three of you to agree, so it makes sense to decide it together once the reject rate is known.

What would change my view: if the old clay body also crazes now, or if the test kiln's firing differs a lot from the main kiln's in cooling rate, then firing becomes a stronger suspect, and B's controlled cooling deserves a second look. But I'd check the clay first.
