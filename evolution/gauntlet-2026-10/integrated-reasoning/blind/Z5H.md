My recommendation is not to buy any of the three kilns by Friday. The evidence says the kiln isn't what's causing the crazing, so none of these purchases would fix it. Here is the reasoning, then the kiln comparison you asked for, then what I'd take to Ines and Tomasz.

## Why the kiln is probably not the cause

Crazing is a mismatch between how much the glaze and the clay body shrink as they cool. The glaze ends up under tension and cracks, and dishwashers and repeated heating cycles bring it out weeks later. The glaze is held in tension by the body, so changing either one changes the fit.

What your own facts show:

- **Timing.** Rejects were about 2% for years and became 9% from May. You changed clay body in April. The glaze recipe didn't change. That is the only major input that changed just before the problem started.
- **The test kiln.** It is a different, newer kiln and it shows about 8% crazing since May, close to the main kiln's 9%. If the main kiln were the problem, the test kiln would look much better. It doesn't.
- **The elements.** You replaced them in January, and rejects stayed low through April. A kiln fault would have shown up then, not four months later.

I'd put roughly 80 to 85% on the cause being a glaze-to-body fit problem from the new clay body. The remaining probability is mostly a changed batch, supplier or process detail that I can't see from here, such as a different firing schedule on certain loads, a different glaze supplier lot, or a shift in how the pieces are cooled. A kiln fault is the least likely explanation.

This changes the decision. If you buy a kiln now, you are likely to spend €15,000 to €22,000 and still have 9% rejects.

## The cost of waiting is smaller than it looks, and the cost of the crazing is larger

At 2,400 pieces a week, 9% rejects is about 216 pieces a week remade for free, against about 48 at 2%. That is roughly 170 extra pieces a week of capacity going to remakes, plus the free replacements, shipping and the reputation risk with 40 restaurants. Getting back to 2% frees about 7% of your output, which on its own is probably close to the room you needed for the two accounts you turned down. So fixing the crazing is probably the cheapest capacity you can add, and it should come before buying anything.

Notes on each:

- **Kiln A** is the only option that adds capacity, which matches your stated problem of turning down work. But you would be scaling up a process that currently throws away 9% of what it makes. A larger kiln with the same body and glaze would produce more of the same rejects.
- **Kiln B** is the one with a real link to crazing, but only a partial one. Controlled, programmable cooling does affect how stress builds up in the glaze, and a slower or better-shaped cooling curve can reduce crazing at the margin. It will not fix a real expansion mismatch between the glaze and the new body. You can test that benefit cheaply first (see below). B also costs less than €15,000, so it falls under Ines's authority and doesn't need all three partners.
- **Kiln C** is the answer to a different question, which is whether you want to add reduction-fired work. Two restaurants asking about reduction glazes is a real signal, but it is a new product line, and it would mean a different kiln type, gas supply and ventilation, and a learning curve. It doesn't address either the crazing or your capacity problem, and it is the most expensive option. I'd treat it as a separate, later decision that needs more demand evidence than two enquiries.

Between the three, if you did need to buy something this week, A is the strongest on your actual stated need (capacity), and I'd rank B second and C third. But I wouldn't buy any of them before knowing the crazing cause, because the answer could change what you need.

## What to do instead of deciding by Friday

1. **Ask for the quote to be extended.** A 6-week lead time and a deadline are a sales tactic. Ask Kiln A's supplier to hold the price for two to four weeks while you finish an internal review. Most suppliers will, and if they refuse you lose very little, since the kiln doesn't solve the current problem.
2. **Test the cause directly, this week.** Take pieces of the old clay body if you have any left, and the new body, and glaze and fire them side by side in the same load, in both kilns. Then run them through your dishwasher cycle or a boiling-then-chilling test. If the old body is clean and the new body crazes in both kilns, the cause is confirmed. This costs almost nothing.
3. **Talk to the glaze and clay suppliers.** Ask the new clay supplier for the thermal expansion figure of the body and compare it with your old body. A glaze that fit the old body well may now be too high in expansion for the new one. The usual fixes are adjusting the glaze (more silica, less of the high-expansion fluxes) or choosing a body that matches the old one. Get a ceramics technician to review the recipe if you don't have one in-house.
4. **Do the sums on the clay.** The new body was cheaper, but 9% rejects, remakes and goodwill likely cost more than the saving. Compare the price per sellable piece, not per kilo of clay.
5. **Then decide on the kiln, with the capacity question separated out.** Once rejects are back near 2%, re-measure how many pieces a week you can actually produce and how much demand you are turning away. If you are still at capacity, A is the natural choice and you can bring it to Ines and Tomasz with real numbers. If cooling control turns out to help your recipes, B is the cheaper step and it is Ines's call.

## What to say to Ines and Tomasz

- All three of you are partly right: Ines is right that cooling control matters to glaze quality, Tomasz is right that reduction glazes are a market signal, and you are right that space is your real constraint. None of those is the cause of the 9%.
- Propose a short, time-boxed investigation (two to three weeks) with a clear question: is the new clay body the cause? Then make the kiln decision with a known rejection rate.
- Agree in advance on the trigger for buying. For example: if rejects are back under 3% and you are still turning away accounts, you buy A. That way the kiln discussion is about capacity, which all three of you can agree is the real need, and Tomasz's reduction idea can be evaluated separately with orders or commitments from the restaurants.

- I'm assuming the test-kiln figure of about 8% is for the same glaze and clay body, fired on a similar schedule. If it used a different body or glaze, my conclusion is weaker.

The short version for Friday: don't sign. Ask for an extension, run the clay test this week, and decide on the kiln once you know whether the crazing is a body-and-glaze problem, which is what the evidence points to.
