## Why the kiln is probably not your crazing problem

Crazing happens when the glaze shrinks more than the clay body as they cool, so the glaze ends up under tension and cracks. Dishwasher cycles then make the cracks grow, which is why restaurants report it 2-3 weeks later. Here is what your own facts say:

- **The timing matches the clay change.** You switched clay bodies in April, rejects jumped in May, and the glaze recipe is unchanged after six years. Rejects were 2% on the old body and are 9% on the new one.
- **Your test kiln rules out the main kiln.** It is two years old, a different machine, and it shows about 8% crazing since May. If the 14-year-old kiln were the cause, the test kiln should be fine. Two kilns with different ages, sizes and elements giving the same result points to something they share, and the shared factor is the clay and glaze.
- **The January element replacement doesn't fit.** It happened four months before the problem started, and the test kiln has no such change.

I'd put roughly 80-85% on "the new clay body no longer fits your satin white glaze." The remaining chance is mostly a firing or cooling contribution that makes a marginal fit worse. Controlled cooling (Kiln B) can help at the edges, but it wouldn't rescue a body and glaze that are mismatched. Kiln C (gas, reduction) changes the firing atmosphere, which would change how your glaze and body behave and could make the fit problem different, not better.

The cheap way to confirm this, which can be done this week:

1. Fire the same glaze on a few test tiles or pieces from the old clay body (if you have any left, or can buy a small batch from the old supplier) and from the new body, in the same load. Compare.
2. Ask the new supplier for the body's thermal expansion data and compare it with your glaze's. A lower-expansion glaze, or a body with higher expansion, usually fixes it.
3. Run the new body through a boiling-water or autoclave crazing test on fired samples, so you don't have to wait 2-3 weeks for restaurant feedback.

Typical fixes are a small glaze adjustment (more silica or a lower-expansion flux, in consultation with your glaze supplier), changing the body or supplier again, or a slightly different firing schedule. These cost hundreds of euros, not €15,000 or more.

## What the crazing is costing you

At 2,400 pieces a week, 9% rejects is about 215 pieces a week, versus about 48 at 2%. That is roughly 170 pieces a week of lost capacity, all remade free. Fixing the crazing recovers about 7% of capacity, which is about the same as what you'd get from running the old kiln slightly harder. It also stops the customer-trust damage with your 40 restaurants. And you're turning away work because you're at capacity while spending that capacity on remakes.
