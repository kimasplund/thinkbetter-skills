No, it doesn't hold up. Four of the five steps have a problem, and once they are corrected the oven pays back in about 137 trading days (roughly 5.3 months), not 77. That misses the board's four-month test. Here is each step in order.

**Step 1: capacity (648 loaves a day, up 216)**

The D9 bakes 72 loaves per load, but the proofing cabinet holds only 60 and proofs in the same 40 minutes. Dough goes straight from proofer to oven, so the proofer limits each load to 60 loaves.

- Output is 9 × 60 = 540 loaves a day, not 648.
- Today's output is 9 × 48 = 432, so the gain is 108 loaves a day, not 216.
- That is below the roughly 150 loaves of unmet demand, so the oven would not clear the backlog.

**Step 2: profit (150 loaves × €6.00 = €900 a day)**

There are two errors here.

- Revenue is not profit. Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 a loaf, and this step ignores them.
- The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which the bakery passes on and does not keep. Net of VAT, a loaf brings in €6.00 ÷ 1.10 = €5.45.
- Contribution per loaf is therefore about €5.45 − €1.70 = €3.75.
- On the 108 extra loaves that is about €405 a day, not €900.

**Step 3: energy (a saving of about €7 a day)**

The per-loaf comparison is correct. €5.20 ÷ 72 is 7.2 cents, against €4.00 ÷ 48 at 8.3 cents. The conclusion drawn from it is wrong, because you pay for loads, not for loaves.

- You still run 9 loads a day.
- Each load costs €5.20 instead of €4.00, so energy rises by 9 × €1.20 = €10.80 a day.
- That is a cost, not a saving.
- It is also worse than it looks, because the proofer limit means each load holds 60 loaves, not 72.

**Step 4: financing (interest of €16,200, total €70,200)**

6% × €54,000 × 5 is the formula for simple interest on the full amount for the whole term. A standard monthly repayment loan reduces the balance each month, so you pay interest only on what is still owed.

- At 6% a year over 60 months, the repayment is about €1,045 a month.
- That comes to about €62,700 in total, with interest of roughly €8,700.
- So Petra overstated the interest by about €7,500.

Whether financing belongs in the payback calculation at all is a separate question. Payback is usually measured on the purchase price against the cash the machine generates, and I would ask the board which basis they use. The conclusion is the same either way, as the next step shows.

**Step 5: payback (77 trading days, about 13 weeks)**

Rebuilding the daily figure:

| Item | Per day |
|---|---|
| Extra contribution (108 × about €3.75) | +€405 |
| Extra energy | −€10.80 |
| Net gain | about €395 |

- On the €54,000 price, payback is 54,000 ÷ 395, about 137 trading days. At six days a week that is roughly 23 weeks, or 5.3 months.
- Counting the loan's total repayments (about €62,700) instead, it is about 159 days, or 6.1 months.
- Four months is about 104 trading days (17.3 weeks × 6). A payback of 137 days fails the test on the cheapest basis.

**What would change the answer**

- **Upgrade the proofer.** If the cabinet were expanded to 72 and the 150 loaves a day of unmet demand were all sold, the net gain would be about 150 × €3.75 − €10.80 ≈ €552 a day. Payback on €54,000 would then be about 98 days, which passes with only a small margin. The proofer upgrade would have to cost under roughly €3,400 for the total to stay inside four months, so it is unlikely to clear the bar once you price it.
- **Selling the old oven.** Any resale value lowers the net outlay and helps.
- **Installation costs.** Any one-off installation, ventilation or electrical work adds to the outlay and hurts.

**Assumptions to check before Thursday**

- All extra loaves sell at full shelf price. The 150 loaves a day of turned-away demand is Petra's figure, so ask how it was measured.
- No extra labour is needed for the added output. I have included none because you gave no labour figures, so any extra labour would lengthen payback further.
- The proofer really is the binding limit on loads. If the cabinet could be reloaded within the 40-minute bake, that would change Step 1, so confirm how it works in practice.

**For the board**

As presented, the case is overstated by a factor of about two. It claims €907 a day, and the corrected figure is about €395. On the existing proofer the D9 does not meet the four-month rule. Petra's underlying idea, that more oven capacity would turn away less demand, is sound, but the proofer has to be part of the proposal. Present the oven and the proofer as one package, with a real quote for the proofer, and re-run the payback on that.
