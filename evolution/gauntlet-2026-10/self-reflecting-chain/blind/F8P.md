**Short answer: no, it doesn't hold up.** Petra's 13 weeks falls apart on four points. Corrected, the payback is about 23 weeks (roughly 5.3 months), which misses the board's four-month test. I'd present it that way on Thursday, along with one route that could pass.

## Step by step

**1. Capacity (72 loaves x 9 = 648/day, up 216).** The oven arithmetic is right, but the proofer isn't considered. It holds 60 loaves and takes 40 minutes, the same cycle as the bake. So it can only feed 60 loaves into each load, however many the oven could take. Realistic output is 60 x 9 = 540 a day, up 108 on today's 432. That is below the roughly 150 loaves of unmet demand, so the proofer, not the oven, caps the gain. (I'm assuming the proofer isn't being replaced or extended as part of this purchase.)

**2. "150 loaves x €6.00 = €900 a day profit."** This is revenue, not profit, and two things are missing:
- The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which isn't ours. Net revenue is €6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45 a loaf.
- Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 a loaf.

That leaves a margin of about €3.75 a loaf. On 150 loaves it would be about €563 a day, not €900. On the 108 loaves the proofer actually allows, it is about €405 a day.

**3. Energy "saving" of about €7 a day.** The per-loaf figures are right (8.3 cents now, 7.2 cents on the D9), but the conclusion is backwards. We aren't baking the same loaves more cheaply. We are running the same 9 loads at €5.20 instead of €4.00. Energy spend goes up from €36 to €46.80 a day, a cost of about €10.80 a day, where Petra shows a €7 gain. It is a small number, but it has the wrong sign.

**4. Financing: 6% x €54,000 x 5 = €16,200 interest.** That formula charges interest on the full €54,000 for all five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance falls each month. The payment is about €1,044 a month, total repayments about €62,600, and interest about €8,600, not €16,200. Petra's total of €70,200 is overstated by about €7,600.

**5. Payback: €70,200 / €907 = 77 days.** The division is correct, but both inputs are wrong. The numerator also mixes in financing cost, which payback tests normally leave out. They measure how fast the purchase price comes back, and interest is a separate cost of funding. I don't know whether your board's rule includes finance charges, so I've shown both.

| Corrected, proofer-limited (+108 loaves) | €395 | €54,000 | about 137 | about 23 | about 5.3 |
| Same, including interest (€62,600) | €395 | about €62,600 | about 159 | about 26 | about 6.1 |

The daily gain of about €395 is 108 loaves x €3.75 margin = €405, less €10.80 of extra energy. Four months is about 104 trading days.

## What could still make it work

If the proofer were expanded so the oven could run full and capture all 150 turned-away loaves, the daily gain rises to about €552 (150 x €3.75 = €563, less €10.80 of energy). The €54,000 then pays back in about 98 trading days, roughly 3.8 months. That just clears the board's test, but only if:
- the proofer upgrade costs little. The headroom is about €3,400 before payback passes four months.
- the board measures payback on the purchase price, not on price plus interest. With interest included, even this case runs to roughly 4.4 months.
- the 150 loaves a day of turned-away demand is real and recurs on all six trading days. That figure would be worth checking, because the whole case depends on it.
