Your doubt is justified, but not for the reason you might expect. Petra's case has five problems, and they mostly overstate the gain. Corrected, the D9 pays back in about 23 weeks (roughly 5.3 months), not 13. That misses the board's four-month rule. Here is each step.

**Step 1: the extra capacity is 108 loaves a day, not 216.**
The oven takes 72 loaves per load, but the proofing cabinet holds 60. You said dough goes straight from proofer to oven, so each load is capped at 60. That gives 60 x 9 = 540 loaves a day, against 432 today (48 x 9). The gain is 108 a day. It does not cover the roughly 150 you turn away, and it isn't 216. The 72-loaf figure only applies if the proofer is also upgraded, and that isn't in the €54,000.

**Step 2: she counts revenue as profit, and counts the wrong volume.**
- She uses 150 extra loaves, but the setup can only make 108.
- She values each loaf at €6.00, which is the shelf price. That price includes 10% VAT, so you keep €6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- She leaves out ingredients and packaging of €1.70 a loaf. The margin is about €3.75 a loaf before energy.

Extra margin is therefore 108 x €3.75 = about €405 a day, not €900.

**Step 3: energy goes up, not down.**
The D9 uses 30% more per load, so €5.20 against €4.00. Her per-loaf figure of 7.2 cents is right only at 72 loaves per load (€5.20 / 72). At the 60 loaves the proofer allows, it is 8.7 cents a loaf, which is worse than today's 8.3. The bill itself rises by 9 loads x €1.20 = €10.80 a day. She shows a saving of about €7 a day, but the real figure is a cost of about €11 a day.

Net extra profit is about €405 - €11 = **about €395 a day**.

**Step 4: the interest calculation is wrong, and it doesn't belong in a payback figure.**
6% x €54,000 x 5 is flat-rate interest. On a standard monthly repayment loan, interest is charged on the falling balance. Repayments would be about €1,044 a month, or about €62,600 in total, so interest is about €8,600 and not €16,200. That error works against the case.

The bigger issue is that payback is normally measured on the purchase price (€54,000), not on price plus interest. Ask the board which they mean. I'd present the figure on €54,000 and mention financing separately.

**Step 5: payback.**
- On €54,000: 54,000 / 395 = about 137 trading days. At six days a week that is about 23 weeks, or 5.3 months.
- On the financed total of about €62,600: about 159 days, or 26 weeks.

Four months is about 104 trading days. Either way, the D9 as specified misses by a margin that isn't close.

**What I'd say on Thursday**
1. Petra is right that the oven would make money. At about €395 a day, a loan repayment of roughly €1,044 a month is covered several times over (about €10,000 of extra margin a month). The case for the investment is real. It just doesn't meet the four-month rule as written.
2. The proofer is the bottleneck. If it can be brought up to 72 loaves, you could sell the full 150 extra loaves: about 150 x €3.75 - €11 = **about €550 a day**. Payback on €54,000 is then about 98 days, or 16 weeks, which is inside four months. That leaves only about €3,000 of headroom for the proofer upgrade, so you need a quote before relying on this.

**Things not in the numbers that the board may ask about**
- Extra labour for shaping, loading and packing 108 or more loaves a day. If that is more than a few euros a day, payback lengthens.
- Whether the old oven has a resale or scrap value, and whether installation, ventilation or electrical work is extra to the €54,000.
- Whether the 150 turned-away loaves are a steady daily figure or an average that includes peak days. If demand is lower on a typical day, everything above gets worse.
- Whether all 108 or 150 extra loaves would actually sell, given the three shops' own sales patterns.

Short version: don't present the 13 weeks. Present about 23 weeks for the oven alone, and about 16 weeks if the proofer is upgraded and its cost fits within the roughly €3,000 of headroom. Then let the board decide how strictly to apply the four-month rule.
