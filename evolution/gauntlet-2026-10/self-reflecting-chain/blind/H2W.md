I went through Petra's case line by line. The idea is sound: you do turn away demand, and a bigger oven could pay. But the numbers as written won't survive the board on Thursday. Corrected, the payback is roughly 5 to 6 months, not 3, which misses the four-month rule. Better to find that out now than in the meeting.

**Step 1: capacity (the proofer caps it at 60 loaves a load)**
The D9 takes 72 loaves, but your proofing cabinet holds 60, and dough goes straight from proofer to oven. So each load is 60 loaves, not 72.
- 9 loads x 60 = 540 loaves a day, up 108 (not 216).
- That is below the roughly 150 loaves of unmet demand, so the oven can't cover it all. The extra sales are 108 a day, not 150.

**Step 2: profit per loaf (revenue is not profit)**
The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which goes to the tax office. Net revenue is €6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45. Less €1.70 ingredients and packaging, the margin is about €3.75 a loaf, not €6.00.
- 108 extra loaves x €3.75 = about €405 a day. Petra's figure was €900.

**Step 3: energy (it goes up, it doesn't save anything)**
The D9 uses 30% more energy per load: €5.20 a load against €4.00. Over 9 loads that is €46.80 a day against €36.00, so you spend about €10.80 more a day.
- Petra's per-loaf figure (7.2 cents against 8.3 cents) only holds with 72 loaves in every load. At 60 loaves the D9 costs 8.7 cents a loaf, slightly worse than now.
- Even at 72 a load, a lower cost per loaf doesn't save money when the total bill rises. The "€7 a day saving" should be a cost of about €11 a day.

Net extra profit is about €405 - €11 = **€395 a day**, against Petra's €907.

**Step 4: financing (interest is overstated)**
Interest of 6% x €54,000 x 5 years assumes you owe the full €54,000 for all five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance shrinks each month. At 6% over 60 months the repayment is about €1,044 a month, so the total is about €62,600 and the interest about €8,600, not €16,200. Her error runs in your favour here, but it should still be fixed.

**Step 5: payback (the test fails either way)**
Four months is roughly 104 trading days at six days a week.
- Counting the loan interest, as Petra did: €62,600 / €395 = about 159 days, or 26 weeks (about 6 months).
- On the purchase price alone: €54,000 / €395 = about 137 days, or 23 weeks (about 5.3 months).

Either way it is outside four months. The arithmetic in her last step is fine (€70,200 / €907 = 77 days). The problem is the inputs.

**What I'd do before Thursday**
1. Don't present the 3-month figure. Present the corrected one, and say plainly that it is a good return that misses the stated hurdle.
2. Find out what it would cost to expand the proofer to 72 loaves. Then the oven makes 648 a day, which is 216 more, but you only need about 150. Demand caps sales at 150, so call it 144 extra loaves usable if the proofer is 72. That gives about €530 a day net, and payback on the oven alone is about 102 days, just inside the four months. With interest it is about 118 days, outside. So whether this clears the bar depends on the proofer cost and on whether the board counts interest.
3. Ask the board how it defines payback (purchase price or total financing cost). It changes the answer.
4. Check these assumptions, because I couldn't from what you gave me:
   - Whether the 150 turned-away loaves are a steady daily figure or a peak-day figure.
   - Whether the three shops can actually sell 108 more loaves a day.
   - Extra labour, which isn't in Petra's case at all.
   - Any trade-in or sale value of the old oven, plus installation and delivery costs.

Petra's instinct is good and the demand is real. If the proofer upgrade is cheap, a corrected case may well clear the hurdle. You'll go in with much more credibility presenting the corrected numbers than defending the first set.
