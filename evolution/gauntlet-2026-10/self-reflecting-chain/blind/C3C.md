**Short answer: no, it doesn't hold up. Corrected, the D9 pays back in roughly 5 months (about 23 weeks), not 13 weeks, so it misses the four-month rule. Petra's conclusion fails on three separate errors, and one of them matters most.**

**Assumptions:** the 150 loaves/day of unmet demand is real and stays at €6.00 a loaf. We trade 6 days a week (about 26 days a month, so four months is about 104 trading days). Labour, maintenance and the old oven's resale value are ignored because the brief gives no figures. Payback is measured against the €54,000 price.

**Step by step**

1. **Capacity (the biggest error).** 72 loaves per load is the oven's rating, but the proofing cabinet holds only 60 loaves. Since dough goes straight from proofer to oven, each load is capped at 60 unless the proofer is upgraded too.
   - Realistic output is 60 × 9 = 540 loaves a day, up 108 from today's 432, not up 216.
   - That is below the 150 loaves of unmet demand, so the extra volume is 108 a day.

2. **Profit per loaf.** Petra treats the €6.00 price as profit. Two things are missing:
   - The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, so the net price is 6.00 ÷ 1.10 = €5.45.
   - Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 a loaf.
   - Margin before energy is about €3.75 a loaf, not €6.00. Her figure of 150 × €6 = €900 a day is overstated by more than half.

3. **Energy.** Her per-loaf comparison is true only if the oven runs full at 72, and it will not.
   - The D9 costs €4.00 × 1.3 = €5.20 a load. At 60 loaves that is 8.7 cents a loaf, which is worse than the current 8.3 cents, not better.
   - Energy is also a cost that rises in total: 9 loads go from €36.00 to €46.80 a day, which is €10.80 more.
   - So energy is a cost of about €11 a day, not a saving of €7 a day. Her sign is wrong, a swing of about €18 a day.

4. **Corrected daily gain.** 108 × €3.75 ≈ €405, minus €10.80 energy, comes to about €395 a day. That is well below her €907.

5. **Payback.** €54,000 ÷ €395 ≈ 137 trading days, which is about 23 weeks or 5.3 months. The board's limit of four months needs about €519 a day.
   - Her financing method is also wrong. 6% × €54,000 × 5 charges interest on the full balance for all five years, but a monthly repayment loan amortises. The payment is about €1,044 a month, total interest is about €8,600 (not €16,200), and the total repaid is about €62,600.
   - Using that financed total instead, payback is about 159 days, or 6 months. It fails the test either way.
   - Financing cost normally sits outside a payback calculation, so the cleanest figure to show the board is the €54,000 one.

**Check on the arithmetic:** her own sums are internally consistent (€900 + €7 = €907, and €70,200 ÷ €907 ≈ 77 days). The problem is the inputs, not the division.

**What to say on Thursday**

- The case as written should not go forward. It rests on a capacity the proofer can't feed, revenue treated as profit, and an energy "saving" that is really extra cost.
- The case is not hopeless. If the proofer were also upgraded to 72, you would sell the full 150 extra loaves: about 150 × €3.75 − €10.80 ≈ €552 a day. That clears the €519 hurdle against the oven price alone, with very little margin and before the cost of the proofer upgrade, which pushes payback past four months again. A quote for the upgrade would tell you where it lands.
- It's worth asking Petra whether the 150 loaves a day is a measured figure, since the result is very sensitive to it. Other options are a smaller upgrade, or using the current oven's spare hours if any exist.
- If you want to keep one number for the board, use roughly 5 months on the oven alone, with the proofer limit as the main reason.
