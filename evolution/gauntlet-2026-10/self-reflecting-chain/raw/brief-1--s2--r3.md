Short answer: no, the case doesn't hold up as written. Step 5's arithmetic is fine, but steps 1 to 4 each have an error, and most of them make the payback look better than it is. Corrected, the D9 pays back in roughly 5.3 months on the purchase price alone, which misses the four-month rule. It's worth fixing before Thursday, because the board will probably spot some of this.

**Step 1: capacity (the proofer caps you at 60, not 72)**
- The D9 takes 72 loaves per load, but the proofing cabinet holds 60 and proofs in the same 40 minutes. Dough goes straight from proofer to oven, so you can only bake 60 per load.
- That gives 9 x 60 = 540 loaves a day. Today you bake 9 x 48 = 432, so the gain is 108 loaves a day, not 216.
- 108 is below the roughly 150 loaves of unmet demand, so every extra loaf should sell.

**Step 2: €6.00 is revenue, not profit**
- €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which belongs to the tax authority. Net price is 6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- Ingredients and packaging are €1.70, so the margin is about €3.75 a loaf.
- Extra margin is 108 x €3.75 = about €405 a day. Petra's €900 is about 2.2 times too high. It counts VAT and ingredients as profit, and it uses 150 loaves when only 108 can be made.

**Step 3: energy goes up, it doesn't save money**
- Today: €4.00 per load, 9 loads = €36 a day.
- D9: €4.00 x 1.3 = €5.20 per load, 9 loads = €46.80 a day. That is €10.80 a day more.
- Petra's 7.2 cents per loaf only works at 72 loaves per load (5.20 / 72). At the real 60 loaves it is 8.7 cents, worse than today's 8.3.
- Also, "saving 1.1 cents on each of 648 loaves" compares per-loaf costs but ignores that total energy spend rises. The €7 a day saving is really a €10.80 a day cost.

**Step 4: the financing calculation is wrong, and it mixes two questions**
- 6% x €54,000 x 5 charges interest on the full balance for the whole term. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance falls each month.
- Monthly payment at 6% over 60 months is about €1,044, so the total repaid is about €62,600 and interest is about €8,600, not €16,200.
- Payback is normally measured against the purchase price. Financing is a separate decision. I show both below.

**Step 5: the sum**
- Net daily gain = €405 - €10.80 = about €395.
- Payback on €54,000 = 54,000 / 395 = about 137 trading days, or about 23 weeks, or about 5.3 months.
- If you include the loan interest (€62,600): about 159 days, about 26 weeks, or about 6.1 months.
- Four months is roughly 104 trading days at six days a week, so both versions fail.
- Petra's 77 days came from stacking errors: revenue treated as profit, 150 instead of 108 loaves, an energy saving that is really a cost, and overstated interest. The interest error partly offsets the others, but not enough.

**What could make it pass**
- If the proofer were upgraded to 72 loaves, you could bake 648 a day. Extra sales would be capped by demand at 150 loaves: 150 x €3.75 = about €563, less €10.80 energy = about €552 a day. That gives payback on €54,000 of about 98 days, or about 3.8 months, which just passes.
- The headroom is thin. At about 104 days the total spend could be at most about €57,400, so the proofer upgrade would need to cost under roughly €3,400. I don't know that cost, so please get a quote.

**Other things to check before Thursday**
- Is the 150 loaves of turned-away demand a reliable daily average across all six days, or a peak figure? Everything scales with it.
- Does the extra output need more labor, or more delivery capacity from the central bakehouse to the three shops? I assumed no extra cost.
- Can the old oven be sold or traded in? That would shorten payback.
- Installation, commissioning, and any downtime during the swap aren't in the €54,000 as far as I can tell.
- I assumed six trading days a week, 52 weeks a year, with no seasonality.

**Suggested line for the board:** "As scoped, with the current proofer, the D9 pays back in about 5 months. It meets the four-month rule only if we also lift proofing capacity to 72 loaves for under about €3,400. We're getting a quote." Or, if the proofer upgrade is too costly, ask the board to treat it as a budgeted item rather than one that has to clear the four-month test.
