**Short answer:** No, not as written. Petra's 13 weeks rests on four errors. The biggest is that the proofer can't feed a 72-loaf oven. On a corrected basis the D9 pays back in about 5 to 5.5 months, which misses the board's four-month test. It would pass if the proofer limit is fixed and the full 150 loaves of demand is real.

Assumption: four months is about 17 weeks, or roughly 104 trading days at six days a week.

**Step 1: capacity (72 x 9 = 648 loaves, up 216)**

The arithmetic is right, but the proofer isn't considered. It holds 60 loaves and proofs for the same 40 minutes as the bake, so dough goes straight from proofer to oven. That means each load can be at most 60 loaves, not 72.

- Realistic output is 60 x 9 = 540 a day, up 108 on today's 432.
- That is below the roughly 150 loaves of turned-away demand, so the oven isn't the only bottleneck.

**Step 2: "150 loaves x €6.00 = €900 profit"**

This treats shelf price as profit. Three things are wrong with it.
- The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which goes to the tax authority. Net revenue is €6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 a loaf. The margin before energy is therefore about €3.75 a loaf.
- Only 108 extra loaves can actually be made (Step 1), not 150.

Extra margin is about 108 x €3.75 = €405 a day, or about €563 a day if 150 loaves were possible. Petra's €900 is overstated by roughly 60 to 120%.

**Step 3: energy "saving" of about €7 a day**

The per-loaf figures are correct: €4.00 / 48 = 8.3c now, and €5.20 / 72 = 7.2c on the D9. But the saving only exists if the D9 runs full at 72 loaves a load. With the proofer limit it makes 60, so energy is €5.20 / 60 = 8.7c a loaf, which is worse than today.

The comparison is also the wrong one. What matters is total spend. Nine loads cost €46.80 a day instead of €36.00, so energy is an extra cost of about €10.80 a day, not a €7 saving. It is also already partly counted in the loaf margin if you apply per-loaf energy twice, so use the €10.80 once.

**Corrected daily gain**

| Case | Extra loaves | Margin | Extra energy | Net per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proofer unchanged (60 per load) | 108 | €405 | €10.80 | about €395 |
| Proofer upgraded, all 150 sell | 150 | €563 | €10.80 | about €552 |

**Step 4: financing "interest = 6% x €54,000 x 5 = €16,200"**

That is simple interest on the full balance for five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance falls each month.
- The payment is about €1,044 a month, so the total repaid is about €62,600.
- Interest is about €8,600, not €16,200.

Payback tests normally use the purchase price anyway, and financing cost is a separate question. Mixing the two overstates the cost in her case. Her figure of €70,200 is wrong either way.

**Step 5: payback**

Petra's 77 days is €70,200 / €907. Both numbers are wrong. Using the €54,000 price:

| Case | Days to pay back | Months | Four-month test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proofer unchanged | about 137 trading days (about 23 weeks) | about 5.3 | Fails |
| Proofer upgraded, 150 loaves sell | about 98 trading days (about 16 weeks) | about 3.8 | Passes, narrowly |

If you count the financed cost of about €62,600 as well, the best case moves to about 113 days (4.4 months) and also fails.

**What this means for Thursday**

- To clear four months on the €54,000 price, the oven needs to earn about €519 a day. That needs about 141 extra loaves a day, so the proofer would have to hold at least about 64 loaves a load. A 72-loaf proofer would match the oven.
- I'd present this as a two-part proposal: the D9 together with a proofer upgrade, with the upgrade cost added to the €54,000. I don't have that quote, so the payback should be re-run once you have it. Every extra €10,000 adds roughly 18 trading days.
- The 150 turned-away loaves a day is Petra's estimate. Check it against shop sell-out times or lost-sales records. If real demand is closer to 100, even the upgraded case fails.

**Not in her case, worth asking before Thursday**
- Installation, ventilation, gas or electrical upgrades, and any downtime during the swap.
- The resale or scrap value of the old oven, which would help.
- Extra labour for shaping and loading 100 or more more loaves a day. If it needs extra hours, it comes straight off the margin.
- Whether the 9 loads a day assumes the same 6-hour baking window, and whether more loaves need more delivery trips to the three shops.

**Bottom line:** the case as written shouldn't go to the board. A corrected version, with the D9 plus a proofer fix and a checked demand number, is much closer, and with the right inputs it can meet the four-month rule.
