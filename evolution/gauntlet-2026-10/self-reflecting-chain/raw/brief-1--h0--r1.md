Short answer: no, the case doesn't hold up. Corrected, the payback is about 137 trading days (roughly 5.3 months), which misses the board's four-month test. Her arithmetic in point 5 is fine (€70,200 ÷ €907 is 77 days), but points 1 to 4 each have a problem, and they all push the same way.

**Point 1: the proofer caps output at 60 loaves a load, not 72.**
Last spring's second rack means the proofing cabinet holds 60 loaves per 40-minute cycle, and dough goes straight from proofer to oven. A 72-loaf oven can't be fed 72 loaves every 40 minutes. So:
- Output is 9 × 60 = 540 loaves a day, not 648.
- Today's output is 9 × 48 = 432, so the gain is 108 loaves a day, not 216.
- That is below the roughly 150 loaves of demand being turned away, so output, not demand, is the limit.

**Point 2: €6.00 is revenue, not profit.**
- The shelf price includes 10% VAT, so the net price is €6.00 ÷ 1.10 = €5.45.
- Ingredients and packaging are €1.70, leaving about €3.75 a loaf.
- 108 extra loaves × €3.75 is about €405 a day, not €900.

**Point 3: the energy line is the wrong way round.**
The per-loaf figures are right only at a full 72-loaf load: €5.20 ÷ 72 is 7.2 cents, against €4.00 ÷ 48 is 8.3 cents. At 60 loaves a load, the D9 costs €5.20 ÷ 60 = 8.7 cents a loaf, which is worse than today. Also, "saving 1.1 cents on 648 loaves" is not how the money works. Energy is charged per load. Nine loads cost €46.80 on the D9 against €36.00 now, so energy is an extra cost of €10.80 a day, not a €7 saving.

**Point 4: the interest figure is wrong, and it doesn't belong in a payback figure anyway.**
- 6% × €54,000 × 5 is flat-rate interest on the whole sum for five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance falls each month, so interest is much lower. The payment is about €1,044 a month, a total of about €62,600 and interest of about €8,600, not €16,200.
- Payback is normally measured against the purchase price, because financing is a separate decision. Adding interest to the cost mixes the two. The board's rule is easiest to defend as €54,000 against the daily gain.

**Corrected payback**
- Daily gain: about €405 − €10.80 = about €395.
- €54,000 ÷ €395 = about 137 trading days, or about 23 weeks (5.3 months at six days a week).
- Four months is about 104 trading days. To meet it, the oven would need to earn about €519 a day.
- If you want to include financing, as Petra tried to, the number is worse: about €62,600 ÷ €395 is about 159 days (roughly 6 months).
- The loan itself would be easy to carry. The monthly repayment of about €1,044 is far below the monthly gain of about €10,000. That is a reason the purchase may be worth making, but it is not the payback the board's rule asks for.

**Is there a version that passes?**
Possibly. The binding constraint is the proofer, not the oven. If the proofing cabinet were extended to 72 loaves, the D9 could bake 648 a day, and the 150 loaves of unmet demand would be the limit:
- 150 × €3.75 = about €563, less €10.80 energy, is about €552 a day.
- €54,000 ÷ €552 is about 98 days, inside the 104-day limit.
- That leaves only about €3,400 of headroom across the whole four months. The proofer extension, any extra labour for 150 more loaves a day, and the sale or scrapping of the old oven would all have to fit inside that. If the extension costs more than a few thousand euros, it fails again.

**What I'd say on Thursday**
1. As written, the case overstates the daily gain by more than double (€907 against about €395), because of the proofer limit, VAT, and the treatment of energy.
2. On the board's rule, the D9 as the bakery is set up today pays back in about 5.3 months and doesn't qualify.
3. If Petra can price a proofer upgrade to 72 loaves, and it is cheap, the case could be brought back to the board as a combined proposal with honest numbers.

**Assumptions and things I couldn't check**
- I used 26 trading days a month (six days a week, 52 weeks a year).
- Extra labour is not in the numbers, because you didn't give it. It would lengthen payback.
- I assumed the 150 turned-away loaves a day are real, steady demand across the shops, and that the bakehouse can distribute them.
- Any resale value of the old oven would shorten payback a little, but not enough to close the gap in the current set-up.
