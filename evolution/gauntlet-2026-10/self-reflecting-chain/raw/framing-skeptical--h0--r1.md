Your doubt is justified. Her five points have four separate problems, and they all push the same way. Corrected, the payback is roughly 5 to 6 months, not 13 weeks, so it misses the board's four-month test. The oven may still be worth buying, but not on this case as written.

I worked through the numbers in her order. I assumed 6 trading days a week and 52 weeks a year, so four months is about 104 trading days. I also assumed the €1.70 per loaf excludes VAT.

**1. The extra volume is 108 loaves a day, not 216.**
The D9 bakes 72 loaves a load, but the proofing cabinet holds 60 and dough goes straight from proofer to oven. Each load is therefore capped at 60 loaves.
- 60 × 9 loads = 540 loaves a day, against 432 now (48 × 9). That is +108.
- Her 648 a day only works if the proofer also holds 72 or more.
- The 150 loaves of unmet demand is only partly covered. I'm taking that figure as real and sellable. It's worth asking where it comes from, for example turn-aways counted at the shops versus a guess.

**2. €900 a day is revenue, not profit.**
She multiplied loaves by the shelf price, which has two errors.
- The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, and you pass that on to the tax authority. Net revenue is €6.00 ÷ 1.10 = €5.45.
- She never subtracted the €1.70 of ingredients and packaging. Contribution is €5.45 − €1.70 = €3.75 a loaf.
- 108 extra loaves × €3.75 = about €405 a day. That is less than half her €900.

**3. The energy point is wrong in direction, and the "saving" is not a saving.**
Her per-loaf figures are right on paper: €4.00 ÷ 48 = 8.3 cents now, and €5.20 ÷ 72 = 7.2 cents on the D9. But that assumes a full 72-loaf load, which the proofer prevents.
- At 60 loaves a load the D9 costs €5.20 ÷ 60 = 8.7 cents a loaf. That is worse than today, not better.
- Cost per loaf isn't the right measure anyway. What matters is the extra money out the door: €1.20 more per load × 9 loads = about €10.80 a day of additional energy.
- She added about €7 a day of savings. The correct entry is a cost of about €11 a day.

Net extra profit is about €405 − €11 = **€395 a day**, against her €907.

**4. The interest calculation is wrong.**
She used 6% × €54,000 × 5, which treats the loan as if you owed the full €54,000 for all five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan the balance falls each month.
- The payment is about €1,044 a month (6% a year, 60 months).
- Total repaid is about €62,600, so interest is roughly €8,600, not €16,200.
- Her mistake here runs in the oven's favour. It overstates the cost, so it's fair to concede it.
- Whether interest belongs in a payback calculation at all depends on how your board defines the test. Many boards measure payback on the purchase price. I've shown both below.

**5. Corrected payback**

| Basis | Cost | Days at €395 | Approx. months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price only | €54,000 | about 137 | about 5.3 |
| Including financing | about €62,600 | about 159 | about 6.1 |

Her arithmetic in the last line is right (€70,200 ÷ €907 ≈ 77 days). The inputs are what's wrong. On either basis, the case misses the four-month threshold. To pass it on the purchase price, you would need about €520 a day of extra profit.

**What I'd say to the board on Thursday**

- The proofer is the real bottleneck. Last spring's second rack lifted the proofer to 60, and that is now the ceiling on a 72-loaf oven. Ask what it would cost to take the proofer to 72 or more.
  - If the oven could then sell the full 150 extra loaves a day, the extra profit would be roughly €550 a day. That puts payback on the oven near 100 days, inside four months, before the cost of the proofer upgrade.
  - That is a rough estimate. It depends on the demand being real, and the proofer cost would have to be added to the €54,000. So treat it as a question to put to Petra, not as a result.
- Other things the board may ask about, which none of us have numbers for yet:
  - Extra labour for the added volume.
  - Whether the three shops can actually sell 108 more loaves a day at full price.
  - The old oven's resale value.
  - Downtime during installation.

The honest summary is that Petra's case overstates the benefit by more than double, but its main idea, that bake capacity limits sales, may be sound. Your instinct was right that it falls apart on the first question. The first question to expect is "can the proofer feed a 72-loaf oven?"
