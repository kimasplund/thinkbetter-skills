**Short answer:** No, the case doesn't hold up as written. Four of her five steps have a problem. With the numbers corrected, payback is about 137 trading days (roughly 5.3 months) on the €54,000 price. It is about 159 trading days (roughly 6 months) if you count the loan interest. Either way it misses the four-month rule. I'd be careful about presenting it on Thursday as is.

I took four months as about 104 trading days (17.3 weeks x 6 days). Adjust that if the board counts differently.

**Step 1: capacity, 648 loaves a day (+216).** This is the biggest error. The proofing cabinet you extended last spring holds 60 loaves per 40-minute cycle, and dough now goes straight from proofer to oven. The D9 can take 72, but the proofer can only feed it 60. That gives 60 x 9 = 540 loaves a day, which is +108, not +216. It doesn't cover the ~150 loaves of turned-away demand. It covers about 70% of it. The 72-loaf figure only becomes real if the proofer is also expanded.

**Step 2: "150 extra loaves at €6.00 is €900 a day of profit."** There are three problems.
- Only 108 extra loaves can be made (Step 1), not 150.
- €6.00 is the shelf price including 10% VAT. The co-op keeps 6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- €6.00 is revenue, not profit. Ingredients and packaging are €1.70 a loaf, so the contribution is about €3.75 a loaf.

108 x €3.75 is about €405 a day, less than half of her €900.

**Step 3: energy "saving" of about €7 a day.** The per-loaf figures are arithmetically right at 72 loaves per load (€5.20 / 72 = 7.2 cents, against €4.00 / 48 = 8.3 cents). But the loads will only hold 60 loaves, so the D9 costs €5.20 / 60 = 8.7 cents per loaf. That is worse than today, not better. More importantly, a lower cost per loaf isn't a saving when you bake more loaves. Total energy goes up. It is 9 loads x €5.20 = €46.80 a day, against €36.00 now. That is a cost of about €10.80 a day, where she has a gain of €7.

**Step 4: financing cost of €16,200.** This formula is simple interest on the full €54,000 for five years. On a standard monthly repayment loan, the balance falls every month, so interest accrues on a shrinking amount. At 6% over 60 months (assuming 6% is the nominal annual rate), the payment is about €1,044 a month. Total interest is about €8,600, and the total repaid is about €62,600. Her €70,200 overstates the cost by about €7,600. That error favours the oven being rejected, which is the opposite direction from her other errors. The bigger question is whether the board measures payback against the purchase price or the financed cost. Ask them. I'd show both.

**Step 5: payback of 77 days.** Her arithmetic is right (70,200 / 907 = 77), but both numbers feeding it are wrong. Corrected daily gain:

| | Per day |
|---|---|
| 108 extra loaves x (€5.45 - €1.70) | +€405 |
| Extra energy (9 x €1.20) | -€10.80 |
| **Net gain** | **about €395** |

- On the €54,000 price: 54,000 / 395 = about 137 trading days, roughly 22.8 weeks or 5.3 months.
- On the financed cost (about €62,600): about 159 trading days, roughly 26 weeks or 6.1 months.

To clear four months (104 days) on the purchase price, the oven would have to earn about €519 a day.

**Is there a path to yes?** Possibly. If the proofer were expanded to 72 loaves, the oven could produce all 216 extra loaves. Sales would then be limited by demand, about 150 loaves a day. That gives 150 x €3.75 = €563, less €10.80 for energy, so about €552 a day. Payback on €54,000 would be about 98 days, inside the limit. The proofer upgrade would have to cost less than roughly €3,400 for the combined purchase to still pay back in four months. I don't know what that costs, so get a quote before Thursday. Even then the case depends on the 150-a-day demand figure holding.

**What I couldn't check, and would confirm before presenting:**
- Whether "roughly 150" turned-away loaves is a measured number or an estimate. Everything above is sensitive to it.
- Whether shaping, labour and delivery to the three shops can handle 60 to 150 more loaves a day.
- Whether the old oven has resale value. That would shorten payback slightly.
- Whether the board's four-month test uses the purchase price or the financed cost.

**Suggested line for Thursday:** the D9 as specified makes about €395 a day, paying back in roughly five and a half months. It would meet the rule only with a proofer expansion that lifts it to about €550 a day. The proposal should be reframed as oven plus proofer, with a quote for the proofer.
