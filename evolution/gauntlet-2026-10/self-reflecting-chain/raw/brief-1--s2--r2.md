Short answer: no, the case doesn't hold up as written. Corrected, the payback is roughly 5 to 6 months, not 13 weeks, so it misses the board's four-month test. There are four errors and one omission. I've gone through them in order.

**Step 1: the proofer caps the D9 at 60 loaves a load, not 72**

Since the proofer upgrade, dough goes straight from proofer to oven. The proofer holds 60 loaves and takes the same 40 minutes as the bake, so it can release at most 60 loaves every 40 minutes. A 72-loaf oven can't be filled from it.

- Realistic output is 60 × 9 = 540 loaves a day, not 648.
- That is 108 more than today's 432, not 216.
- It does not cover the roughly 150 loaves a day you turn away. It covers 108 of them.

Petra's "216 extra" is the oven's capacity. The extra loaves you can actually make are 108.

**Step 2: she counts revenue as profit, and VAT as income**

- The €6.00 shelf price includes 10% VAT. Net revenue is €6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- Ingredients and packaging are €1.70, so the margin is about €3.75 a loaf, before energy.
- Her figure of 150 × €6.00 = €900 treats gross revenue as profit.
- Corrected: 108 × €3.75 = about €405 a day.

**Step 3: the energy "saving" is really an extra cost**

Per loaf, 7.2 cents versus 8.3 cents is correct only if every load is a full 72. Even so, a lower cost per loaf is not a saving when output rises, because total energy spend goes up.

- Today: 9 loads × €4.00 = €36.00 a day.
- D9: 9 loads × €5.20 (30% more) = €46.80 a day.
- That is €10.80 a day more, not €7 saved.
- At the true 60 loaves a load, the D9 costs 8.7 cents a loaf, which is worse than the current 8.3 cents.

The €907 a day becomes about 108 × €3.75 − €10.80 = about €395 a day.

**Step 4: the interest calculation is wrong, and the test is on the wrong base**

- 6% × €54,000 × 5 is simple interest on the full balance for five years. A standard monthly repayment loan reduces the balance as you pay, so that overstates the interest.
- At 6% over 60 months the payment is about €1,044 a month. Total repaid is about €62,600, so interest is about €8,600, not €16,200.
- Payback is more usually tested against the purchase price or cash outlay. I'd show the board both bases and say which one the policy means. I've assumed it is the purchase price, but I don't know your board's definition.

**Step 5: corrected payback**

Four months is about 17 weeks, or about 104 trading days at six days a week.

| Basis | Cost | Days at about €395 a day | Weeks | Months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price only | €54,000 | about 137 | about 23 | about 5.3 |
| Including real interest | about €62,600 | about 159 | about 26 | about 6.1 |
| Petra's figures | €70,200 | 77 | 13 | 3 |

On either basis it fails the four-month rule. Petra's 13 weeks comes from overstating the daily gain by more than double, and then overstating the cost.

**What could rescue it**

The binding constraint is the proofer, not the oven. If the proofer were upgraded so the D9 could run full, the picture changes:

- Demand is the limit at 150 extra loaves a day, so about 582 loaves, which is 9 loads.
- Daily gain would be about 150 × €3.75 − €10.80 = about €552.
- On the purchase price alone, €54,000 / €552 is about 98 trading days, inside the four-month window.
- That leaves only about €3,400 of headroom (104 days × €552 less €54,000) for the proofer upgrade, installation and everything else. A proofer upgrade is unlikely to cost that little. Please get a quote.

**Things the case leaves out**

I haven't priced these, and each one lengthens the payback:

- Installation, electrical or gas work, and downtime.
- Resale value of the old oven. This helps the case.
- Extra labour, or overtime, for 25% more output.
- Whether the three shops can actually sell the extra loaves. The 150 turned-away loaves are your own estimate, so check it against the sales records.
- Whether you can still supply all three shops across the same baking hours.

**For Thursday**

I'd say the Tarrow D9 doesn't meet the four-month rule on the current proofer. It pays back in about 5 to 6 months. The case only works if the proofer is upgraded cheaply, within about €3,400. I'd suggest asking Petra to rework the numbers with these corrections, and getting a proofer quote before presenting.

Assumptions: six trading days a week all year, the 150 turned-away loaves occur every trading day, and the existing 432 loaves keep selling as now.
