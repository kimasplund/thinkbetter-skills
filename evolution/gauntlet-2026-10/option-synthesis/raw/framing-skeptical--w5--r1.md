# Options: Wintering 600 colonies to deliver 420 graded colonies to the Senna Vale co-op

**Decide by:** Oct 20 (the haul is late October) · **Objective:** at least 420 colonies with 8+ frames on Mar 1 · **Horizon:** Mar 1, with trucks on Feb 20

**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- The 104 of 150 that graded last spring were wintered outdoors. If they were not, your own outdoor record is worse than I show.
- The 600 colonies are all candidates for the contract, and the loss and grading rates quoted to you apply to an average colony of yours.
- The co-op pays $190 only up to 420. A graded colony beyond 420 earns nothing under this contract. If the contract pays for extras, hauling more colonies south looks better than I show.
- The cellar's "about 500" includes Agnete's 300, so about 200 spaces are free for you.
- The $15,000 must cover everything paid up front, including $3 wraps on the colonies that stay outdoors. I assume no return-haul bill from Dunmere and that trucks can collect from wherever the colonies are. Both need checking.
- Outdoor grading is about 60% of 600. That sits between your 63% survival (37% average loss) and your 69% from last spring. The range I use is 50% to 69%.

## The recommendation

**Do not haul everything south, because you can't pay for it. Spread the risk across three sources: about 200 colonies into Agnete's cellar, about 100 to Dunmere Flats, the other 300 in wraps outdoors, and take Rafe Mulvaney's 80 colonies as a standby, in writing, before you commit the cash.** This week, make four calls: the cellar owner, Rafe, the co-op and the Dunmere yard. Run mite washes on a sample from every yard, too.

- **The full haul is impossible.** It costs 600 × $63 = $37,800 up front against $15,000 of credit. The most you can haul is about 220 colonies, and that leaves $0 in reserve.
- **Your current plan misses 420 on arithmetic alone.** Your last three winters left 396, 360 and 378 live colonies before anyone counted frames. Not one of those winters would have reached 420. Expect a shortfall of 40 to 120 colonies, and each short colony costs you $230 ($190 you don't earn plus the $40 charge).
- **You have Rafe's offer backwards.** Compare a $150 colony not with the $190 you'd collect but with the alternative, a short colony that costs you $40 and earns nothing. A purchased colony nets you +$40 against -$40, so each one is worth $80 to you. It also costs nothing up front. That is the cheapest cash-light cover you have. Its weakness is that the offer is verbal, so I treat it as insurance and do not rely on it alone.
- **Your brother is right about one thing and wrong about another.** Copying the neighbours is not the flaw. The flaw is that their 15% loss and 90% grading are self-reports, and what kills 35 to 40% of your colonies may travel south with them (see the shared assumption below).
- **Cost.** The up-front outlay is about $10,800, leaving about $4,200 of the line.
- **Where it lands on Mar 1.** I expect about 430 graded colonies, with about 370 in a bad case. Chance it misses 420 by Mar 1: ~10%. That figure assumes Rafe delivers; without him the chance is ~40%. Chance the current plan misses: ~90%.

I generated this recommendation myself, so I am the interested party. Everything was compared in one context, not blind, and the final stress test of the recommendation was also run by me rather than a separate reviewer. Read the percentages as one analyst's estimates.

## What the choice had to satisfy

I wrote this down before building the options. I could not seal it independently, so treat it as written, not notarised.

- **Hard constraints.** Up-front cash must not exceed $15,000, wraps included. Colonies must be collectable by the trucks on Feb 20 and must be in a place that really has room.
- **Guards (a challenger must be at least as good as keeping things as they are).**
  - At least $2,500 of the line left after paying up front.
  - Live colonies on Mar 1 no fewer than the outdoor plan gives.
  - The bad-case shortfall cost no higher than the outdoor plan's.
- **Value dimensions.**
  - Expected graded colonies.
  - Net cash through Mar 31.
  - Cost of each extra graded colony.
  - How early I learn whether it is working.
  - Fit with your trucks and the co-op's grading.
- **Adoption.** A plan replaces the current one only if it clears every hard constraint and guard and beats it on expected graded colonies and net cash. If two leaders sit within the noise of each other, I say so rather than force a pick.

## How each finalist dies

### Wrap outdoors (current plan)
**Strongest case:** it costs $3 a colony, it needs no new partner and no extra cash, and it is the one thing you've run for years.
**Killing modes:** dominated by one risk, the arithmetic. At 34 to 40% losses, 600 colonies leave 360 to 396 before anything is graded, and 420 needs 70% of the whole yard to grade. Your best result, 69%, is just short of that. It has missed every recent year, so I give no split.
**Chance it misses the objective:** ~90%, reference class your own three winters; the only disanalogy is that the 150 sample was small and may have been your better colonies.
**Most dangerous:** another 40% winter, which arrives silently in the first cold snap and is not reversible.
**Hidden assumption:** that this winter will look like the best of your last three. **Cheapest falsifier:** a mite wash on 10 colonies per yard this week (a jar and an hour, nothing else). A majority of samples over about 3 mites per 100 bees, a threshold I have not sourced for your region, says a heavy loss is coming.

### Haul south as far as cash allows
**Strongest case:** the highest reported survival (about 85%) and grading (about 90%), a route half the valley uses, and no single building to fail.
**Its own arithmetic:** 220 colonies at $63 is $13,860, plus $1,140 of wraps on the rest, which uses the entire line. Central result is about 426 graded, so it barely clears 420 with nothing in reserve.
**Killing modes:**
- ~20%, because the 90% is neighbours' self-report and mites or virus load travel with the colonies. First sign: a high mite count at the yard in November.
- ~15%, because with $0 in reserve a hay, fuel or feed overrun in January had no money to meet it. First sign: line headroom under $2,500 before Jan 31.
- ~10%, because half the valley now uses Dunmere, so rent, feed supply or yard space tightened (success breeds its own squeeze). First sign: a notice of changed terms.
**Chance it misses the objective:** ~50%, reference class your neighbours' reported 90%; the disanalogy is that you have no figure for their colonies' condition going in.
**Most dangerous:** the empty reserve, which is silent until it is needed.
**Hidden assumption:** that the yard, not the colony, produces the 90%. **Cheapest falsifier:** ask two of those neighbours for their actual numbers (colonies in, graded out) and their fall mite counts. The call is free and takes two days. If their colonies went in with low mite loads, the 90% says little about yours.

### Agnete's cellar
**Strongest case:** the cheapest way to add graded colonies, at about $75 each extra, 40 km away, with four years of loss data (16% average) behind it.
**Its own arithmetic:** 200 colonies × $18 = $3,600, and about 160 graded at her 80%. With the other 400 outdoors that gives about 400, which is 20 short.
**Killing modes:**
- ~20%, because the cellar colonies came out too late or too cold. Her 80% may be measured at a different date, and a Feb 20 pickup means colonies pulled from storage in the coldest weeks must reach 8 frames by Mar 1. First sign: she gives you a pull-out date after Feb 5.
- ~15%, because the cellar held fewer than 200 for you (the 500 may include her 300, or the owner changed terms). First sign: no written number of spaces by Oct 20.
- ~10%, because a ventilation or temperature fault in one building hit all 200 together. First sign: logger readings outside the range Agnete says is safe.
**Chance it misses the objective:** ~70% on its own (central 400, bad case 340); reference class one operator over four winters; the disanalogy is that her grading date and standard are unknown.
**Most dangerous:** the building fault, which is silent in the dark and irreversible once the cluster breaks.
**Hidden assumption:** that her 80% came from comparable colonies on your grading date. **Cheapest falsifier:** ask her and the owner this week, for no cost. If she cannot give you a pull-out date that fits Feb 20, or her 80% is measured later than Mar 1, the number falls.

### Rafe's colonies as cover
**Strongest case:** zero cash now, a fixed $150 price, and it caps the loss on a short year at 80 colonies.
**Its own arithmetic:** 80 × $150 = $12,000 owed at the end of March. Each colony bought lifts you from -$40 to +$40.
**Killing modes:**
- ~20%, because he was not bound. A verbal offer made in October is sold elsewhere or repriced in February, when every beekeeper in the valley with the same winters is short. First sign: no written terms by Oct 20.
- ~15%, because the colonies were refused or failed grading on arrival, or the co-op does not accept colonies you didn't winter. First sign: the co-op's contract says nothing on substitutes.
- ~10%, because the co-op paid late while his invoice came due and the line was already drawn. First sign: the co-op's payment date drifts past Mar 31.
**Chance it misses the objective:** ~50% as a top-up on the outdoor plan alone (reference class: valley-wide short years, when cover is scarce; the disanalogy is that this offer is one broker's word).
**Most dangerous:** the verbal promise, silent until February, when no alternative supply exists.
**Hidden assumption:** that he holds the colonies for you and that the co-op accepts them. **Cheapest falsifier:** ask him for the offer in writing with grade certification, and ask the co-op if substitutes count. Cost nothing, two days. Either refusal kills it.

**What the whole field assumes:** that where the colonies winter decides how many grade. Every option moves colonies around; none addresses why your own losses sit at 35 to 40%. If mites or virus entering winter is the cause, the cellar and the south both inherit it. Test this first.

## The decision rule

**IF** by Oct 20 you hold (a) the cellar owner's written space count of at least 150 with a pull-out date that fits Feb 20, (b) Rafe's offer in writing, grade-certified, payable when the co-op pays, and (c) the co-op's written yes that bought-in colonies count, **THEN** run cellar 200, Dunmere about 100, outdoors 300, with $4,200 left.
**ELSE IF** the cellar gives fewer than 150 spaces, **THEN** haul about 160 to Dunmere (about $10,100), put the cellar's spaces to use, and stop when uncommitted cash reaches $2,500.
**ELSE IF** Rafe or the co-op refuses substitutes, **THEN** haul about 130 and fill the cellar, and ask the co-op for a later grade date or a tolerance on the 420.
**Capability test first:** mite washes this week, before any money is committed. If most samples are high, treat first and haul only colonies strong enough to grade.
**Cheapest decisive experiment:** the mite washes plus the four calls. Cost under $100, five days. The readout that flips the call is a high mite count (hauling doesn't fix it) or a missing written confirmation (use the fallback).
**Runner-up:** cellar 200 and outdoors 400 with Rafe's 80 as standby and no south haul ($4,800 up front). Switch if the Dunmere yard's terms change or mite counts show the hauled colonies won't grade.
**If passing over the runner-up was the mistake:** it is Mar 1 and the cellar held its 200 and graded 80%, Rafe delivered when needed, and the $6,300 haul bought almost nothing beyond about 22 extra surviving colonies, worth roughly $3,300 at Rafe's $150. Net cost about $3,000. The call stands, as that is the price of not depending on two unwritten promises.
