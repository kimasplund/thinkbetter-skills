Go with 2:1 (two parts sugar to one part water, by weight). In fall the bees are storing, not stimulating, and the thicker syrup needs far less work to cure and cap. 1:1 is the spring mix, used to encourage brood rearing.

The checks that matter:

1. **Mix by weight, not volume.** 2:1 by weight is about 2 lb of sugar per 1 lb (roughly 1 pint) of water. By volume it comes out thinner than you intend. Use plain white cane sugar, and warm the water so it dissolves, but don't boil it or scorch the sugar.
2. **Timing and temperature.** Bees need daytime highs of roughly 50 F (10 C) or above to take down and cure syrup. If your nights are already near freezing, 2:1 is still the right ratio, but feed now and use a feeder directly over the cluster. Syrup that sits uncapped in the cold ferments or molds.
3. **Know the target.** Most hives in cold climates need about 60-80 lb of stores to get through winter. Hefting the back of the hive, or weighing it, tells you more than counting jars. A deep box full of syrup will take a few weeks to cap, so don't wait.
4. **Robbing.** With 12 hives, feed late in the day, reduce entrances, and don't spill syrup. Weaker colonies get robbed first.
5. **Mites first.** Treat varroa before or while feeding. Fall bees raised on high mite loads often die in midwinter regardless of how much syrup they stored.

A full options analysis would be overkill for this decision. It is cheap, reversible and well settled in beekeeping practice. If your local winters are unusually mild or severe, your regional bee association can fine-tune the target weights.
