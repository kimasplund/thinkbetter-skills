# Handover: blossom-end rot (BER), Lindqvist Glasshouses

For: Daniel Achterberg, head grower
Assumptions: it is about week 42, the crop runs to week 2 (roughly 12 weeks left), and "drip EC" means the EC of the solution delivered at the dripper. Check these against the records.

## Situation

- Since mid-August, 6-9% of fruit has had BER. Nearly all of it is in compartment 3 (of 3, across 4 ha of truss tomatoes).
- Your job is to choose and implement a fix. Below is what we know, what we only suspect, what is still open, and what not to spend time on.

## What the evidence says

**Calcium supply is not the problem.**
- Feed solution lab tests in weeks 32, 34 and 36 all showed calcium on target.
- Leaf tissue calcium is normal.
- So calcium is reaching the plant. The likely fault is how calcium is moving to the fruit, or what is limiting it. Adding more calcium does not address that.

**Climate in compartment 3 is the leading explanation.**
- Night humidity there is 92-95%. In the other compartments it is 85-88%.
- The old vent motors in compartment 3 only open to 40%, so we can't dehumidify the way we can elsewhere.
- High night humidity means low transpiration. Calcium moves in the water stream, and fruit gets little of it when transpiration is low.
- This is our best explanation, not a proven one. Nobody has yet shown that BER tracks humidity, for example by day-to-day or row-to-row comparison.

**The EC difference is the open question, and it matters.**
- Drip EC in week 36 was 2.8 in compartment 1, 3.0 in compartment 2 and 3.9 in compartment 3.
- A high root-zone EC makes it harder for the plant to take up water. It also cuts transport of calcium to the fruit, and it adds to the humidity effect.
- We never established why compartment 3 is so much higher. Possible causes include a different dosing or recipe setting, a mixing or injector fault, a different substrate or irrigation strategy, or an old reading that nobody corrected.
- Check this first. It is cheap to check, and it could be a cause or an amplifier. It may also be something we can fix within days, unlike the vents.
- We have no drain EC or slab EC numbers in this summary. Ask whether they exist.

## What not to waste time on

1. **Raising calcium nitrate, as the feed rep keeps suggesting.**
   - Calcium in the feed and leaf is already on target.
   - Extra calcium nitrate also raises EC, and compartment 3 is already at 3.9.
   - It would probably make things worse. Only revisit this if new lab data shows a real calcium shortfall.
2. **Foliar calcium sprays, or any foliar application.**
   - Our buyer Grenmark Foods renewed us in their "Clean Leaf" programme in July. It allows no foliar applications of any kind after first fruit set.
   - A spray risks the programme, which is a commercial problem much bigger than the BER. Do not trial it, even on a small section.
3. **More feed lab tests for calcium.**
   - Three clean results are enough. More testing only repeats what we know.
   - A lab test for another purpose is fine, for example drain or slab analysis to explain the EC.
4. **Ordering the vent motors as the in-crop fix** (see section 4).

## Constraints and options

**Gas**
- Our fixed-price contract covers 410,000 m3 to the end of March. We have used 296,000, so 114,000 m3 remains.
- Anything above that is billed at spot, currently about four times the contract rate.
- Heating and venting is the usual way to drive humidity down at night, and it burns gas. Winter demand will also rise.
- Before choosing a climate fix, work out the weekly gas burn and project it to the end of the crop. Then see how much headroom is left for extra night heating in compartment 3.
- Even a modest extra burn in one compartment could push us into spot pricing late in the winter.

**New vent motors for compartment 3**
- The quote is €48,000 with a 10-week lead time.
- If ordered now, they would arrive around week 52. That leaves only a couple of weeks of crop.
- So the motors are unlikely to help this crop. They look like a decision for the next crop, with installation during the turnaround. That decision is yours and the owner's.
- Don't treat them as the answer to the problem we have today.

**In-crop options to evaluate (each one is your call)**
- Find out why compartment 3's EC is high, and bring it toward the 2.8-3.0 seen elsewhere. A higher root-zone EC is a documented way to reduce calcium to the fruit.
- Do whatever is possible with climate inside the 40% vent limit. Options include night heating with a small vent gap, minimum pipe temperature settings, and screen or air-movement adjustments. Each one costs gas, so cost it first.
- Remove affected fruit early so it doesn't distract the plant or add to the loss. Keep it in the grading data.

## Suggested first week

1. Pull the EC and irrigation history for compartment 3. Find out whether the 3.9 is a setting, a fault or a real feature. Take drain and slab EC readings in all three compartments.
2. Log night climate in compartment 3 against the others, and count BER per row or per day. See whether the rate tracks humidity.
3. Calculate the gas run-rate against the 114,000 m3 remaining.
4. Decide the fix, with the cost shown, before any climate changes are made.
5. Tell the feed rep the lab data shows calcium is not the issue, so we won't be adding more calcium nitrate.

## Open questions / things I could not confirm

- The cause of compartment 3's high EC.
- Whether BER rose or fell week by week. We only have a range (6-9%).
- Whether anything differs in compartment 3 besides vents and EC, for example variety, planting date, or substrate.
- The gas burn rate per week.
- Whether the previous head grower has notes. She worked on this for three weeks, so it is worth asking her for them.
