# Handover for Daniel Achterberg: blossom-end rot, Lindqvist Glasshouses

## The problem in brief

- 4 ha of truss tomatoes in three compartments. The crop runs until week 2.
- Since mid-August, blossom-end rot (BER) affects 6-9% of fruit, nearly all of it in compartment 3.
- Your job is to choose and implement a fix. This document gives you what we know, how sure we are, and where not to spend time.

## What we know

| Finding | Source | How much to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| Feed solution calcium on target in weeks 32, 34, 36 | Lab tests | High. Three consistent results. |
| Leaf tissue calcium normal | Tissue test | High for "calcium is in the plant". It does not show whether calcium reaches the fruit. |
| Compartment 3 night humidity 92-95%, versus 85-88% in the others | Climate records | High. It is measured. |
| Compartment 3 vent motors open only to 40% (old motors) | Known equipment limit | High |
| Drip EC in week 36: C1 2.8, C2 3.0, C3 3.9 | Drip readings | Medium. Check the meter calibration and sampling point (see 4a). |

## Our working explanation, and its limits

Calcium reaches fruit mainly in the transpiration stream. Calcium is present in the feed and the leaves, but the fruit may not be receiving it.

Two factors in compartment 3 can each reduce the flow of water and calcium to the fruit:

1. **Low night-time transpiration.** Humidity of 92-95% is the explanation we favoured. It fits the pattern: the problem is concentrated in the compartment with the worst night humidity.
2. **High root-zone salinity.** Drip EC is 3.9, about 1 unit above the other compartments. High EC makes water uptake harder and is a well-known driver of BER, even when calcium is plentiful.

We treated humidity as the main cause. The EC gap is just as large a difference between compartment 3 and the rest, and we have not tested it. Nobody has shown that humidity rather than EC, or the two together, is the cause. Treat the cause as probable, not proven. Rough confidence is about 60% that humidity contributes and about 50% that EC contributes, and both can be true.

Open question: why is compartment 3's EC 0.9-1.1 higher than the others? It could be a different setpoint, a dosing or mixing fault, a different substrate or drain pattern, or a bad reading. Nobody has established this, and it is the cheapest lead to follow.

## Suggested first steps (cheap and fast, before any spending)

a. **Verify the EC.** Calibrate the meter. Measure drip and drain EC in all three compartments on the same day. Find out what setpoint compartment 3 is actually running.
b. **If the 3.9 is real, lower it.** Bring compartment 3 toward the 2.8-3.0 the others run, in steps, and watch fruit over the following 2-3 weeks. BER shows up with a lag, so judge on newly set fruit, not on fruit already affected.
c. **Quantify night climate in compartment 3.** Log humidity, and the temperature at the fruit and crop level. Check how the heating pipe and vent are being used at night compared with compartments 1 and 2.
d. **Count by truss and by date.** Record when affected fruit set, and on which trusses. This tells you whether the problem began with a specific event, such as an EC change or a weather spell, in mid-August.

Do (b) and (c) in a way that lets you separate their effects if you can. Changing everything at once will fix or not fix it without telling you why.

## Decisions you will face

**Night humidity versus gas.** Humidity can be reduced by heating and opening vents slightly, but this burns gas. Our fixed-price contract covers 410,000 m³ to the end of March. We have used 296,000, leaving 114,000 m³. Anything above that is billed at spot, currently about four times the contract rate. I have not given you a forecast of what we will burn through to March. Build one before you commit to a gas-heavy fix, and price the overage for compartment 3 alone. A modest improvement in humidity is only worth it if it costs less than the fruit it saves.

**New vent motors for compartment 3.** The quote is EUR 48,000 with a 10-week lead time. Given the lead time, they would arrive late in the crop, so they can only help for the last weeks before week 2 and for next season. Do not order them as an emergency fix for this crop. Decide on them as a capital investment, and only once your tests show humidity is a real cause. Ask whether the existing motors or linkages can be adjusted to open further for much less money. I do not know if that is possible.

## Constraints you cannot cross

- **Grenmark Foods "Clean Leaf" programme** (renewed in July): no foliar applications of any kind after first fruit set. This rules out calcium sprays and any other foliar product on the crop. Breaching it risks our main buyer. Do not use foliar products, even as a trial, without speaking to me first.

## What not to spend time on

- **Re-testing feed calcium or leaf tissue calcium.** Three feed tests and the leaf test are consistent and normal. More tests will tell us the same thing.
- **Raising calcium nitrate, which our feed supplier's rep keeps recommending.** We have deliberately not done it. Calcium is not short, so more will not help, and it adds to EC, which is already high in compartment 3. Do not change this unless EC and humidity are first ruled out and a new calcium measurement points to a shortage. Expect the rep to ask again.
- **Foliar calcium sprays.** They are forbidden under Clean Leaf (section 6).
- **Compartments 1 and 2.** Their rate is low and their climate and EC look normal. Use them as your reference, not as a problem to solve.
- **Emergency ordering of the vent motors** (section 5).
- **Re-opening the diagnosis from scratch.** The previous head grower and I spent three weeks on it. The list above is what that work produced. The unfinished part is the EC difference and the proof of the humidity link, and that is where your effort should go.
