I'd reframe the goal. A 4-3 board and a two-thirds threshold mean "get everyone on side" will fail if it's a sales campaign. Your job is to produce a deal that the skeptics, the debenture holders, the volunteers and the Vantongerens can each accept, and to run a process members see as fair. Most of what follows is about what to change in the deal, because that is what moves votes.

## The arithmetic

Two-thirds of 410 is 274 yes votes if the bylaws count all voting members, or two-thirds of those voting if they don't. Check the wording today, because it changes the strategy.

- If it's two-thirds of all members, 137 "no" votes or abstentions kill it. Your 140 debenture holders could do that alone, and they are the group the offer treats least well.
- Either way, a 4-3 board can't carry a 67% vote. You need most of the three dissenters, or at least their neutrality.

## Who matters and what they need

- **Debenture holders (about 140).** They gave $500 in 1987 and hold lifetime passes. Alderline's pass product may be cheap and fair as a price. For them it is a loss of a promised right and of status, and possibly a legal problem (see below). Offer them something clearly better than "moved onto a pass product".
- **Volunteers (about 60 patrol and school).** This is the biggest operational risk, and it's easy to miss. You can't open without certified patrollers. If a corporate operator takes over and the volunteers feel they are now unpaid labour for a company, some will stop. Then the hill can't open, whoever owns the lifts. Their consent matters more than their vote count suggests.
- **The Vantongeren family.** The 1987 lease is personal to the co-op. Handing operation to a third party may breach it or need their consent. They have never raised the rent, and they are a goodwill relationship, not a contract party to be worked around. Don't let Alderline or the board approach them first as a legal formality.
- **The three "no" directors.** Their concern is club feel, which is really about control, identity and who decides things. That can be addressed with specific rights, not reassurance.
- **Dariusz and the other two co-signers.** Their interest is real and legitimate: personal liability on a $600k line. It is also a conflict of interest, and the dissenters and members will notice. The co-signing is probably the best argument for the deal, but it will lose force if it appears to be driving it.
- **Your nine employees.** They are promised jobs, but check for how long and on what terms, and whether you are among them.
- **Alderline.** Their interest is a 20-year return. Their alternatives are other hills, so you have leverage only if the deal is genuinely attractive to them, but they also need member votes and a working volunteer base.

## What you all already agree on

Nobody wants the hill to close. Everybody wants better snow reliability, the hill kept as a community asset, and the jobs kept. That can open every conversation. The real disagreement is about how much control and identity to trade for capital, and that can be negotiated in terms of specifics.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Treat these as conditions you take back to members, not wishes.

1. **Protect the club feel in writing.** Ask for a standing operating committee with elected co-op seats and real consultation rights on pass pricing, hours, events and local access. Ask for a community-day schedule, a minimum number of local and youth programs, and a ban on turning the lodge into something members can't use. Make the committee's rights part of the agreement, not a side letter.
2. **Debenture holders.** Check whether the 1987 terms are a legal entitlement. If they are, you can't give them away by vote. Either way, ask for lifetime passes honoured in full, or a clearly equivalent lifetime product at no charge. Don't offer a discount on Alderline's pass. If you want something to ease Alderline's cost, offer holders a voluntary buy-out at a fair price, and don't force it.
3. **Volunteers.** Keep patrol and ski school independent or affiliated rather than absorbed. Ask for family passes at least equal to today's, a written volunteer charter, and Alderline paying for certification, training, equipment, insurance and liability cover. Add a clause that Alderline can't replace volunteers or change the terms without the committee's agreement. If Alderline needs paid patrollers to meet its own standards, say so openly and make the transition a negotiated one.
4. **Release the guarantees.** The $600k line should be repaid or refinanced as part of closing, or the guarantees released, with that as a closing condition. Otherwise the directors who co-signed keep the personal risk while the club's decision rests partly on it. Ask Alderline to fund this out of the first-year payments or an upfront payment.
5. **Lease consent.** Get the Vantongerens' written consent before the vote, and make it a condition of the agreement. If the lease can't be assigned, Alderline may need to take a sublease, or the family may want a new lease directly. Treat this as something to negotiate with them, perhaps for longer security of tenure or a fair rent they actually choose, not as an obstacle.
6. **Capital commitments you can enforce.** The $4.2M should be tied to milestones and dates (snowmaking in year one or two, the quad by a set season), with security, a parent guarantee, or a right to terminate if they miss it. Specify who owns the new equipment on exit.
7. **A way out.** Twenty years is long. Ask for performance triggers (investment, snow-day targets, service levels), a review at years 5 and 10, and defined exit terms. These should cover step-in rights, buy-back of improvements at a formula price, a ban on Alderline selling the agreement without consent, and what happens to pass products and staff if it ends.
8. **Fee and information rights.** An annual fee needs a floor, an index, audit rights and a say over capital changes to the lifts and lodge, which the co-op still owns and may still be paying to insure and inspect. Be clear on who pays insurance, lift inspections and major repairs. Those are your largest fixed costs.
9. **Employees.** Ask for a stated term (for example, five years) with the same terms and conditions, not just "retain".

Note that item 4 and the lease consent are things you need whatever happens at the vote. Start on them now.

## How to run the process

1. **Fix the conflict of interest openly.** Have Dariusz and the other co-signers disclose their position in writing to members. Ask them not to be the lead voices for the deal. Better advocates are a respected dissenter who now supports it, or a debenture holder. Members will trust the presentation more if its main supporters are not the ones with the most to lose personally.
2. **Bring the dissenters in on the terms.** Ask the three "no" directors to write the club-feel protections. If they can name the terms that would change their vote, you have a negotiating brief. If their real objection is that no corporate operator is acceptable, you will know that too, and you can plan on opposition rather than hoping for a conversion.
3. **Talk to the volunteers and debenture holders separately and early.** Hold a meeting for each, before the general presentation, and ask what they would need. Do it to find out, and be ready to change the deal as a result. Ask Alderline to attend so members can hear them directly.
4. **Get an independent view.** A short legal review of the lease, the debenture terms and the bylaws, and a one-page financial comparison of the status quo (including a realistic downside, because thin snow isn't going away) against the deal. Members believe a neutral summary more than they believe a recommendation. Include the alternatives to Alderline: a different operator, a shorter trial term, a partnership limited to snowmaking, a member levy or bond, a loan restructuring. Showing the board has tested them makes "yes" more credible.
5. **Be honest about the downside of saying no.** If the hill is likely to close or need a levy, members should see the numbers and make the choice. Use real figures, not pressure.
6. **Fix the timeline.** Send the agreement and summary at least 30 days ahead, hold two or three Q&A sessions, allow proxy or online voting if the bylaws permit, and keep the AGM procedure above reproach. A disputed vote is worse than a lost one.
7. **Ask the board for a clear mandate.** You were asked to bring a plan to get this approved. A better version is a plan to bring the best possible deal to members and let them decide. That also protects you personally.

## Timeline

- Now to 3 weeks: confirm the vote threshold, get legal advice on lease assignment and debenture rights, open talks with the Vantongerens, and meet the dissenting directors.
- Weeks 3 to 8: meet volunteers and debenture holders, put the list above to Alderline, and get their answers in writing.
- Weeks 8 to 12: revise the terms, produce the independent summary, and have the co-signers' disclosure out.
- Before the AGM: member sessions, then the vote. Count likely support honestly, group by group. If you don't have a credible path to the threshold, consider asking Alderline for an extension or a one-season trial rather than forcing a vote in March.

## What I'd expect

I'm assuming the board numbers, legal terms and bylaws are as you describe, and I haven't seen the agreement itself. On that basis I'd put the odds as reasonable but not strong: roughly even if you do only the process work, and noticeably better if Alderline agrees to the debenture, volunteer and lease items.
