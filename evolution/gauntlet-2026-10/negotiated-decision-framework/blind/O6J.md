The short version: a 4-3 board vote is not a mandate, and the arithmetic at the AGM is harder than it looks. Your job between now and March is to turn the three "no" directors and three outside parties (debenture holders, volunteers, the Vantongeren family) from obstacles into signatories of the terms. Below is who matters, what each really needs, what to ask Alderline to change, and a sequence.

## Where the vote is actually won or lost

- Two-thirds of 410 is 274 yes votes if the bylaws count all members, fewer if they count votes cast. Check this first, because it changes your campaign (turnout vs. persuasion). Also check proxy rules.
- The 140 debenture holders are 34% of the membership. If they vote as a bloc against, they can block the deal on their own. They are the swing group, and they are the group the current offer treats worst: it ends a lifetime pass they paid for in 1987.
- Your 60 volunteers are probably a large share of your most engaged members, and they decide whether you can open at all. They are also the people most likely to read "corporate operator" as "we are now unpaid labour for a company."

## What each party really needs

**Dariusz and the co-signers.** His position is "approve the deal." His interest is getting personal liability off three people's balance sheets. Those are different things, and the second can be met in ways that don't depend on the deal passing. Two consequences:
- Put the release of the $600k personal guarantees into the Alderline term sheet explicitly (Alderline repays or refinances the line at closing, or the co-op does so from a defined source). Right now the offer doesn't appear to address it.
- Members will notice that the loudest advocate has a personal financial stake. Have him disclose it plainly, and let someone else (you, or a no-voting director) present the financial case. Otherwise the opposition gets an easy story: "the treasurer is saving himself."

**The three no-voting directors.** Their stated worry is club feel. That is a real interest, and it is the kind that can be written into a contract, unlike a general fear of corporations. Ask them to specify what "club feel" means in concrete terms (who sets pass prices, who runs events, who uses the lodge, who patrols, who has a voice) and make that list the spine of your asks. Give them the job of leading that negotiation with you. People who write the protections rarely lead the opposition to them. If they can't name anything contractual that would satisfy them, you've learned their objection is about something else (trust, process, or being overruled) and you should deal with that directly.

**Debenture holders.** What they bought is a lifetime pass. A promise to "move everyone onto our season-pass product" does not honour that unless the product is free for them for life. Ask a lawyer now whether the debentures are a binding contractual right, because if they are, the co-op may not be able to waive them by a two-thirds vote, and you'd be heading into a legal problem as well as a political one. Whatever the answer, the fair and cheap fix is for Alderline to honour the lifetime passes (perhaps non-transferable, for the holder's life, with a reasonable blackout or day-limit if Alderline needs one). At 140 passes, the cost to them is small next to $4.2M of capital. You are right that your regular passes are cheap, so there is room to move everyone else to Alderline's product, with a cap on annual increases for the first several years so members aren't surprised.

**Volunteers (patrol and ski school).** They aren't in the offer at all, yet the hill can't open without certified patrollers. Three things to pin down:
- What Alderline's plan for patrol is: keep volunteers, hire paid patrollers, or a hybrid. If they plan to replace volunteers, that changes the economics and the culture, and members deserve to know before voting.
- Whether volunteers keep family passes, and who carries liability and insurance for them under an operator.
- Who is accountable for certification and the daily-opening requirement.
Meet with the patrol and ski school leads before the AGM, show them the draft terms, and let them say what would make them stay. If the volunteers walk, the deal has no operating base, whatever the vote says.

**The Vantongeren family.** This is the item most likely to sink the deal after it passes. The 1987 lease is personal to the co-op. Putting a third-party operator on the upper runs may count as an assignment or sublease, which would need their consent, and it hands them leverage they've never used, at a $11k rent they've never raised. Don't let them first hear about this at the AGM. Approach them early and respectfully, find out what they'd need (a rent reset, a longer term, a say in who operates on their land, assurance about liability and land use), and make their written consent a condition of closing. A better lease secured now is worth far more than a dispute in year two. Have a lawyer read the lease first so you know what you can and can't promise them.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Group these by what they protect.

1. **Money and risk for the co-op**
   - Retire or refinance the $600k line and release the personal guarantees at closing.
   - A fee with a guaranteed floor, not only a share of revenue, since thin snow is what hurt you. Ask them to show you modelled downside winters.
   - Capital commitments in the contract with dates (snowmaking, then the quad), a completion deadline, and a remedy if they slip: fee reduction, step-in rights, or termination.
   - Maintenance and condition obligations for the lifts and lodge you still own, with handback standards at the end.

2. **Control and exit**
   - Termination rights for the co-op on material breach, and a review point (say year 5 and year 10) with a right to exit if capital promises or service levels weren't met. Twenty years is long; this is the biggest structural concern.
   - What happens if Alderline sells or is acquired: consent or a right to terminate on change of control.
   - Co-op reversion rights and a clear plan for what you'd inherit (staff, equipment, season-pass data) if it ends.

3. **Members**
   - Lifetime passes honoured for debenture holders.
   - Price caps on member passes and a guaranteed member tier for the first years.
   - Seats for the co-op on an advisory body with real information rights, and reserved lodge or hill time for club events and racing.

4. **People**
   - Employee protection is currently a promise. Make it a contract term with a defined period (for example, at least three years), with terms at least equal to current ones, and notice before any change.
   - The patrol and ski-school arrangements above, in writing.

5. **Third parties**
   - Vantongeren consent to the arrangement, and a plan for the lease if Alderline's tenure ends.

You won't get everything. Rank these before you go in. My suggested priority is: guarantee release, debenture passes, the Vantongeren consent, exit and breach rights, then the patrol terms. Items that cost Alderline little but matter greatly to members (the lifetime passes, price caps, advisory seat) are the easiest to win, so ask for those first and don't trade them away.

## How to get members on side

Sequence matters more than speeches.

1. **Now to about 4 weeks:** Get legal advice on three questions: the voting threshold and proxy rules, whether the debentures are enforceable rights, and whether the Vantongeren lease needs consent. These shape everything else.
2. **Weeks 2 to 6:** Meet separately with the Vantongerens, the patrol and ski school leads, and the debenture-holder group. Bring the question, not a pitch: "Here is the offer; what would you need to see?" Take what you learn to Alderline as non-negotiable changes backed by named groups, which is much stronger than your own preferences.
3. **Parallel:** Sit down with the three no directors and agree the club-feel list. Aim to have at least one of them willing to say publicly "I voted no, I negotiated these protections, and I now support it." Even one is worth more than a hundred of your own arguments. If none will, treat that as a signal about the deal.
4. **Before the notice goes out:** Circulate the revised terms and a plain summary with the downside shown: what happens in a bad snow year under each path, including "do nothing." Include a no-deal alternative that is credible. Members are more likely to trust a proposal when the alternative is described fairly, and you'll need it because the other option is not "nothing": three losing winters in five, a bank line personally guaranteed by three directors, and presumably no money for snowmaking.
5. **Late winter:** Hold two or three open sessions and a member Q&A, with Alderline present for some and absent for others so people can ask you freely. Put a one-page comparison on the table: what changes for a debenture holder, a regular member, a volunteer, and an employee.
6. **Count early.** Before the AGM, work out how many yes votes you have, from direct conversations and the member list, and where the gap is. Spend the last weeks on the debenture holders and volunteers, since that's where the votes are.

## Things to avoid

- Don't let the pitch rest on "the board recommends it" when the board split 4-3. Present the minority view honestly and show how the revised terms answer it.
- Don't present the loss history as the whole case. Members know it. Show what the capital buys and the odds it works, and be candid that snowmaking helps but doesn't remove risk.
- Don't let the vote be the first time any of the three outside parties hears the terms.
- Don't promise anything on Alderline's behalf that isn't in the signed agreement. The employee assurance is the example: it should be a clause, not a verbal reassurance.

## If Alderline won't move

Some asks (guarantee release, lifetime passes, exit rights) are such core protections that a refusal tells you something about how they'll behave over 20 years. In that case, you have a few choices: a shorter initial term with renewal options, a phased deal (snowmaking first, wider operating rights later), or going back to the board and members with an honest "not on these terms." Also consider quietly sounding out one other operator or lender, even if only to understand your leverage. You'll negotiate better knowing Alderline isn't your only route.

## Assumptions I made

I don't know your bylaws, the debenture wording, the exact terms of the 1987 lease, or Alderline's financials, so several points above depend on a lawyer's reading. I'm also assuming the $4.2M is committed capital rather than a target. If any of those assumptions are off, the order of priorities may change, but the core approach holds: get the legal facts straight, get the three outside parties and the dissenting directors to shape the terms, and bring members a deal that visibly protects the people who built the club.
