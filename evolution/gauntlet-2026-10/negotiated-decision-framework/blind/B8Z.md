## Fix these before anything else (they could sink the deal regardless of the vote)

1. **The Vantongeren lease.** It's personal to the co-op. An operating agreement in which Alderline runs the hill, and especially uses the upper runs, may count as an assignment, sublease or change of use. Have a lawyer read the 1987 lease now. Then talk to the Vantongerens early and in person, before they hear about it from a member. A family that has never raised the rent is a goodwill asset, and the upper runs are probably a large share of your terrain. Ask for their written consent or a new lease that names the arrangement. Ask Alderline to make the deal conditional on that consent, and to pay any rent increase the family asks for.

2. **Volunteer patrol and ski school.** You need certified patrollers on duty every day to open, and about 60 volunteers do this in return for family passes. Alderline's promise to keep the nine employees doesn't cover them. Find out what Alderline plans: paid patrol, which is costly and which they may price into the fee or pass terms, or keeping the volunteers. If they keep the volunteers, ask what the volunteers get, who carries liability, who certifies and schedules them, and whether the family passes stay. Talk to patrol leadership before the AGM. If they feel taken for granted, they could walk out, and then the hill can't open.

3. **The 140 debenture holders.** They paid $500 in 1987 for lifetime passes. Moving them to Alderline's pass product may be fair in price terms, but they hold a legal right. Get advice on whether you can change it without their individual consent. Ask Alderline to honor lifetime passes (or something clearly equal) in the agreement. This group is about a third of your votes and it's the group most attached to the club.

4. **Dariusz's position.** He and two other directors personally guaranteed the $600k line. If the deal retires the line or releases the guarantees, those three have a personal financial stake in the yes vote. That doesn't make their judgment wrong, but members will notice, and the no-voters will say so if you don't. Disclose it openly in the member package. Ask Alderline whether the deal repays or replaces the line, and make that a visible term. Consider having the three directors abstain from the board's recommendation, or at least state the interest on the record. Whether the deal is good for the co-op shouldn't depend on freeing the guarantors.

## What to ask Alderline to change

Aim for terms that protect the co-op if things go badly, since that is what the no-voters are worried about:

- **Term and exit.** Twenty years is long. Ask for a performance review at about years 5 and 10, with the right to terminate if snowmaking and the quad aren't built on a set schedule, if the hill's operating days fall below a stated minimum, or if fees are missed.
- **Investment.** Put the $4.2M in the contract with dates, specifications and a remedy if it isn't delivered. Ask who owns the snowmaking and the chair at the end, and what happens to them on early termination. Ask for a bond or guarantee from the parent company, not just a subsidiary.
- **Fee.** Find out whether it's fixed, a floor plus a share of revenue, or something else. Ask for a guaranteed minimum that covers your fixed costs (insurance, inspections, the lease), and for audit rights over the revenue they report.
- **Maintenance and condition on return.** Co-op keeps ownership of lifts and lodge, so specify who maintains and who pays for major repairs, and what condition they must be in at the end.
- **Employees.** Put the "keep all nine" promise in writing with a stated period (at least 2 to 3 years), and say what happens on later turnover.
- **Club character.** Ask for concrete protections: a co-op advisory committee with real consultation rights, protected community events and days, local-resident or member pricing for a set period, and no rebranding of the hill without approval.
- **Passes.** Get the member pass prices and any cap on increases in writing for a number of years. You said your passes are cheap, so members will see a large jump unless the agreement limits it.
- **Governance.** Ask for a change-of-control clause (the co-op approves or can exit if Alderline is sold), limits on assigning the agreement, and clarity on who holds insurance and liability.
- **Lender and legal.** Have the co-op's own lawyer, not Alderline's, review the draft, and a financial adviser model the co-op's finances under the deal and under no deal.

Decide beforehand which of these are must-haves (lease consent, debenture rights, termination rights, written investment commitment) and which are nice-to-have, so you know what you can trade.

## A realistic no-deal comparison

Members will ask "what happens if we vote no?" Prepare an honest answer. You've lost money in three of five winters; show what the finances look like with the current line, what the realistic alternatives are (snowmaking financed by a member levy or a grant, a smaller operating partner, a lease to a different operator, a staged deal), and how long you can last. Don't make it a scare tactic. If members feel pushed, the 140 old-timers and the three dissenting directors will dig in harder. If the no-deal path really is closure, say so with numbers.

## Process to build trust before March

- **Meet the dissenters first.** Ask the three no-voting directors what specific terms would change their vote. They've named "club feel", which is vague. Turn it into concrete contract clauses (the list above) and give them a role in negotiating with Alderline. If you can get one or two of them to support an amended deal, that does more than any presentation.
- **Send the full draft agreement and a plain-language summary to every member**, well ahead of the AGM, including both the benefits and the risks. Include the board's 4-3 split and the minority's reasons in their own words.
- **Hold two or three information sessions** (one evening, one weekend, perhaps one online) with Alderline present for questions and then a session without them, so members can speak freely. Hold a separate session for debenture holders and one for patrol and ski school volunteers.
- **Make an independent voice available**, such as the co-op's lawyer or an adviser, who can answer questions about the contract without a stake in the result.
- **Check the AGM mechanics.** Confirm notice periods, proxy and mail-in voting, and whether a quorum or a two-thirds-of-all-members rule applies. Make it easy to vote, because many people who like the club but rarely come will decide the result. Consider an advisory straw poll beforehand to find out where members stand.
- **Be ready for the possibility of amending the offer or delaying.** If after negotiation you're still short, the right move may be to postpone to a special meeting rather than force a vote that fails 55-45 and splits the club.

## What to say

For members who are inclined to yes: the snow problem is real, the co-op keeps the lifts and lodge, and the new money is contractually protected. For the skeptics: here is what we got changed because you raised it, here is how you can leave if Alderline underdelivers, and here is what the board gave up to get those protections. Be specific about trade-offs and don't claim the deal has no downsides. A pitch that admits the risks will be believed more than one that doesn't.
