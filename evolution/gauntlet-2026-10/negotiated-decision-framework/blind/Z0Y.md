The deal is probably worth doing, but as it stands I don't think you can get it through a two-thirds vote. Several groups hold a veto, and some of them don't know yet that they do. I'd spend the time before March fixing that, not on persuasion.

## Where the vote actually sits

- **The debenture holders alone can block it.** About 140 of 410 members is roughly 34%. If turnout is high, they are more than the one-third needed to defeat the motion. Moving them onto Alderline's pass product may also be a legal problem, not only an emotional one. Check with a lawyer what the 1987 debenture terms promise. If the lifetime passes are contractual rights, the co-op can't hand them over to Alderline without each holder's consent, and a majority vote won't change that. Find this out first.
- **The Vantongeren family holds a hard veto.** The lease is personal to the co-op. An operating agreement that gives Alderline control of the hill may count as an assignment or a change of use, and the family could refuse to consent. Without the upper runs you probably don't have a viable hill. They have never raised the rent, so they are your most generous counterparty. Don't let them hear about this from the grapevine. Go to them early, ask for written consent, and ask what they would need in return. Possible answers are a rent guarantee, a right of first refusal if the hill is ever sold, or a named contact at Alderline.
- **The volunteers are your operational veto.** You can't open without certified patrollers, and about 60 people give their time for family passes. A corporate operator may bring different liability and insurance requirements, may want to professionalize patrol, and may not value the family-pass arrangement. If they feel replaced, they won't do the work. Ask the patrol and ski school leads what would make them stay, and get Alderline to put it in writing.
- **Dariusz has a conflict that needs handling.** He and two other directors personally guaranteed the $600k line, and he is the strongest advocate. His reasoning may be sound, but the three no voters and the members can fairly ask whether the deal is mainly a way to get him out of the guarantee. Treat it as a disclosure matter. Have the directors state the guarantee openly at the AGM, and ask Alderline to repay or replace the line, or release the guarantors, as part of the deal. If the guarantee is released as a stated term, the issue becomes a benefit to the co-op, and it stops looking like a hidden motive.

## Why people are on each side

Everyone wants the hill to survive and stay a good place to ski. The disagreement is mostly about who controls it and what happens if it goes badly.

- **The three no votes** say "club feel," but the underlying worry is probably losing control and being unable to undo the decision. That can be addressed with contract terms, so ask them what specific things they are afraid of losing.
- **Debenture holders** care about a promise made to them and about being treated as founders, not customers.
- **Volunteers** care about being valued and about the family passes.
- **Staff** care about job security. "Keep all nine" needs a stated duration, pay and conditions, and what happens at renewal.
- **Alderline** wants a long, predictable operation and a return on $4.2M. That gives you leverage on anything that doesn't threaten the return.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Work through these in order of how much they matter.

1. **Protect the debenture holders.** Honor the lifetime passes for the life of the holder, or offer a permanent equivalent at no cost. Whether this is a legal obligation or a goodwill gesture, it is likely the cheapest way to remove a one-third block. It also costs Alderline little, given how low pass prices are.
2. **A written commitment for the volunteer programs.** Keep patrol and ski school volunteer-run, keep the family passes, give the co-op a seat on safety decisions, and confirm who carries liability and insurance for volunteers.
3. **Make the capital commitment enforceable.** The $4.2M should have a schedule (snowmaking by a named winter, the quad by another), a minimum standard, and a remedy if Alderline misses it, such as a reduced fee, a right to terminate, or a bond. Without these, the promise is just words.
4. **Exit and reversion terms.** Define what happens on early termination, on Alderline's sale of the agreement, on default, and at year 20. The co-op should keep a right to take back operations if Alderline fails to perform or is sold to a party the co-op doesn't accept. Consider a review at years 5 and 10, with termination rights if agreed measures (snow days operated, lift uptime, season-pass numbers) are missed. These terms are what turn a 20-year lock-in into a decision members can reverse.
5. **Guard "club feel" with something specific.** Offer a members' advisory council with real consultation rights, a pass price cap or index for existing members, set community days, local hiring preference, and a limit on branding changes. Ask the skeptics to draft this list. It makes them co-authors of the terms.
6. **Release the guarantors and settle the line.** See above.
7. **Employees.** Specify the 9 jobs for a minimum term, with comparable pay and conditions, and no termination without cause during the capital build.
8. **Fee and financial terms.** Look at a floor plus a share of revenue, not a flat fee, and audit rights on the books. Make sure you can see how the $11k lease payment and insurance and lift inspection costs are allocated, and who pays if there is a bad snow year.
9. **Lifts and lodge.** Confirm that ownership stays with the co-op, that Alderline carries maintenance and capital replacement to a stated standard, and that the assets come back in at least the condition they were handed over.

Alderline may refuse some of these. How it responds is useful information. If it won't accept performance terms or an exit, that tells you what it's really offering.

## Compare against the alternative

Members will be voting against something. Put the fallback in front of them honestly: three losing winters out of five, what the next bad winter costs, what the $600k line looks like, and what the co-op could do alone (a smaller snowmaking investment, a member levy, a grant, or a different operator). If staying independent is also unattractive, that supports the deal. Test this with the three no votes, because a credible alternative is the strongest thing they have. If you can't answer "what is the plan if this fails," the vote will probably fail too.

## A sensible order of work

1. **Now to three weeks:** get legal advice on the debenture terms, the lease consent, the vote threshold and turnout rules, and the guarantee. Then meet the Vantongeren family and the patrol and ski school leads.
2. **Weeks 3 to 8:** negotiate the list above with Alderline. Bring the three dissenting directors into the process, perhaps one on the negotiating team, so the board stops being split 4-3.
3. **Weeks 8 to 12:** circulate the revised terms and a plain summary with the fallback case. Hold open sessions, ideally some on the hill, and a specific meeting for debenture holders. Have a director who is a no vote speak on what changed.
4. **Before the AGM:** count the votes. Phone the 140 debenture holders and the largest blocs, and find out who is undecided and why. If the number doesn't reach two-thirds, don't force it. Ask the board to defer the vote or revise the terms instead of losing and having the issue closed.

## What to tell the board

Tell them you can bring a plan to get this approved, but that it depends on the debenture holders, the Vantongerens and the volunteers, so winning the vote by persuasion alone isn't realistic. Also tell them that a unanimous board is not necessary, but a board that is open about the guarantee and has taken the dissenters' conditions into the contract will be far more credible with members than one that argues them down.

I've assumed that the debenture rights, the lease and the vote rules are as you describe them. Confirm each with a lawyer before you commit to a timeline.
