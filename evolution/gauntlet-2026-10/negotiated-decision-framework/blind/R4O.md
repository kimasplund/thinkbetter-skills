I'd change the goal slightly. Don't aim for "get this approved." Aim for a vote that members trust and that the deal can survive. A 4-3 board split and a two-thirds threshold mean you need about 274 of 410 members. A plan built on persuasion alone will probably fail, and if it passes on a thin or resented margin, the volunteers you depend on may quietly leave. The better plan is to fix the problems that are real, then let the members decide with good information.

Assumptions: I haven't seen the term sheet, the bylaws, the debenture terms or the Vantongeren lease. Several points below depend on what those say, so get them checked by a lawyer.

## Four issues that could sink the deal, or the co-op, if they're missed

**1. The Vantongeren lease is personal to the co-op.**
This is the most serious gap. The upper runs sit on land leased to the co-op alone. If Alderline "operates the hill," that may count as an assignment, a sublease or a change of use, and that may need the family's consent. If consent isn't given, the lease could be at risk, and Alderline's $4.2M would then be spent on a hill that has lost part of its terrain.
- Talk to the Vantongerens early, and do it before the deal is public if you can. They have been fair for decades and deserve to hear this from you, not from rumor.
- Ask for their written consent, or a new lease or amendment, that explicitly covers an operator arrangement and runs for the full 20 years.
- They have never raised the rent, but an operator's arrival is the moment they may reasonably want to renegotiate. Know what they would want.
- Make written landlord consent a condition precedent in the agreement. Alderline should not be able to close without it.

**2. Dariusz and the co-signers have a conflict of interest.**
Dariusz has pushed hardest, and he and two other directors personally guarantee the $600k line. If the deal retires or releases that guarantee, he has a personal financial stake. That doesn't make him wrong, and the co-op may well owe him a release. But the no-voters will see it, and it can taint the whole process.
- Disclose it openly, in the member package, before anyone else raises it.
- Check what the bylaws say about director conflicts, and consider having the three co-signers abstain from the board's final recommendation or from leading the campaign.
- Ask whether the deal actually deals with the line. If it doesn't, the guarantee risk remains with the three of them. If it does, members should know. Either way, have someone without a stake present the financials.
- Separately, the co-op probably should find a way to release the co-signers from their guarantees, whether or not the deal passes. That is a fairness issue on its own.

**3. The 140 debenture holders and their lifetime passes.**
You say moving everyone to Alderline's pass "seems fair." That's your view, but for these members it may be a legal right, and it's a third of the electorate. Taking away a lifetime pass someone bought for $500 in 1987 without their consent could be a breach of contract or of the debenture terms. Even if it isn't, it's what the no-campaign will focus on.
- Get the actual debenture terms read by a lawyer. What exactly was promised, and can it be varied by a member vote?
- Ask Alderline to honor lifetime passes for living holders, at least in a basic form (for example, a lifetime season pass, possibly non-transferable). The cost to Alderline is probably small compared with the cost of losing the vote. Ask them for the numbers.
- Talk to this group directly, in their own meeting, before the AGM.
- Don't assume they are one bloc. Some will want a fair buyout, some the pass, some just respect.

**4. Volunteer patrol and ski school.**
About 60 volunteers run patrol and ski school, and you need certified patrollers on duty to open at all. A for-profit operator that relies on unpaid labor has a legal and ethical problem. Volunteers may reasonably refuse to work for free for a corporation, and the family passes they get in return would be replaced by Alderline's product. If patrol thins out, the hill doesn't open, however much snowmaking there is.
- Meet the volunteer leads early and ask what would make them stay. This is a live deal-breaker, not a detail.
- Ask Alderline to say in writing how it will staff patrol and ski school: paid, volunteer, or mixed. If it intends to keep volunteers, it needs to guarantee them passes and recognition, cover them for liability and workers' compensation as applicable, and confirm that its insurer accepts the arrangement.
- Have the agreement say who holds the patrol certification and insurance responsibility.
- Check whether the volunteers are members with votes. Many probably are.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Treat these as asks to negotiate, not demands. Rank them: some are must-haves and the rest are trade-able.

**Must-haves**
- Landlord consent from the Vantongerens as a condition to closing (above).
- Lifetime-pass treatment for debenture holders (above).
- A written patrol and ski school plan, including volunteer terms and insurance.
- Real protection of the co-op's assets. The co-op keeps ownership of the lifts and lodge, which is good. The agreement should say who pays for maintenance, inspections, insurance and capital repairs, what condition the assets must be returned in, and what happens if Alderline defaults or walks away.
- Performance commitments for the $4.2M: what is built, by when, to what specification, and what happens if it isn't delivered. Ideally tie the money to milestones, with security or a guarantee if Alderline is a subsidiary.
- Exit rights. Twenty years is long. Ask for termination rights for the co-op on defined failures (missed investment, unsafe operation, unpaid fees), a buy-back or step-in option, and clarity on what happens at the end of the term: the new snowmaking and chair should remain with the hill.
- Employee protection beyond "they've promised." Nine jobs should be in the contract, with a defined period, not a verbal promise.

**Important, negotiable**
- Fee structure. Is the annual fee fixed, a percentage of revenue, or both? Is there a floor? Does it get adjusted for inflation? Members will ask what the co-op gets in a bad snow year. If the fee is only a share of revenue, the co-op still carries the weather risk it wants to be rid of.
- Pricing protections: caps on season-pass increases for the first several years, and local or member rates, so that "cheap" doesn't become "expensive" after the vote.
- Community features: a local advisory committee with real consultation rights, a say over hours, events, ski school for local kids, and use of the lodge for club events. This addresses "club feel" with something enforceable.
- Reserved matters: Alderline can't sell, assign or change control of the agreement without co-op consent.
- Data and records: audit rights over revenue if the fee depends on it.

## How to build support, in order

1. **Get the facts first (now to about 8 weeks out).** Independent legal review of the agreement, the lease and the debenture terms. Someone neutral, such as an accountant who isn't a director, should model the co-op's finances under three scenarios: the deal, no deal, and a modest alternative such as a smaller capital campaign or other operator. Show the no-deal path honestly. "We have lost money in three of five winters" is a real argument, but members will want numbers, including what the $600k line costs and what a bad winter does.
2. **Negotiate the asks above.** Go back to Alderline with the list and be willing to say that the vote won't pass without these changes. That is leverage, because they need two-thirds of your members too. If they won't move on lifetime passes, landlord consent or volunteers, treat that as information about what they will be like for 20 years.
3. **Bring the dissenting directors in.** The three no votes are your best guide to member objections. Give them the job of writing the "protections" list, or of stress-testing the draft. If the final package includes enforceable club-feel provisions they helped write, they may support it or at least stop opposing it. A board that presents 4-3 will not persuade 274 members.
4. **Segment your outreach.** Different groups worry about different things:
   - Debenture holders: passes and respect.
   - Volunteers: recognition, passes, liability, and whether they are still needed.
   - Regular skiers and families: pricing and access.
   - The Vantongerens: the lease.
   - Employees: contract terms (note they may also be members).
   Hold small meetings by group, not only one big town hall. Include a Q&A document answering the hard questions in plain language, especially the awkward ones, such as Dariusz's guarantee.
5. **Make the information package fair.** One document with the agreement summary, the financial scenarios, a list of changes you secured, and a place for the dissenting directors to state their case. A balanced package builds trust, and it makes it harder to challenge the vote afterward.
6. **Check the process.** Confirm notice periods, quorum, proxy rules and whether the two-thirds is of members present or of all 410. Consider allowing proxies or remote and mail voting so turnout isn't the failure point. If the AGM can't be held with enough people, you could lose on logistics.
7. **Have a fallback.** Decide now what happens if the vote fails or if Alderline refuses key changes. If a "no" means closing in a year or two, say so. If it doesn't, say what the alternative is. It may be worth asking whether a vote could be delayed a season if the key conditions, such as landlord consent, aren't settled by March. A delayed deal with consent is better than a rushed vote that falls apart later.

## A caution about your role

The board asked you to bring a plan that "gets this approved." As general manager you can legitimately advocate for it, but you will do best if you are seen as running a fair process and not a campaign. If the facts support the deal, a transparent process will carry it. If the Vantongeren consent or the debenture rights turn out to be real blockers, you'll want to learn that in January, not in March.
