Short version: don't try to "get everyone on side." Your job is to get from 4-3 to a two-thirds member vote, and the deal as offered probably can't get there. Fix three structural problems with Alderline first, then run a member process that is clearly not a sales campaign. The lease and the debenture holders are the biggest risks, and neither is a persuasion problem.

## The numbers you are up against

Two-thirds of 410 is about 274 yes votes. If everyone votes, 137 "no" votes kill it. The 140 debenture holders are a blocking bloc on their own. If they feel their lifetime passes are being traded away, they can stop the deal without any help from the three dissenting directors. Treat them as the main constituency, not a footnote.

Turnout also matters. If you get 250 voters, you need about 167 yes. Part of the plan is getting the people who feel positive but passive to actually show up.

## Who really matters, and what they want

- **Debenture holders (~140).** Their stated position is "keep my lifetime pass." Underneath, they paid $500 in 1987 to help build something, and they want that to be recognized. A pass swap that Alderline calls fair is, to them, a broken promise. Many of them are also probably the most emotionally tied to the club feel.
- **The three dissenting directors.** Their stated worry is that a corporate operator will kill the club feel. The specific fears are probably: pricing, crowding, loss of local control, volunteers being treated as free labor, and the club becoming a brand. Those are things you can write into a contract. Vague culture fears can't be answered, but specific ones can.
- **Dariusz and the two other co-signers.** I'll be direct about this. The three of them have a personal financial stake, because they are on the hook for the $600k line. That is a conflict of interest, and the three no-voting directors and some members will notice it. Even if his judgment is sound, he is the wrong person to lead the pitch. Disclose it up front. Better still, make retiring the guarantee an explicit ask of Alderline (see below), so that the co-signers' risk is handled in the deal rather than lurking behind it.
- **The ~60 volunteers.** Patrol and ski school are the hill's real operating capacity. You cannot open without certified patrollers on duty. They are paid in family passes. Alderline's own pass product could be seen as replacing that deal. If a corporation takes over and treats them as unpaid staff, a meaningful number could quit, and then the lifts don't turn no matter how much snowmaking exists. Many are probably also members and voters.
- **The nine employees.** They are protected by the promise, but ask how long and on what terms. "Keep all nine" at signing is different from "for five years at current pay."
- **The Vantongeren family.** They are not at the table, but they may be the most important party in the whole thing. See next section.
- **Alderline.** They want a long-term, low-risk operating base and a pass-product footprint. The 20-year term suggests they need that time to earn back $4.2M, so they will resist short outs but may give on protections.

## The problem nobody has raised yet: the lease

The upper runs sit on Vantongeren land under a 1987 lease that is personal to the co-op. "Personal" usually means it can't be assigned, sublet or handed to another operator without the landlord's consent. A 20-year operating agreement in which Alderline runs the hill may well count as that. I haven't seen the lease and you need a lawyer to read it, but the risk is clear: if the family can object or terminate, the deal could lose the upper runs, and the quad chair investment could end up in a worse position than before.

Also consider the human side. The family has held the rent at $11k for years. That is goodwill, and it was given to a member-owned neighbor. A corporate operator replacing the co-op could change how they feel, and they should hear about it from you before they hear it anywhere else.

What to do:
1. Get a lawyer to read the lease now and say whether the operating agreement requires consent.
2. Talk to the Vantongerens early, in person, before anything is announced widely.
3. Make written landlord consent, possibly with a new or extended lease directly benefiting the co-op, a condition precedent in the Alderline agreement. If the family won't consent, the deal doesn't close and Alderline bears no penalty from the co-op, and members vote knowing that.
4. Expect the family to ask for something, such as higher rent or a longer term. Better to know what, and who pays, before March than after.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Group these by what they protect.

**Protect the co-op's assets and exit**
- Lifts and lodge stay co-op property, which is already the offer. Add that Alderline's improvements, including snowmaking and the new quad, become co-op property or are bought out at a fixed schedule if the agreement ends early.
- No assignment or change of control without co-op consent. You are agreeing to this operator, not to whoever buys them.
- A defined exit. Termination rights if they miss operating standards (days open, lifts running, maintenance, snowmaking commissioned by a date), and a requirement to hand back the hill in good operating condition.
- The $4.2M tied to a dated schedule with the quad and snowmaking milestones written in, with security or a guarantee if they slip. A promise that isn't scheduled isn't an investment.
- Alderline carries lift inspection, insurance and operating liability and indemnifies the co-op. Check that the co-op isn't left holding the risk for operations it no longer controls.

**Protect the money**
- Annual fee: a guaranteed minimum, with a revenue share above it, indexed to inflation. Ask what happens in a bad snow year. If the fee drops to nothing then, the co-op has taken on the downside it was trying to escape.
- The $600k line of credit: Alderline either pays it down or the co-op uses part of the first fees to retire it, with a date, so the three co-signers are released. This also removes the question of whether Dariusz is pushing the deal to free himself.

**Protect members**
- Debenture holders: keep a lifetime pass of equivalent access for them. It is a small cost compared with the risk of losing a bloc that can defeat the vote. If Alderline refuses, at least offer a lifetime pass for the holder's life, not a discount, and have the co-op check what the 1987 debenture terms actually say. It may be a legal right, not just a courtesy.
- A price cap for member and local season passes, e.g. capped annual increases for some years. You think the passes are cheap, and that's the point: a big price step-up is the first thing members will notice.
- Protect the real fairness issue. Moving everyone onto Alderline's pass product is reasonable in principle, but it should apply on a phase-in, not overnight.

**Protect the volunteers and staff**
- Keep the patrol and ski school as volunteer organizations, with the family-pass arrangement written in. Alderline should be obligated to supply the passes and the equipment, and should not be able to cut them without cause.
- Check who employs, insures and manages the patrol under the new agreement, and what certification and liability look like. Ask the patrol leaders what they would need, directly.
- Employee protection for a stated period, with severance if Alderline ends employment without cause.

**Protect the club feel, which is what the dissenters care about**
- A co-op seat or standing advisory committee with real information rights: operating data, pricing plans and capital plans.
- Defined community items: member nights, a local ski program, events, lodge access for co-op business, use of the hill for community events.
- A review at years 5, 10 and 15, with rights to renegotiate or exit if the standards aren't met.

You won't get every one of these. Rank them. My view is that the must-haves are the lease consent, the snowmaking and quad schedule, the minimum fee, the exit and handback terms, the volunteer program, and the debenture solution. The rest are tradeable.

## How to run the member process

1. **Settle the lease and Alderline asks first, before you campaign.** If you go to members with an unfinished deal, every unanswered question becomes a reason to vote no.
2. **Make the board's disagreement visible and useful.** The three no votes are your best test. Invite them to write the "conditions we would need" list and take it to Alderline. Some of their conditions will be in the contract, which gives them something to vote for. If they remain opposed, give them space to put their view in the member materials. A 4-3 board that hides its split will look like a stitch-up.
3. **Let Dariusz disclose, and let others lead.** Someone not involved in the guarantee, perhaps a respected long-time member or a director who supports the deal but has no financial stake, should present the finances. Dariusz can explain the line of credit in the open.
4. **Show members the status quo honestly.** You've lost money in three of five winters. Show what it looks like if nothing changes: the cost of keeping going, the risk to the line of credit, and what the board would have to do (levies, closing runs, debt). A clear "no" case is more convincing than any pitch, and it makes the vote about choices, not loyalty.
5. **Consider a middle path as a fallback.** For example: a smaller snowmaking investment, a shorter trial term, a management contract instead of full operation, or a step-in right if Alderline underperforms. If the vote looks short, offering a shorter term (e.g. 10 years with extension) may be what converts the "not yet" crowd.
6. **Go to the debenture holders directly.** Hold a session for them, written confirmation of what they keep, and a named person to ask. A vague letter won't do it.
7. **Meet the volunteers separately**, with the patrol and ski school heads in the room, and get them to say publicly whether the arrangement works for them. Their endorsement carries weight with other members.
8. **Hold member information sessions and a Q&A in the lodge** over the winter, with the draft agreement and a plain-English summary available. Give an independent legal and financial review of the agreement, so members don't have to rely on the board's version.
9. **Count the votes.** Build a list of the 410 members, find out who is firmly for, firmly against, or undecided, and focus on getting out the vote. A member who is for but doesn't turn up counts as a no against a two-thirds threshold. Check the by-laws on proxies and mail-in voting.
10. **Be willing to delay.** If the lease consent or the debenture issue isn't resolved by the March AGM, a deferred vote is better than a failed one. A defeated deal is hard to bring back, and Alderline may well accept more time if the alternative is a no.

## My honest read

On the information you've given me, I wouldn't bring the offer to the AGM in its current form. The risks are not about whether members like corporations. They are a lease that may block the deal, a bloc of 140 members with a real grievance, a volunteer workforce that the operation depends on, and a treasurer whose personal exposure invites suspicion. All four can be addressed by asking Alderline for the right terms. If they will agree to the lease consent, the debenture solution and the volunteer protections, I'd say the odds of reaching two-thirds are decent. If they won't, that tells you something about the partner, and the members deserve to see that too.

Assumptions I made: I haven't seen the lease, the debenture terms, the by-laws or the Alderline draft, so check each of those with counsel before relying on any of the above.
