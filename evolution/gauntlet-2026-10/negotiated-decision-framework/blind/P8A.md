I'd change the goal slightly. Don't aim for a plan that gets this approved. Aim for a deal members can see is sound, with a process they trust. A two-thirds vote on a 4-3 board split will only pass if the three dissenters and the skeptical members feel heard and the terms actually address their worries. If you manage the vote instead, a narrow win will leave you with a split co-op for 20 years.

**Check these first, because they could sink the deal**

1. **The Vantongeren lease.** You said it is personal to the co-op. Handing operation of the hill to Alderline may count as an assignment, sublease or change of use. If it does, the family can refuse consent or reopen the rent, and the upper runs are probably half your terrain. Have a lawyer read the lease now. Then talk to the family before the AGM, and ideally get a signed consent or estoppel letter. They have never raised the rent, so treat them as a stakeholder and not a formality. Make family consent a condition precedent in the Alderline agreement.
2. **The 1987 debenture holders.** About 140 of your 410 voting members hold lifetime passes that they paid for. Moving them onto Alderline's pass product may breach the debenture terms or the co-op's bylaws, and it is the group most likely to vote no. Don't say it seems fair given how cheap the passes are. For them it is a loss of something they bought. Get legal advice on what the debentures actually promise. Then ask Alderline to honour lifetime access in writing, as a binding obligation that survives a sale of the operating agreement. A fallback is a negotiated buyout that members choose, not one imposed on them.
3. **Patrol and ski school.** About 60 volunteers get family passes, and you need certified patrollers on duty to open each day. Under a for-profit operator, the legal and insurance position of those volunteers may change. Ask Alderline to confirm in writing that the volunteer program continues, that the family passes continue, and who carries liability and insurance. If the volunteers walk, the hill can't open, whatever the contract says. Talk to patrol and ski school leaders early and ask what they would need.
4. **Dariusz and the guarantees.** Dariusz and two other directors personally guaranteed the $600k line. That is a real interest, and it makes the strongest advocate for the deal also the one who gains most from it, if the deal reduces the debt. Ask for two things. First, Dariusz and the other two guarantors should disclose the interest openly, and the board should decide whether they abstain or at least say so at the AGM. Second, negotiate how the guarantee gets released: does Alderline's fee or investment repay or refinance the line, and when? This is a legitimate ask, but members will discount the recommendation if the guarantors' interest comes out later.

**What to ask Alderline to change**

- **Term and exit.** Twenty years is long. Ask for performance milestones on the $4.2M (snowmaking and the quad, with dates), a cure period and termination rights if they miss them, and a termination right for the co-op after, say, year 10 or on a change of control. Include the condition of the assets on handback.
- **Fee.** Is the annual fee fixed, indexed to inflation, or tied to revenue? Ask for a floor plus a share of revenue, and for audit rights. Check whether the fee covers your fixed costs (insurance, inspections, the $11k lease).
- **Ownership and capital.** Confirm the co-op keeps title to the lifts and lodge, and settle who pays for maintenance and replacement, and who owns the new quad and snowmaking when the agreement ends.
- **Employees.** Keep all nine is a promise, so ask for a defined period (for example 3 years), the same terms, and a clause that binds Alderline's successors.
- **Pricing and community.** Cap increases on the member pass, guarantee local or member rates, and keep community days, a youth program and access to the lodge. This speaks directly to the club-feel objection.
- **Governance.** Give the co-op a seat on an advisory committee, consultation rights on major changes (hours, pricing, lodge use) and reserved matters it can veto. Ask for annual reporting to members.
- **Reputation and finance.** Ask for references from other hills Alderline operates, and for evidence of its financial strength. Consider a parent guarantee or a performance bond.

**How to get members on side**

- **Bring the three dissenters in.** Ask them to name their conditions and write them down. Some of those conditions are probably things you can negotiate (governance, pricing, protections). Have one or two of them join the negotiating group. If the dissenters help shape the deal, it is much harder to say it was pushed through.
- **Show the alternative.** Members will compare the deal with an imagined status quo. Lay out the finances plainly: three losing winters in five, the $600k line, what happens if you do nothing, and other options such as a smaller capital campaign, a different operator, or a government grant. If you can't show that the alternatives are worse, the vote will be hard.
- **Independent advice.** Commission a lawyer's and an accountant's review that members can read, and consider an independent summary of the offer. A recommendation from the board that includes guarantors will carry less weight than one from someone with no stake.
- **Meetings before the AGM.** Hold several sessions, including one for debenture holders and one for volunteers, with questions submitted in advance and the answers published. Send a plain-language summary with the full contract available to read.
- **Know the arithmetic.** Check the bylaws: two-thirds of votes cast, or of all 410 members? If it is of all members, you need about 274 yes votes, so turnout and proxies matter. Count where the votes are likely to come from, starting with the 140 debenture holders.
- **Be honest about what you don't know.** Where terms are still being negotiated, say so. If Alderline won't move on the debenture and lease points, that is important information for the board, and it may be a reason to delay the vote past March. A deadline from the buyer should not decide it. Ask Alderline whether the timetable is flexible.

**A sensible sequence**

1. Now: legal review of the lease, debentures and bylaws, and open the conversation with the Vantongeren family.
2. Next few weeks: meet with patrol and ski school leaders and with the dissenting directors, and settle the list of must-haves.
3. Negotiate with Alderline, with the must-haves as conditions, and get the key points in writing as amendments to the term sheet.
4. Publish the independent review and the comparison with alternatives. Hold the member sessions.
5. Final recommendation to the AGM. The board then tells members which of their concerns were met and which were not, and lets them decide.

Assumptions: I haven't seen the offer or your bylaws, so the legal points above are things to check with counsel and not conclusions.
