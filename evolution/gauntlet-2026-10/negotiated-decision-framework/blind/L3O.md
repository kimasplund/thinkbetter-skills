Congratulations on getting an offer this substantial. Before you build the AGM plan, I'd change the framing. At the moment the plan is "win the vote." The vote can be won, but only if the groups who can sink it are dealt with before March rather than persuaded at the meeting.

## The vote arithmetic

Two-thirds of 410 members is 274 yes votes. Check your bylaws on whether that means two-thirds of all members or two-thirds of those voting. Either way, the numbers are tight.

- The 140 debenture holders are 34% of the membership. If the threshold is measured against all members, the other 270 members can't reach 274 alone, so the debenture holders are effectively a veto. If it's measured against votes cast, they're still enough to block if they turn out in force.
- The board is split 4-3. A split recommendation tells members the board itself isn't sure. The three dissenters are the best predictor of how the floor will feel.
- "The new chair will win them over" doesn't work as a plan. The chair arrives after the vote, and the people worried about club feel are voting on the agreement, not on the chair.

## Where the real risks are

**1. The debenture holders.** They paid $500 in 1987 for lifetime passes. Moving them onto Alderline's season-pass product is, from their side, giving up something they own. You see the cheap passes as making the move fair, but they may see it as an extinguished right. Ask whether the lifetime passes are legally binding obligations, and get a lawyer to say so. Then ask Alderline to honor them: a lifetime pass for each existing holder, perhaps non-transferable, with a defined number of days or full access. Honoring about 140 passes costs Alderline little against $4.2M of investment, and it turns your largest at-risk bloc into people who are better off than today. If Alderline refuses, you've learned something important about the deal.

**2. Dariusz and the guarantees.** Dariusz and two other directors personally guaranteed the $600k line. That is a real, understandable worry, but it is also a personal financial interest in the outcome. Members who learn about it from someone else will take it as proof the board is motivated by self-protection. So:
- Disclose it yourselves, early and plainly, in the member package.
- Have the three guarantors consider not leading the advocacy, and think about whether they should abstain on the board recommendation (ask your lawyer about your bylaws and conflict rules).
- Better still, make releasing the guarantees an explicit term. Ask whether Alderline will pay off or refinance the line, or whether the co-op can restructure it, so the guarantors are released no matter how the vote goes. If the guarantors' exposure is resolved, their reasons to push drop, and the members' suspicion goes with it.

**3. The volunteers.** About 60 volunteers run patrol and ski school, and you can't open without certified patrollers. They are paid in family passes. Under a commercial operator, three things can happen: Alderline's insurer may require paid or contracted staff, volunteers may feel they are working free for a corporation, or the family passes may go away. Any of these could empty your patrol roster. This group has no formal vote beyond their membership, but they have a practical veto because the hill can't open without them. Ask Alderline to commit in writing to keep the volunteer patrol and ski school model (or a paid equivalent with transition support), to keep the family passes, and to name who is responsible for certification and liability. Talk to patrol leadership before March, not after the board announces.

**4. The Vantongeren lease.** The upper runs sit on a 1987 lease that you've paid $11k a year on, with a family that has never raised the rent. A 20-year operating agreement probably involves a third party using land you only lease. Check the lease for assignment, sublease and change-of-use terms and for its expiry date. If the family's consent is needed, get it. Don't let Alderline find out about this late. Beyond the legal point, this is a relationship you don't want to damage: if the family hears about the deal at the AGM, it could read as being cut out of something personal. Meet them first, show them the plan, and ask what would make them comfortable. A lease extension to match or exceed the 20 years would also protect Alderline's investment, so it's an easy thing to ask both parties to support.

**5. The three dissenting directors.** Their concern is club feel. That's a legitimate interest that can be written into the contract. Ask them directly what would have to be true for them to support it, and treat their answer as a list of terms to negotiate for.

## What to ask Alderline to change before March

Rank these by what they protect.

*Protecting the co-op's future*
- **Exit and termination rights.** What happens if Alderline underperforms, is sold, or walks away in year 8? Ask for clear performance standards, cure periods, and the right to terminate. Ask what state the hill must be in when it comes back to you.
- **Change of control.** Alderline shouldn't be able to assign the agreement to another operator without co-op consent.
- **Capital commitments in the contract.** $4.2M and the quad chair should be written in with dates, specs and penalties for not building, backed by a bond, guarantee, or escrow if possible, not a promise in a letter. Ask who owns and maintains the snowmaking infrastructure, and what happens to it at the end of the term.
- **Lifts and lodge maintenance.** You keep ownership, but who pays for upkeep and inspections? Make sure the co-op isn't left with obligations without the income.
- **Annual fee.** Is it fixed, indexed, or a share of revenue? A revenue-linked component with a floor protects you against a bad year.

*Protecting members and staff*
- Lifetime passes honored for the debenture holders.
- A pass-pricing guarantee for members, such as a cap on increases for the first several years, and a local or member rate.
- Volunteer patrol and ski school protected, as above.
- Employee commitment extended: "keep all nine" should say for how long, on what pay and terms, and what happens afterward.
- Governance: a co-op seat or advisory committee, notice requirements before major changes (hours, pricing, events), and a few reserved matters such as community days or access that need co-op consent.

*Protecting the transaction*
- Release of the personal guarantees (see above).
- A review point, say at year 5 and year 10, with defined remedies.

## Getting members on side

1. **Fix the substance first.** Present a deal that answers the debenture holders, volunteers, landowner and guarantor concerns, rather than a sales pitch for the current one. You will be much more persuasive saying "we asked for this and got it" than "trust us."
2. **Hold member meetings well before the AGM.** Have at least two information sessions and an FAQ. Make sure the three dissenting directors can voice their views at them, and let them present their concerns openly. A board that hides its dissent loses trust.
3. **Get the financial picture out.** Show what happens to the co-op if the vote fails: three losing years in five, the line of credit, the thin-snow exposure, what snowmaking would cost the co-op alone. Members need to see that "no" is also a choice with costs, and that you've looked at alternatives (other operators, a smaller capital program, a membership drive or levy). If you haven't tested alternatives, do that before March. A vote against a single offer feels very different from a vote between real choices.
4. **Get independent advice.** An independent lawyer and, ideally, a financial reviewer for the co-op, not Alderline's counsel, to examine the agreement and say so in the package. That's credible in a way your own advocacy can't be.
5. **Count votes.** Build a list of members, with the debenture holders, families of volunteers and the long-tenured members flagged. Reach all 410, and especially the ones who don't normally come. A two-thirds threshold means you need turnout. Check the rules on proxies and mail or online voting.
6. **Consider your own role.** As GM you're likely to be affected by this deal, and members know it. Present facts and let the board and independent advisers carry the recommendation. It protects both the vote and you.
7. **If the terms can't be fixed.** Ask whether a trial or phased structure could work: a shorter initial term, or a first phase that covers snowmaking and the chair, with the option to extend. A 20-year commitment is hard to unwind. If Alderline won't move on protections members care about, a 4-3 board and a 34% bloc of unhappy members suggest the vote may not pass anyway, and it's better to know that before March.

## Suggested sequence

- **Now to 8 weeks out:** legal review of the lease, bylaws, debenture terms and the draft agreement; conversations with the Vantongerens, patrol leadership, and debenture holders; open the negotiation list with Alderline.
- **6 to 8 weeks out:** Alderline's revised term sheet; board vote again on the improved terms.
- **4 to 6 weeks out:** member package with the agreement summary, the independent review, the guarantor disclosure and the cost of saying no.
- **2 to 4 weeks out:** two member sessions and targeted outreach.
- **AGM:** a clear presentation, time for the dissenting directors, and a vote the members trust.

If the debenture holders and the volunteers are brought on side with real commitments and the Vantongerens are consulted, you're well placed. If those groups are only addressed at the AGM, 274 votes is a hard number to reach.
