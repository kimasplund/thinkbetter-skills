Your doubts are well placed, and I'd treat them as an asset. The way to get this through in March, if it deserves to go through, is to change the deal until the people who would otherwise vote no can live with it. A sales campaign for the current draft is the wrong approach. Here is how I'd read the situation, then what to ask Alderline for, then how to run the next five months.

## Where the vote is really decided

You need two-thirds of 410 voting members. Check the bylaws on whether that means two-thirds of votes cast or of all members. If it is all members, you need 274 yes votes. A 4-3 board split is a weak base for that, so assume the vote is genuinely in play.

Three groups decide it, and none of them is the board.

**The roughly 140 debenture holders.** They paid $500 in 1987 for lifetime passes. "Move everyone onto Alderline's season pass" probably means those passes end or become something Alderline can reprice. That is about a third of your electorate, and the draft may be taking away the one concrete benefit they hold. They are also the people with the deepest attachment to the club. I'd expect this group to sink the deal unless it is fixed. Get a clear answer from a lawyer on what the debentures actually promise and whether the co-op can lawfully trade that away by a vote.

**The roughly 60 volunteers.** Patrol and ski school run on volunteers who get family passes. You can't open without certified patrollers on duty. If an operator takes over, volunteers may feel they are now working unpaid for a corporation. If the family passes change, they may just stop turning up. Many are probably voting members, and they hold the operational key to the hill. The draft needs to say plainly what happens to the volunteer program, the passes, and who carries liability and certification costs.

**The undecided middle.** They mostly care about three things. Will the hill stay open? Will the snow be better? Will my pass cost go up sharply? Pass prices are your weak point, because you've said yourself that yours are cheap. A pass that rises 60% is a bigger risk to this vote than anything in the operating agreement.

## Three things the draft may be hiding

**The Vantongeren lease.** The upper runs sit on their land under a 1987 lease with a handshake-level relationship behind it. A 20-year operating agreement where a corporation runs the hill may need the family's consent, or may let them reopen the terms. Nobody should assume a family that has never raised the rent will feel the same about a corporate operator. Talk to them now, before the board or Alderline does it for you. If they object or see leverage, you want to hear it in October, not February. Make the agreement conditional on their written consent, and ideally on a new long lease at the current rent.

**Dariusz.** He and two other directors personally co-signed the $600k line. That doesn't make his judgment wrong, since the debt is real. But he has a personal financial interest in a deal that would likely clear the line, and members will notice. Handle it openly. The co-signers should disclose the guarantee in the member package, and the deal should say what happens to the line. If Alderline's capital or the fee retires or refinances the line and releases the guarantors, say so as a term, and say it as a benefit to the co-op's risk, not as a favour to three directors. Be careful not to turn this into an attack on Dariusz. If he feels cornered, he will dig in harder, and he is your most committed advocate.

**Your own position.** You manage the hill, you have doubts, and the board wants you to run the campaign. Don't fake enthusiasm. Members will read it. Your credible role is the person who made sure the deal was tested properly and the members got the facts. That is a legitimate way to be "on side": you are for a good decision, and for a fair process. If you come to believe the deal can't be fixed, tell the board that in time for them to change course. A forced approval you privately doubt is worse for you than an honest no.

## What to ask Alderline to change

Put these in writing as a term sheet and make approval conditional on them. The first group matters most.

**Protect what members already hold:**
- Debenture holders keep lifetime access, written into the agreement, with no repricing and no conversion. If Alderline won't do that, ask for a defined, generous alternative such as a fixed lifetime pass at no charge, in exchange for a clear member vote on it.
- Member pass prices capped for the first several years, with increases tied to an agreed index. Publish the proposed pass prices before the AGM, not after.
- A written volunteer program: patrol and school stay volunteer-run unless the co-op agrees otherwise, volunteers keep their family passes, and Alderline takes responsibility for certification and liability cover. Check this with the patrol and school leads before you put it in front of Alderline, since they know what would make people walk.

**Make the deal safe to leave:**
- A real exit. Twenty years is a long time for a first-time partner. Ask for a shorter initial term, say 7 to 10 years, with renewal by mutual agreement, or at least termination rights for missed performance.
- Performance covenants. The $4.2M should have a schedule: snowmaking by a set winter, the quad by another, with penalties or termination if missed. Without that, the investment is a promise.
- Reversion. If Alderline leaves or is terminated, the snowmaking and the chair stay with the co-op at no cost, and the hill returns in working order.
- No sale or assignment of the agreement to a third party without co-op consent, and a change-of-control trigger if Alderline itself is bought.
- A floor on the annual fee, or a formula with a minimum. "An annual fee" is currently too vague to judge. Ask for the number, how it changes, and what happens in a bad snow year.

**Protect the people and the club feel:**
- Employee protection beyond "we'll keep all nine": a stated period, wage and role commitments, and what happens if they leave.
- A member advisory council with real consultation rights on pass products, events, hours and community use. Don't promise "club feel" in a brochure. Write down specific things such as member nights, local events, and the lodge staying open to the community.
- Co-op ownership of lifts and lodge stays as is, with clear maintenance and insurance responsibilities, so you aren't left holding the costs of an asset someone else is wearing out.

**Fix the lease and the debt:**
- Vantongeren consent and the continuation of the lease on its current terms.
- A clear treatment of the $600k line and the guarantors.

## What I'd do between now and March

1. **Get independent advice first.** A lawyer who has seen operating agreements, not only the co-op's usual one, and ideally someone who can model the finances. The board shouldn't rely on Alderline's numbers alone. You need to know what "lost money in three of five winters" costs in dollars, and what the co-op's realistic alternative is if the vote fails: smaller capital program, a loan for snowmaking, a different partner, selling debentures or memberships. The no side will point to alternatives, and the yes side needs an honest answer. If the realistic alternative is closing in a few years, say that, because it changes how members weigh the risk.

2. **Ask Alderline why March.** If their deadline is driving the schedule, test whether it is real. A rushed vote on a 20-year deal is how you get a 55% yes and a split club. If you need an extra AGM or a special meeting in the spring, ask for it.

3. **Meet the groups separately before any big meeting.** Debenture holders, patrol and school, the Vantongerens, the staff, and the three directors who voted no. Ask each: what would make this acceptable, and what would make you vote no? Take the answers back to Alderline as demands that came from the members. That is much stronger than the board's own wish list.

4. **Bring the three no-voting directors into the negotiation.** Give them a defined role in shaping the term sheet. Their concern about club feel is exactly what the advisory council and the member protections are for. If they feel the final deal reflects their input, some may move to yes or at least stop campaigning against it. If they still vote no, you want to have shown that you took them seriously.

5. **Publish plain facts.** A short member package with the term sheet, the fee, the pass prices, what changes for debenture holders and volunteers, how the guarantee is handled, and a candid list of risks. Hold several open sessions, including one at the lodge on a ski day. Let Alderline answer questions, but have a co-op chair run the room.

6. **Count votes honestly.** About six weeks out, estimate yes, no and unsure by group. If debenture holders and volunteers are still majority no, no messaging will fix it, and the right move is to go back to Alderline or pull the item.

## A realistic bottom line

If Alderline accepts most of the first two groups of changes, you have a credible deal that can win members who are anxious about the hill's future and not simply loyal to the status quo. If they refuse to protect the debenture lifetime passes, the volunteers, and an exit, you have your answer too, and you can take the board a clear reason for walking away or going back to the table. Either way, you will have given members a decision made on facts, and that is the best protection for the club, and for you, whichever way the vote goes.
