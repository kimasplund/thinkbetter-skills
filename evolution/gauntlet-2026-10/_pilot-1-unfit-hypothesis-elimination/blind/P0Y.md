**Short answer:** the most likely cause is the July 28 inline density meters on FV3 and FV4. They start the cold crash as soon as gravity hits target, which cuts off the time the yeast needs to reabsorb diacetyl. It isn't L22, and it isn't Pia. Don't spend the $1,800 yet.

**What the table shows**

All five buttery batches (2, 4, 7, 8, 11) ran on FV3 or FV4, and all had 9 to 10 days from pitch to crash. Every batch on FV1 or FV2 was clean, and each of those had 13 to 15 days. Both facts hold across brewers and yeast lots.

**Why the other suspects don't fit**

- **Yeast L22:** Batch 2 was buttery on the old lot, L19. Batches 3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 12 were clean on L22. If L22 were the problem, FV1 and FV2 batches would be buttery too.
- **Pia:** Her buttery batches (2, 4, 8) were all on FV3 or FV4. Her batches on FV1 and FV2 (6 and 10) were clean. Marta (batch 7) and Arlo (batch 11) also made buttery beer when they used the auto-crash tanks. The brewer pattern is a side effect of which tank was used. Pia happened to get FV3 and FV4 more often.
- **Mill gap:** It applies to every batch since July 20, and most batches were clean.
- **Peracetic sanitizer:** It applies to every batch after August 10, and most were clean. It also wouldn't explain a split by tank.
- **Infection and temperature:** Plates from batches 7 and 8 grew nothing. Temperature stayed within 0.5°C of setpoint on every tank. Neither fits a pattern tied to specific tanks.

**The test case: batch 9**

Batch 9 ran on FV3, but the controller was in manual mode for most of it. A person decided when to crash, it took 15 days, and the beer was clean. That is the same tank, the same controller hardware and the same yeast lot, with only the automatic crash removed. It points at the automatic crash trigger, not the tank.

**The mechanism**

Diacetyl is a normal by-product of fermentation. Yeast reabsorb it near the end of fermentation, which is why lagers usually get a diacetyl rest (warm hold) before the crash. Crashing at the first moment gravity reaches target stops that cleanup early. The FV1 and FV2 batches only crashed when someone took a hydrometer reading, so they got extra warm days by accident.

**Caveat:** In this data, tank, controller type and days-to-crash all move together. Batch 9 is the only one that separates them. Treat this as a strong lead, not proof.

**What to check, cheapest first**

1. **Force-test the beer now.** Take a sample from a buttery batch, warm it to about 60°C for 15 to 30 minutes, cool it and taste it. A forced diacetyl test shows whether the beer has precursor that more time would clear. It costs nothing.
2. **Change the process on FV3 and FV4.** Have the controller hold at fermentation temperature, or raise it a few degrees, for 2 to 3 days after target gravity, and only then crash. If the controller can't do that, put it in manual mode and crash by hand after a diacetyl check.
3. **Brew the next two or three batches this way** and see whether they come out clean. As a control, run one batch on FV3 or FV4 with the old auto-crash behavior if you can accept the risk.
4. **Check the controller settings.** Confirm that the firmware update from batch 9 didn't change the setpoints. Also check whether the crash trigger is based on gravity alone.
5. **Look at the dates.** I don't know when batches 1 through 4 were brewed relative to the August 10 sanitizer change. If any buttery batches came before it, that is further evidence against the sanitizer.
6. **Only if the beer is still buttery after longer rests**, test the yeast, for example with a lab viability check and a split trial of L19 against L22.

**For the head brewer and Pia**

The data doesn't support dumping L22, and it clears Pia. The buttery batches follow the tank and how long they sat before the crash, not the person. A quick conversation about the rest period will likely settle it.
