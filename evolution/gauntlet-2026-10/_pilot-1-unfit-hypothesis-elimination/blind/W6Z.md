**Short answer:** the most likely cause is a hydraulic problem on the Ardent Avenue feed to Linden Heights, probably a valve that wasn't fully reopened after the Sept 4 repair. It is not Well 7, and it is probably not the Hollis Park flushing. I'd treat this as a strong lead to confirm in the field before Thursday, not yet a proven cause.

## Why the evidence points there

**The water is carrying iron, not manganese.**
- Complaint samples show iron at 0.6 to 1.1 mg/L, against a normal level under 0.1.
- Manganese is only 0.02 to 0.03 mg/L.
- Well 7's iron is 0.04 mg/L, so it cannot be the source of the iron. Its manganese is high in the raw water, but plant effluent manganese is unchanged at 0.010 to 0.014 mg/L. The treatment plant is removing it.
- The slight manganese rise in the complaint samples fits what you'd expect when old cast-iron pipe scale is disturbed. That scale carries some manganese along with the iron.

**The pressure drop is the key clue.**
- Linden Heights fell from 60-64 psi in June to 49-53 psi on Sept 15, a loss of roughly 10 psi.
- Hollis Park is unchanged at 71 psi.
- A drop in one zone only means something is restricting the supply to that zone. Well 7 adds supply and would not cause it. Flushing in Hollis Park would not either, and it ended Sept 12.

**The timing fits the main repair.**
- The Ardent Avenue main is the 12-inch line between the Calder Road tank and Linden Heights.
- The repair was Sept 4 and complaints started Sept 5.
- Well 7 came online Sept 2 and the first complaints came three days later, so Well 7 fits the timing worse.

**The geography fits.** 54 of 63 complaints are in Linden Heights, which is mostly 1950s unlined cast iron. That pipe holds heavy iron deposits (tubercles) that release when flow speed or direction changes. If a valve is partly closed, flow is forced through other, smaller paths at higher velocity, and that scours the deposits loose. Worst-in-the-morning also fits, because morning demand surges are when the velocity changes are largest.

## How the other explanations hold up

| Explanation | What fits | What doesn't | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well 7 manganese | Timing is near (Sept 2). | Complaint water is high in iron, not manganese. Well 7's iron is low. Plant effluent manganese is unchanged. It does not explain the Linden Heights pressure drop. | Unlikely as the main cause |
| Hollis Park flushing stirred up sediment that traveled | Plausible for some Hollis Park complaints. | Complaints began Sept 5, four days before flushing started on Sept 9. Hollis Park has only 6 complaints, and its pressure is normal. Linden Heights is the problem zone. | Unlikely, at most a minor contributor |
| Lead service line replacement in Hollis Park | Local construction disturbance can cause brief discoloration. | It could explain some of the 6 Hollis Park complaints, but not 54 in Linden Heights. | Minor, local only |
| Valve or flow restriction after the Ardent Avenue repair | Explains the Linden Heights-only pressure loss, the Sept 5 start, the iron signature and the old pipe. | Not yet physically confirmed. | Leading explanation |
| Something else that reduces Linden Heights pressure (a second leak or break, a closed valve elsewhere, low level at the Calder Road tank) | It would produce a similar picture. | No direct evidence yet. | Keep open until the field check is done |

Well 7 may still play a smaller part. A new 15% source can shift flow directions in the network, and that can stir up deposits. That is a hydraulic effect, though, not a water chemistry effect. Taking Well 7 offline is not supported by the data, and it would cost you 15% of system supply while the discoloration continues.

## What to do before Thursday

1. **Check all four valves used on Ardent Avenue.** Confirm each one is fully open by counting turns against the valve record. A partly open valve is the first thing to rule out. Do this in the next day or two.
2. **Look at the pressure data around Sept 4-5.** If you have SCADA or logger data, find out whether Linden Heights pressure dropped suddenly then. A step change on Sept 4 or 5 would confirm the lead.
3. **Check the Calder Road tank levels and any other valve work or leaks** in the Linden Heights feed. This covers the other possibilities.
4. **If a valve is partly closed, open it slowly.** Opening it fast can cause a surge and a fresh wave of discolored water. After it is open, flush Linden Heights in a planned sequence. Then retake hydrant pressures.
5. **Test the explanation.** If it's correct, pressure in Linden Heights should recover toward 60 psi and complaints should fall over the next one to two weeks. If pressure doesn't recover with all valves open, the restriction is elsewhere and the search continues.

## What I'd tell the council

- The discoloration is iron from old cast-iron pipes in Linden Heights, not a contamination event. Chlorine residual is normal and the treatment plant output is unchanged.
- The pattern points to a change in flow and pressure in Linden Heights after the Sept 4 main repair. The leading suspect is a valve that was not fully reopened. Crews are checking it.
- Well 7 and the Hollis Park work were looked at carefully. The test results do not support either as the main cause, so there's no need to take a source offline.
- Say plainly that the field check is still to be completed, and that you will report the result. If the valve check finds nothing, say that the investigation continues and that you will return with the next findings.
- On health: iron and manganese at these levels are mainly an aesthetic issue (color, staining, taste). Check your state's secondary standards and your own notification obligations before you say more than that. Iron at 0.6 to 1.1 mg/L is above the usual 0.3 mg/L secondary guideline, so say that openly.

If the valve check is done before the meeting, lead with that result. A confirmed and corrected cause is a much stronger message than a likely one.
