Short version: it's not a ghost, it isn't Pia, and it very probably isn't the yeast lot. Don't spend the $1,800 yet. Your own table points at the new auto-crash on FV3 and FV4, which cuts the beer's finishing time short.

**What the table shows**

Sort your 12 batches by days from pitch to crash:

- 9 to 10 days: batches 2, 4, 7, 8, 11. All five are buttery.
- 13 to 15 days: batches 1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12. All seven are clean.

That is a clean split with no exceptions, and no other column does that.

The tanks line up too:

- Every buttery batch was in FV3 or FV4, the two tanks with the inline density meters that start the crash on their own when gravity hits target.
- FV1 and FV2 are crashed by hand after a hydrometer reading. All six batches in those tanks were clean, and they ran 13 to 15 days.
- Batch 9 is your natural experiment. FV3's controller was in manual for most of that batch, so a person decided when to crash. It ran 15 days and came out clean. It is the only FV3 or FV4 batch that was clean, and it is the one where the automation was off.

**Why the mechanism makes sense**

Diacetyl is made during fermentation. Healthy yeast then reabsorbs and reduces it, but only while the beer is still warm and the yeast is active. A controller that crashes the moment gravity reaches target ends that cleanup window early. Hitting target gravity doesn't mean the diacetyl has been cleaned up. Your hydrometer-and-human routine was accidentally adding a few days of warm rest, and the automation removed it. The timing fits as well. The meters went in July 28, and the first problem batch you list is batch 2, right after that.

**Why the other suspects don't hold up**

- **Yeast lot L22.** Batch 1 was on L19 and was clean. Batch 2 was also on L19 and buttery. L22 has 7 clean batches (3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12 and others in the 13+ day group) and 4 buttery ones. If L22 were the cause, the clean L22 batches would need explaining, and they are explained by tank and timing, not by lot. The one caveat is that a weak lot could make the problem worse in a short-rest tank. But the lot is not the primary driver, and a lab switch wouldn't fix FV3 and FV4.
- **Pia.** She brewed 3 of the 5 buttery batches, but she also brewed 2 clean ones (6 and 10), and those were in FV2 and FV1. Her buttery batches were in FV3 and FV4. She was simply scheduled into the auto-crash tanks more often (4 of her 5 batches), while Arlo and Marta got them 2 and 1 times out of 4 and 3. Marta's batch 7 in FV3 and Arlo's batch 11 in FV4 were buttery too. Same tank, same result, different brewer. Please tell her that directly.
- **Mill gap.** Applied to every batch since July 20, clean and buttery alike, so it can't separate them.
- **New peracetic sanitizer (Aug 10).** You didn't list dates per batch, so check which batches came before and after it. Batch 2 was buttery, and if it brewed before Aug 10, the sanitizer can't explain it. Even if it overlaps, it should hit FV1 and FV2 as well, and they're clean.
- **Heat and chiller.** Tank temperatures stayed within 0.5°C of setpoint, and it's not tank-specific.
- **Infection.** Plating batches 7 and 8 found nothing. That doesn't rule out a bug, but an infection that only shows up in two tanks and not in batch 9 in FV3 would be a strange coincidence.

**Be skeptical of me too**

- Twelve batches is a small sample. Tank, auto-crash and short time are fully tangled together in your data. I can't separate "the controller crashes too early" from "something specific about FV3 and FV4", such as sampling, a fitting, or a cleaning problem. Batch 9 is one batch.
- Rennick is right about one thing. The panel could be biased once they expect diacetyl. The tank pattern, though, is the sort of thing bias doesn't create by itself, because the tasters didn't know which tank had the meter. If they were blind to tank and batch, you have strong evidence. If not, run a blind retest.

**What to do before spending anything (all roughly free)**

1. Change the FV3 and FV4 controllers so they hold at fermentation temperature for 2 to 3 days after gravity target, then crash. Most controllers let you add a delay or a diacetyl rest step. Or put them back in manual for the next two Copperline batches and crash after 13 or more days.
2. Run a forced diacetyl test on the next FV3 or FV4 batch right before the crash. Warm a sample to about 60°C for 15 to 30 minutes, cool it, and smell it against an unheated sample. If it stinks only after heating, the beer still has precursor left and wasn't ready to crash.
3. Take a hydrometer reading at the moment the controller crashes. Check that the density meter's target is what you think it is. A meter that's reading slightly high would crash early.
4. Pull the Aug 10 date and match it to each batch, so the sanitizer is formally excluded.
5. Have the panel taste the next batches blind, with a mix of tanks.

**What would change my mind**

- If FV3 or FV4 stays buttery after a proper rest, with the crash delayed to 13 or more days, then look at the tank, its fittings, and the sanitizer.
- If a 14-day batch in FV1 or FV2 comes out buttery, the days theory is wrong.
- If both of those pass, then yeast L22 is the next thing to test, using a side-by-side batch on L19. Only then would the $1,800 make sense.

My confidence is around 80 percent that early crashing on FV3 and FV4 is the cause. That's high enough to try the free fix first, and not high enough to say the yeast is cleared until you've run it.
