**Short answer:** Don't dump L22 yet, and it isn't Pia. Your data points to the automatic cold crash on FV3 and FV4. Those controllers start the crash the moment gravity hits target. That cuts the warm period short, and the yeast never gets time to reabsorb diacetyl. Your own table fits this, and it can be checked for almost nothing before you spend $1,800.

**The pattern in the table**

Every buttery batch (2, 4, 7, 8, 11) was on FV3 or FV4, and every one was crashed at 9 to 10 days. Every batch that took 13 days or longer was clean, whoever brewed it, whatever the tank, whatever the yeast. The split is perfect: 9 to 10 days is buttery, 13 to 15 days is clean.

Diacetyl is a normal by-product of fermentation. Healthy yeast cleans it up at the end of fermentation, if the beer stays warm long enough after gravity is reached. A crash that starts at target gravity chills the yeast before that cleanup finishes. That would give you buttery beer with a clean plate count and in-spec temperature logs, which is what you have.

**The key batch is batch 9**

FV3's controller was in manual mode for most of batch 9, so a person decided when to crash. It ran 15 days and came out clean, in the same tank that gave buttery batches 2 and 7. That points at the controller's automatic crash, not the tank or the people. It is a single batch, so it is strong evidence but not proof.

**What to check before spending anything**

1. **Look at the FV3/FV4 controller settings.** Find what triggers the crash and whether there is any hold or diacetyl rest after target gravity. My expectation is that there is none.
2. **Run a forced diacetyl test.** Take a sample from the next FV3 or FV4 batch at the moment the crash begins. Warm it to about 60°C for 15 to 30 minutes in a sealed container and compare it against a chilled control. If the heated sample tastes buttery, the beer still has diacetyl precursors and needed more time. Repeat it on an FV1 or FV2 sample at crash for comparison.
3. **Test the fix on the next FV3 or FV4 batch.** Add a hold of 2 to 3 days at fermentation temperature, or raised to about 18 to 20°C, after target gravity, before the crash starts. Or switch the controller to manual and crash only after the panel or a forced test says it's clean. That takes minutes to try and costs nothing.
4. **Do one cross-check.** Brew a batch on L22 in FV1 or FV2 with the usual 14 days. This is just routine, since your recent FV1 and FV2 batches already show L22 working.

**What would change my mind**

If the longer hold on FV3/FV4 still gives buttery beer, I'd reopen the other candidates. Look at the yeast lot first, then sanitizer residue, then a pitch-rate or oxygenation difference on those tanks. If a batch crashed early by hand in FV1 or FV2 also comes out buttery, that confirms the timing explanation even more strongly.

**Confidence:** about 80%. The pattern is clean and the mechanism is well known. But there are only 12 batches, and one batch (9) carries much of the separation between tank and crash timing. Keep the yeast order on hold until the longer hold has been tried.
