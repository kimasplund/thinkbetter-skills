**Short answer:** this looks like pseudohyperkalemia from delayed transport of unspun, refrigerated blood at Ridgeway and Millbrook. Neither the Analyzer A lot nor Tomas explains your numbers. I'd bring that to Friday, along with a few quick checks that would confirm it.

## What the numbers say

**The excess is almost entirely at two sites.** Ridgeway and Millbrook send 30% of volume and produce 83% of the high results. If the overall rate is 6%, their own rate works out to about 16-17%. The other 70% of volume produces about 1.5%, which is your old baseline. So the rest of the network hasn't changed, and the problem is local to those two clinics.

**What those two clinics share is the courier change on September 7.** They went from a 90-minute loop to a single 3:30 pm pickup. Harbour is on the same contractor, but it spins samples on site, and its rate is at baseline. The difference between Harbour and the other two is whether the sample gets centrifuged before it waits.

**The time-of-draw split fits a delay effect.** At those clinics, 11% of samples drawn before 11 am flag high, against about 1% of those drawn after 1 pm. Morning samples wait four to seven hours before pickup, and afternoon samples wait about two hours or less. A dose-response pattern like this is hard to explain with an analyzer or a person. It is what a delay effect looks like.

**The mechanism is the cold-hold policy combined with the long wait.** Whole blood kept in a fridge has its cell membrane pumps (Na/K-ATPase) shut down, so potassium leaks out of the red cells into the serum. That is a well-known pre-analytical error. The August 1 policy was harmless while pickups were every 90 minutes. Since September 7, the fridge hold has become a multi-hour hold, and that is when the rise began. That also explains why the hemolysis index is normal in 93% of flagged samples: this leakage is not hemolysis. Normal calcium and sodium argue against EDTA carry-over from a wrong order of draw.

**The two ED patients are consistent with this.** Both had normal potassium on repeat, which is what you'd expect from a false result.

## Why not the two proposed fixes

| Candidate | What argues against it |
|---|---|
| Analyzer A reagent lot | The QC shift was +0.08 mmol/L, which is too small to move 1.5% to 6%. Analyzer A runs about 60% of volume and 57% of flagged samples, so flagged samples are not over-represented there. A lot problem would also not be limited to two clinics or to morning draws. |
| Tomas / 23-gauge butterfly needles | He works at Ridgeway only, but Millbrook has the same pattern. Difficult draws and small-bore needles usually raise hemolysis, and yours is normal. This would not produce a morning-versus-afternoon gap either, unless he only works mornings (worth checking). Fist-clenching and tourniquet time can raise potassium without hemolysis, so a small contribution is possible. It can't account for the whole pattern. |
| Cold-hold policy alone | The policy has been in place since August 1, and the rise began September 8. The policy matters only in combination with the longer wait. |

Retraining Tomas is reasonable as a general technique review, but it should not be presented as the fix. Quarantining the lot would take out 60% of your capacity and would not change the outcome.

## What I'd bring to Friday

1. **The site and time breakdown above**, shown as a chart of flag rate by clinic and by draw time.
2. **Collection-to-receipt and collection-to-centrifuge times** for flagged and unflagged samples at Ridgeway and Millbrook. I assumed these delays from the pickup schedule and haven't seen the actual figures, so this is the key data to pull. I'd expect flagged samples to have much longer times.
3. **A quick confirmation test.** For about 20 patients at Ridgeway or Millbrook, draw paired tubes. Centrifuge one within 30 minutes, with no fridge. Send the other by the usual route. I'd expect the delayed tubes to run higher by 0.5 to 1+ mmol/L. If it's quick to do, also rerun a few flagged samples on Analyzer B to clear the lot completely.
4. **Look at the other flagged samples.** Did any flagged samples come from a clinic or time window outside this pattern? If so, they need a separate explanation.

## Actions I'd recommend now, before the meeting

- **Stop refrigerating unspun potassium specimens.** If whole blood must wait, room temperature is better than a fridge, with a time limit. Check this against your analyte stability requirements.
- **Centrifuge on site at Ridgeway and Millbrook** before the courier pickup, as Harbour already does. This is the most direct fix, and it has a working example in your own network.
- **Reinstate a mid-day pickup** with the contractor, or restore the old loop for those two clinics.
- **Lookback.** Review all outpatient potassium results above 5.5 from these two clinics since September 8. Tell the ordering clinicians that many are probably spurious, so recalls and ED referrals can stop. Consider a comment on future results from these sites until the fix is in.
- **Keep the reagent lot under watch,** but leave it in use. Continue QC and compare it against the previous lot if you want a clean answer.

## Confidence

I'm fairly confident (roughly 80-85%) in the delay-plus-cold explanation, based on the site pattern, the Harbour contrast, and the draw-time gradient. It would move to about 95% if the paired-tube test shows the expected difference. The main thing I can't see from here is the actual transport times, so confirm those first.
