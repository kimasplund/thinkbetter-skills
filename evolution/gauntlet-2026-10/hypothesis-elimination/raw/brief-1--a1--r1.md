**Short answer:** The pattern fits pseudohyperkalemia from delayed separation of chilled specimens. It does not fit the reagent lot or Tomas's technique. The trigger is the September 7 courier change, working together with the August 1 cold-hold policy. I'd hold off on quarantining the lot and on retraining Tomas until you've run a quick test or two.

**Why I think it's the courier plus the fridge**

1. **Timing and place.** The rise began around Sept 8, the day after the courier change. Ridgeway and Millbrook are the two sites that went from a 90-minute loop to one 3:30 pm pickup. They send about 30% of outpatient volume but account for 83% of the high results.
2. **Harbour is the control.** It uses the same contractor but spins samples on site before pickup, and it is normal (5% of flags). The difference between it and the other two sites is whether the sample is separated before the wait.
3. **Time of draw.** At Ridgeway and Millbrook, 11% of samples drawn before 11 am flag high, against about 1% drawn after 1 pm. Morning samples wait the longest, roughly 4 to 7 hours, before they get to the lab. Afternoon samples barely wait. A reagent or a phlebotomist wouldn't care what time of day the draw was.
4. **Why the fridge matters.** Cold slows the Na/K-ATPase pump, so potassium leaks out of red cells into the unspun serum or plasma. Before September, the 90-minute loop meant the cold-hold policy did little harm. A long cold wait is what produces the leak. This would explain why nothing changed on August 1.
5. **Normal hemolysis, calcium and sodium.** Cold-induced leakage happens without hemolysis, so a normal hemolysis index (93%) is expected. Hemolysis would have been the signature of a bad draw.
6. **Inpatient and ED flag rates are unchanged.** Those samples reach the lab quickly.
7. **Both ED patients had normal potassium on repeat,** which is consistent with a spurious result in the original sample.

**Why the other two candidates are weak**

- **Reagent lot (Sept 4).** The QC mean shifted +0.08 mmol/L. That is small and cannot move a result from about 5.0 to above 5.5 for a whole group of patients. Analyzer A runs about 60% of volume and 57% of flags, which is proportional, so there is no enrichment on that analyzer. The lot also can't explain why flags are concentrated at two sites and in morning draws. The shift is worth monitoring, but it is not the cause.
- **Tomas and the 23G butterfly.** Difficult draws can raise potassium, but they usually raise the hemolysis index too, and that is normal here. The rise also appears at Millbrook, where Tomas doesn't work, and it follows time of day rather than who drew the sample. Retraining him won't fix this. It may be a minor contributor at Ridgeway. Check by comparing his flag rate to the other Ridgeway phlebotomists within the same draw-time bands.

**Caveat:** I don't have the data behind these numbers. The two other clinics account for 12% of flags, and I can't tell whether that is proportional to their volume or whether they also wait for pickup. Check that, because it tests the explanation.

**What to bring on Friday**

1. **Hypothesis and evidence.** A one-slide summary of the points above. Include the flag rate by site and draw hour, before and after September 7.
2. **Check that confirms or rejects it, this week.**
   - Pull the collection time and the lab receipt time for flagged and non-flagged samples. If the explanation is right, the flags will cluster at long intervals (more than 2 hours).
   - Paired recollection: at Ridgeway or Millbrook, draw two tubes from the same patient and spin one on site immediately. Send the other through the normal courier and fridge route. Compare the two. If the sent tube is higher by 0.5 mmol/L or more, you have the proof.
   - Check whether the two other clinics are on the same pickup schedule and what their flag rates by draw time look like.
   - Ask whether the Analyzer A flagged samples are the same ones that came from the delayed sites. If you rerun a sample of flagged specimens on Analyzer B, you can rule the lot in or out directly.
3. **Immediate actions (no need to wait for proof).**
   - Spin and separate on site at Ridgeway and Millbrook, as Harbour does, or restore a mid-day courier pickup. Do this now.
   - Revise the cold-hold policy. For potassium, whole blood should not be chilled. Room temperature is better, and unspun samples should be separated within 2 hours. Check the policy with your tube and analyte stability references.
   - Add a courier contract requirement with a maximum transit time.
4. **Patients.** Review all outpatient potassium results above 5.5 from Sept 8 onward, especially those from Ridgeway and Millbrook, and look for repeat results. Tell clinicians that results from these sites since Sept 8 may be falsely high, so they can stop recalling patients on the basis of one result. Consider an advisory comment on affected reports. Document the two ED visits as potential patient-impact events and handle them under your incident process.
5. **Lot and Tomas.** Tell your director that quarantining the lot would disrupt 60% of volume and probably won't change the flag rate. Offer a cheaper check: run retained flagged samples on both lots or both analyzers. Tell Ridgeway that you'll look at his data within the same draw-time bands, but that retraining shouldn't be the response until you've done that.

**Bottom line:** Fix the sample pathway (separate before the wait, stop chilling unspun blood), confirm with the paired recollection, and notify clinicians about the possibly spurious results. That is more likely to fix the rate than quarantining the lot or retraining.
