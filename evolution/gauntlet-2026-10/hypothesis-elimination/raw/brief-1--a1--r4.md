**Short answer:** This is most likely pseudohyperkalemia. Unspun samples from Ridgeway and Millbrook now sit for hours, partly in a fridge, before they reach the lab. Neither the Analyzer A lot nor Tomas explains the pattern. I'd hold off quarantining the lot and retraining Tomas until you've tested this.

**Why the courier change plus cold-hold fits best**

- **Timing.** The courier contractor started September 7 and the rise began September 8. The other changes don't line up as well: the fridge policy dates from August 1, Tomas started September 1, and the reagent lot changed September 4.
- **Location.** Ridgeway and Millbrook account for 83% of high results from about 30% of volume. They are the two sites that went from a 90-minute loop to one 3:30 pm pickup. Harbour uses the same contractor but spins samples on site, and its rate is normal (5% of high results). That is a strong natural control. The courier itself isn't the problem. Unspun whole blood waiting for transport is.
- **Time of day.** At those two sites, 11% of samples drawn before 11 am flag high, against about 1% of those drawn after 1 pm. Morning samples wait the longest for the 3:30 pm pickup, which is a dose-response pattern.
- **Mechanism.** Cells in unspun blood leak potassium when they are kept cold, because the Na/K-ATPase pump stops working in the fridge. The August 1 policy sends any specimen waiting more than 30 minutes to the fridge. With a 90-minute loop that meant a short cold exposure. With a 3:30 pm pickup it can mean 4 to 6 hours or more. The two changes interact: each was tolerable alone, and together they aren't. This also explains why the fridge policy didn't cause a problem for five weeks.
- **Inpatient and ED.** Their samples reach the lab quickly, and their flag rates haven't moved.
- **Both ED patients had normal potassium on repeat.** That fits a spurious result.

**Why the other explanations fit worse**

- **Analyzer A lot.** The QC mean shifted by +0.08 mmol/L. That is too small to turn 1.5% into 6% on its own. Analyzer A runs about 60% of volume and 57% of flagged samples, so it is not over-represented. The lot also can't explain the site and time-of-day pattern. It may add a small bias, so check it, but it isn't the main cause.
- **Tomas and difficult draws.** Traumatic draws and small needles cause hemolysis, and the hemolysis index is normal on 93% of flagged samples. Calcium and sodium are normal. Tomas is at one site only, so he can't explain Millbrook, and he can't explain the morning versus afternoon gap. Retraining him is not the fix, and acting on this now could unfairly blame a new employee.
- **Hemolysis.** Hemolysis can't account for most flagged results, given the normal index. Note that leakage from cold-stored cells usually doesn't raise the hemolysis index, so a normal index doesn't rule out pseudohyperkalemia.

**What to bring to Friday, in order**

1. **A quick test of the hypothesis. This is the most important item.** Pull collection time and receipt/centrifuge time for outpatient potassium results since August. Compare flagged and unflagged samples. I'd expect flagged ones to have delays of more than 3 to 4 hours. If you can also see which samples were refrigerated, add that.
2. **Rate by delay bucket**, split by site. Plot the flag rate against hours from collection to spin. You should see a clear gradient, and Harbour should stay flat.
3. **A paired check.** Over a few days, draw two tubes from the same Ridgeway or Millbrook patient. Spin one immediately on site, and send the other by the usual route. Compare potassium. If the transported tube is consistently higher, the hypothesis is confirmed. Also run a few of the same samples on Analyzer A and the other analyzer to rule out the lot.
4. **Rerun check on the lot.** Run retained samples on both analyzers and look for a systematic bias of about 0.1 mmol/L. This answers the director's question with data and costs little.
5. **Patient-safety actions now**, while you confirm:
   - Stop refrigerating potassium specimens. Cold storage is the wrong condition for potassium, and the fridge policy needs a carve-out. Keep samples at room temperature, or better, spin them on site.
   - Have Ridgeway and Millbrook centrifuge on site before pickup, as Harbour does. Alternatively, restore a shorter courier loop. Whole blood should be spun within about 1 to 2 hours of collection.
   - Add a comment to outpatient potassium reports above 5.5 mmol/L from unspun transported samples, or flag them for a repeat. Tell clinicians that results since September 8 from these sites may be spuriously high, and ask them to repeat before acting on them.
   - Review the recalled patients from the affected period, and consider an incident report for the two ED visits.
6. **Process points.** Courier SLA and pickup times should be reviewed with the contractor, and the contract change should have gone through a specimen-stability check. Pre-analytical delay should be monitored as a standing metric.

**Assumptions.** I don't have collection and receipt timestamps, whether the clinics hold samples in the fridge, or the exact volume split for the other two clinics. If the delay data don't show the gradient, I'd move next to the reagent lot and the Ridgeway draw technique.

**For the director and Ridgeway.** Quarantining the lot would cost you capacity on the analyzer that runs 60% of volume, and I expect it would not change the flag rate. Retraining Tomas is unlikely to help either. The evidence points to the transport delay combined with refrigeration, and that is something you can test this week.
