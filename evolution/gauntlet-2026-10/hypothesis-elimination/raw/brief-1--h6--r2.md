**Short answer:** The most likely cause is pseudohyperkalemia from specimen handling. Whole blood from Ridgeway and Millbrook is now sitting unspun for hours, and much of that time it is refrigerated. Neither the Analyzer A lot nor Tomas explains your pattern. I'd bring the courier change to Friday as the lead item, with a quick test that can confirm it before the meeting.

## Why I think it's pre-analytical

**Mechanism.** Potassium leaks out of red cells when unseparated blood is cold, because the Na/K pump stops working. It also leaks with long contact time before centrifugation. This gives falsely high potassium with a normal hemolysis index, normal calcium and normal sodium, and normal values on a fresh repeat. That matches what you describe, including the two ED patients who had normal potassium on repeat.

**Timing.** The cold-hold policy began August 1 and caused no problem for five weeks, because your own driver's 90-minute loop kept total hold time short. The contractor change on September 7 moved Ridgeway and Millbrook to one pickup a day at 3:30 pm. Your flag rate rose on September 8. Cold storage was already in place, and the long wait is what changed.

**Time of day.** At those two sites, 11% of samples drawn before 11 am flag high, against about 1% of those drawn after 1 pm. Morning samples wait roughly 4 to 7 hours for the 3:30 pickup, and afternoon samples wait much less. The dose-response by draw time is hard to explain with an analyzer or a phlebotomist, and it is what delay-plus-cold predicts.

**Site.** Harbour uses the same contractor but spins samples on site before pickup, and it is essentially unaffected (5% of the excess). Ridgeway and Millbrook make up 83% of the high results from 30% of the volume. Inpatient and ED samples reach the lab quickly and have not moved.

## Confirm it before Friday

1. **Transit times.** Pull collection time and lab receipt time (and centrifuge time if you have it) for flagged and unflagged outpatient potassium since Sept 8. If the cause is delay, flagged samples will cluster at the longest intervals, and the flag rate will rise steadily with hold time across all sites. This is the single most informative check.
2. **Split-sample test.** Take fresh outpatient blood and spin half within 30 minutes. Hold the other half unspun in the fridge for 5 to 6 hours, then spin and run both. Do the same with a room-temperature hold if you can. A 0.5 to 1+ mmol/L rise in the cold-held aliquot settles the question.
3. **Analyzer check.** Rerun a few dozen flagged retained samples on Analyzer B. If the values come back similar, the reagent lot is cleared. If you want, check lot against analyzer for the recent flag rate. I expect no difference.
4. **Recollects.** Count how many flagged patients had a fast repeat that was normal, and compare against the 5.5 threshold.

## What to do now

- **Fix the handling.** The best fix is to centrifuge samples on site at Ridgeway and Millbrook (gel-separator tubes) before pickup, as Harbour does. Alternatively, add a midday courier run, or return to the earlier loop. Please check with the contractor about which is feasible.
- **Revisit the cold-hold rule.** Refrigeration of unspun whole blood is the wrong choice for potassium. Samples that can't be spun should be kept at room temperature for a defined, short window, with an exception for analytes that need cold. This rule is fine when transit is 90 minutes, but unsafe with a 6-hour wait.
- **Interim lab safeguard.** For outpatient potassium above 5.5 from affected sites, add a comment such as "possible delayed-separation artifact; consider prompt redraw and immediate processing", and give clinicians a path to a quick recollect before an ED referral.
- **Look back.** List patients flagged since Sept 8 who were recalled, treated or sent to the ED without a normal repeat. Tell their clinicians about the artifact. Also check for any patient who was given potassium-lowering treatment based on a false result.

## What to tell the director and Ridgeway

- Quarantining the Analyzer A lot would disrupt 60% of your volume and fix nothing, since the data don't point to the lot. Keep monitoring QC, and have the reagent vendor assess the small QC shift as routine, not as the cause.
- Retraining Tomas is not the fix. If Ridgeway wants to review its draw technique, that is reasonable good practice, but it should not be presented as the explanation.

**Bottom line for Friday:** the September 7 courier change, combined with the August cold-hold policy, is probably producing false hyperkalemia in morning-drawn samples. The evidence is the draw-time pattern, the clinic pattern (with Harbour as a natural control), the normal hemolysis index, and the normal repeat tests.
