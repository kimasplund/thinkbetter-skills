**Short answer: this is real, it is not the reagent lot, and it is not Tomas. The best-supported cause is the courier change combined with the August fridge policy. Whole blood now sits unspun for hours, cold, and potassium leaks out of the cells. This is pseudohyperkalemia. Ansel is wrong that it is noise, and so are the two fixes your director and Ridgeway are proposing.**

## Why this is not noise

- The rate went from 1.5% to 6% and stayed there for three weeks. That is a step change with a start date.
- The excess is concentrated. Ridgeway and Millbrook send about 30% of volume and produce 83% of the flagged samples. That works out to roughly 16-17% of their outpatient samples flagging. The other 70% of volume produces 17% of the flags, which is about 1.4% of samples, which is your old baseline. **The other three sites haven't moved.** All of the excess is at two sites.
- Two patients went to the ED for a potassium that wasn't real. That is already patient harm, whatever Ansel thinks of the flag rate.
- Inpatient and ED rates being flat is not reassuring. It points to the cause. Those samples reach the lab in minutes.

## What fits the data

| Observation | Delay-plus-cold explanation |
|---|---|
| Only Ridgeway and Millbrook | They went from a 90-minute loop to one pickup a day at 3:30 pm, with samples unspun until then. |
| Harbour is at 5% of flags | Same contractor, but Harbour spins on site before pickup. This is your natural control. |
| Draws before 11 am flag at 11%, after 1 pm at about 1% | Morning samples wait the longest, perhaps 5-7 hours. Afternoon samples wait minutes. This dose-response gradient is the strongest evidence in your data, and no other candidate predicts it. |
| Hemolysis index normal on 93% | Cold-induced potassium leak happens without cell lysis. The index is blind to it. |
| Calcium and sodium normal | Consistent with leak from intact cells. It argues against contamination (e.g. EDTA carry-over would drop calcium). |
| Inpatient and ED unchanged | Short transport times. |
| Policy dated August 1, problem dated September 8 | The fridge was harmless when the wait was 90 minutes or less. Chilled whole blood held for hours blocks the Na/K pump, and potassium moves out of the cells. The two changes only became dangerous together. |

You said you doubt the cold-hold policy matters because it predates the problem. That is the wrong inference. It was a latent hazard that the courier change activated.

## What the other suspects don't explain

- **Analyzer A reagent lot.** 57% of flags on a platform that runs 60% of volume is no enrichment at all. A QC shift of +0.08 mmol/L cannot turn a 4.2 into a 5.6. It would also hit inpatient and ED samples on the same analyzer, and it would not produce a morning/afternoon gradient or a site pattern. Quarantining the lot would remove 60% of your capacity and fix nothing. Your unease about the lot is reasonable, but it is not a cause. Track it separately.
- **Tomas.** More difficult draws would show up as hemolysis, which is normal here. He does not explain Millbrook, which has the same pattern without him. He does not explain the time-of-day gradient. The nurse manager's observation is plausible but it is one site and one impression. Retraining him will not change the numbers. It may still be worth doing for other reasons, but don't tell the quality meeting it is the fix.
- **Other causes** (true hyperkalemia, a new patient mix, fist clenching, contamination). None of them fits a sharp onset on September 8, a site pattern, a time-of-day pattern, and flat inpatient rates at once. I can't rule out a small second contributor, such as difficult draws at Ridgeway adding to it. The main effect is not in doubt.

## How sure I am

About 80-85%. The pattern is very specific, but I'm working from summary numbers, not sample-level timestamps. A few cheap checks will either confirm it or break it.

## Checks to run before Friday (a few hours of work)
