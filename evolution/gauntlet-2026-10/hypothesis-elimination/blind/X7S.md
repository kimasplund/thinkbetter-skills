**Short answer:** This looks like pseudohyperkalemia from specimens sitting unspun for hours, and refrigerated while they wait. It doesn't look like an analyzer lot problem or a phlebotomy technique problem. I'd hold off on quarantining the lot and retraining Tomas, and bring the evidence below to Friday instead. The evidence is strong but circumstantial, so I've listed checks that would confirm it.

**Why I think it's transport delay plus cold storage**

1. **The location pattern points at the courier change.** Ridgeway and Millbrook send about 30% of volume but produce 83% of the high results. They are the two sites that went from a 90-minute loop to a single 3:30 pm pickup on September 7. That is one day before your rise started. Harbour uses the same contractor but spins on site, and it is basically unaffected (5%). The contractor isn't the problem. Samples that go unseparated for hours are.

2. **The time-of-day pattern fits.** At those two sites, 11% of samples drawn before 11 am flag high, against about 1% of those drawn after 1 pm. Morning samples wait the longest for a 3:30 pm pickup. Afternoon samples wait the least. A reagent or a needle wouldn't care what time of day the draw happened.

3. **The cold-hold policy is the likely amplifier.** Cold slows the Na/K-ATPase pump, so potassium leaks out of red cells into the serum. Whole blood that sits in a fridge for hours reads falsely high. The policy started August 1, but it did little harm while the courier came every 90 minutes. Since September 7 the fridge is holding specimens for 3 to 6 hours or more. Neither change alone would do this, but together they do. That is why the rise began in September and not August.

4. **The hemolysis index doesn't rule this out.** Normal hemolysis, calcium and sodium are what you expect with pseudohyperkalemia from cold or delayed separation. This leak happens without rupturing the cells. That also argues against a traumatic draw. Tomas's 23-gauge butterfly needles would more likely show up as hemolysis. 93% of flagged samples have a normal index.

5. **The analyzer evidence is weak.** Analyzer A runs about 60% of volume and accounts for 57% of the flags. That is proportional, so the analyzer isn't over-represented. A 0.08 mmol/L QC shift inside limits can't turn 4.5 into 5.8. It could nudge borderline results by a hair, but it can't explain a four-fold jump concentrated at two sites. Inpatient and ED flag rates, which also run on your analyzers, haven't moved.

6. **Tomas is probably a coincidence.** Millbrook has the same problem and no new phlebotomist. Ridgeway's difficult draws are real, but they would show up as hemolysis, and they don't explain the time-of-day pattern. Retraining him on this evidence would be unfair to him and wouldn't fix the problem.

**What I'd do before Friday**

*Right now, for patient safety:*
- Tell the Ridgeway and Millbrook physicians that outpatient potassium results above 5.5 from September 8 onward may be spurious. Ask them to recollect before acting, and to get a fresh sample spun promptly.
- Consider adding an interpretive comment to potassium results from those sites. For example, it could say that the sample was delayed or unseparated and that results above the range should be confirmed.
- Consider a look-back on patients who were treated or sent to the ED on these results.

*Fix the cause:*
- Suspend the cold-hold rule for potassium specimens. Hold them at room temperature (never refrigerated) until they are spun. Keep the fridge for tests that need it, such as ammonia on ice or other cold-sensitive analytes.
- Best fix is to spin at Ridgeway and Millbrook, as Harbour does. That needs a centrifuge and training. Interim option is to restore a midday pickup. Use gel-separator tubes if you can.

*Confirm it with data (should take a day or two):*
- Pull collection time and lab receipt time for flagged and unflagged samples at those two clinics. Potassium should rise with elapsed time. If it doesn't, my hypothesis is wrong.
- Rerun a few flagged samples that are still available on Analyzer B. If B agrees with A, the lot is cleared.
- Look at paired repeats for the flagged patients, such as the two sent to the ED. Fresh spun samples should be normal.
- Do a quick pilot. Spin a few morning samples at Ridgeway before transport and compare with the transported tube from the same patient. A big difference proves the mechanism.
- Check the other two clinics, which contribute 12%. They may have fewer delays, but find out what their transport looks like.

**What I'd say about the two proposals**
- *Quarantine the lot:* Not supported. The numbers are proportional by analyzer. If you want to be careful, run a side-by-side comparison with the old lot, but don't pull it based on this data.
- *Retrain Tomas:* Not supported by the data. Offer ordinary draw-technique support if the nurse manager wants it, but don't tie it to the potassium issue.

**Assumptions I made:** I assumed the specimens are whole-blood tubes that go to the central lab unspun, that 3:30 pm is the only pickup, and that the Analyzer A and B methods are comparable. If any of these are wrong, tell me and I'll adjust.
