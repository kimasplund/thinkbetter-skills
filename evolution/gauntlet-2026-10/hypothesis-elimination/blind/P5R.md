I think this is pseudohyperkalemia from delayed separation, and the cold-hold policy turned the courier change into a problem. I don't think it's the reagent lot or Tomas.

**What's going on**

- When whole blood sits unspun for hours, potassium leaks out of the cells. It's worse when the blood is cold, because refrigeration shuts down the Na/K-ATPase pump that keeps potassium inside. Hemolysis isn't needed, which fits your normal hemolysis index on 93% of the flagged samples.
- The cold-hold policy started August 1, and nothing changed then. The courier change on September 7 is what matters. Ridgeway and Millbrook went from a 90-minute loop to one pickup a day at 3:30 pm. Those samples now sit in a fridge, unspun, for up to several hours.
- The timing fits. The jump started September 8, the first full day after the courier change.
- Morning draws wait longest, which explains 11% flagging before 11 am against about 1% after 1 pm. The afternoon samples barely wait.
- Harbour uses the same contractor but spins samples on site, and only 5% of the flags came from there. That is a natural control. Inpatient and ED samples reach the lab quickly, so their rates haven't moved.
- Ridgeway and Millbrook send about 30% of outpatient volume but produced 83% of the flags.

**Why not the other two**

- **Analyzer A lot:** The QC shift is +0.08 mmol/L, far too small to turn 1.5% into 6%. Analyzer A ran 57% of the flagged samples and does about 60% of the volume, so the flags aren't concentrated on it. A lot problem wouldn't hit only two clinics, or only morning draws. The lot was introduced September 4, and the jump started September 8. I wouldn't quarantine it on this evidence. It would cost you capacity and fix nothing.
- **Tomas:** Difficult draws and small-gauge needles can cause hemolysis, but your hemolysis index is normal. He started September 1, a week before the change. He only works at Ridgeway, and Millbrook has the same problem. At most he is a minor contributor, and retraining him won't fix this. Pinning it on him would also be unfair.

**What to bring Friday**

1. A flag rate by clinic, split by draw time. Add collection-to-centrifugation time if you can get it from receipt or LIS timestamps. I'd expect the flag rate to climb steadily with that interval.
2. A quick confirmation, if you can do one this week. Draw paired samples at Ridgeway or Millbrook. Spin one immediately and send the other through the normal courier. A gap of 0.5 mmol/L or more would settle it. Ideally, add a plasma-versus-serum or room-temperature-versus-fridge comparison.
3. A rerun of a sample of flagged results from the lot. If the high values were pre-analytical, Analyzer A should reproduce them on spun, fresh samples, which would also clear it.
4. Fixes, most effective first.
   - Spin on site at Ridgeway and Millbrook, as Harbour does, using gel-barrier tubes. This is the most robust fix.
   - Or restore frequent pickups.
   - Whichever you choose, change the cold-hold rule. Whole blood for potassium should not be refrigerated. Spin it, or keep it at room temperature. Cold-hold is fine for other analytes, so the policy needs to carve potassium out.
5. Patient safety actions, which I'd do before Friday.
   - Tell clinicians now that outpatient potassium results above 5.5 from Ridgeway and Millbrook since September 8 are suspect, and should be repeated before treatment or an ED referral.
   - Add a comment on those results.
   - Look back at the roughly 4.5% excess of flagged results since September 8 and at who was recalled or treated.
   - Check whether any patient was given potassium-lowering treatment based on a false result.

My assumptions: the flagged samples were mostly unspun during transport, and the clinics' fridges are about 4°C. I haven't seen your timestamps, so the paired test is what would confirm this. I'd also look at low potassium results in the same population, since that would be unusual if only a lot or needle problem were present.
