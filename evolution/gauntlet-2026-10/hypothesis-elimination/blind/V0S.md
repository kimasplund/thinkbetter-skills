**Short answer:** The most likely cause is false (pseudo) hyperkalemia. Whole blood is sitting unspun in a clinic fridge for hours before it reaches you. Cold stops the cell pumps that hold potassium inside red cells, so potassium leaks into the serum or plasma. This is a problem with how the specimens are handled, not with the analyzer lot or one phlebotomist. I'd recommend against both the lot quarantine and the retraining as the main response.

**Why this fits your data**

The cold-hold policy started August 1, but the flags didn't rise until September 8. The policy was harmless while Ridgeway and Millbrook had a 90-minute courier loop. On September 7 they moved to one pickup a day at 3:30 pm. Specimens that were chilled for about an hour are now chilled for up to 5 to 6 hours. The flag rise began the next day.

| Observation | What it says |
|---|---|
| Ridgeway and Millbrook send 30% of volume but account for 83% of the high results | The problem follows the sites with the long wait, not the instrument |
| Harbour spins on site before pickup and sits at 5% of high results | Same contractor, same pickup, but separated cells and no leak. This is your natural control |
| 11% flagged for draws before 11 am vs about 1% after 1 pm | Morning draws wait longest for the 3:30 pm pickup. This dose-response pattern is hard to explain any other way |
| Hemolysis index normal on 93% of flagged samples | Cold leakage raises potassium without lysing cells. This argues against a traumatic draw |
| Calcium and sodium normal | No sign of EDTA contamination or a general specimen defect |
| Inpatient and ED rates unchanged | Those samples reach the lab fast |
| Two ED patients had normal repeat potassium | This is what a handling artifact looks like |

**Why the two proposed fixes don't hold up**

- **Analyzer A reagent lot.** The QC mean moved up 0.08 mmol/L, which is small and within limits. A shift that size can't turn 1.5% into 6%. The share of flagged samples on Analyzer A (57%) is slightly below its share of volume (60%), so the analyzer isn't over-represented. Millbrook is also affected, and Harbour isn't, which a lot problem wouldn't explain. The lot is worth a quiet comparison, but quarantining it would disrupt 60% of your volume and fix nothing.
- **Tomas and the 23-gauge butterfly.** Difficult draws and small needles usually raise potassium through hemolysis, and your hemolysis is normal. Millbrook has the same problem without a Tomas. The morning/afternoon split doesn't fit a single person's technique either. Retraining him won't hurt, but it isn't the cause. Ridgeway's nurse manager may be seeing a real effect, but it isn't driving these numbers. Any extra hemolysis from his draws would be a small separate issue.

**What I'd like to see confirmed**

I'm working from your summary, so these checks would make it airtight:

1. Pull draw time and lab receipt time for every outpatient potassium since September 1. Flag rate should climb steadily with elapsed time to centrifugation, regardless of site, instrument or phlebotomist. Also check whether the other two clinics are on the new courier schedule and what their volumes are. That would explain their 12% share.
2. Compare Ridgeway and Millbrook flags on Analyzer A and Analyzer B. If the rate is similar on both, the lot is cleared.
3. Compare Tomas's draws with other Ridgeway phlebotomists at the same draw time. Expect no difference in potassium.
4. Run a quick test. Collect paired tubes from a few Ridgeway or Millbrook patients. Spin one immediately and let the other go through the fridge and the 3:30 pm pickup. Expect the held tube to read higher, probably by 0.5 to 1+ mmol/L after several hours, and the spun tube to be normal.
