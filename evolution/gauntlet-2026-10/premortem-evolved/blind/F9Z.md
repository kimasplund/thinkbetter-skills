# Premortem: Tidewell x Calloway Markets, 48 stores, 3 flavors, March 1 2027

**Short answer:** Not as written. The velocity is plausible and the account is real. The structure is the problem: about $97k of liquidity is needed before the first net dollar arrives, and you have $60k.

**Horizon:** September 30, 2027 · **Success was defined as:** "by September, Calloway is our biggest account and the business is profitable."
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure ("if this dies, this is what killed it"), not the probability the plan fails overall. They sum to ~100. **Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon, as written:** ~75%.

**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- Your average selling price is about $12 a jar, so $9k a month is roughly 750 jars a month. Calloway at the buyer's expectation (864 jars a week) would be about five times your entire current volume.
- The existing business roughly pays for itself and your draws and throws off no spare cash. January falls in the farmers-market trough.
- Calloway invoices as jars ship. The free opening case covers the first two weeks of sales, so the first invoice is the week of March 15. Net-60 runs from invoice, and slotting and promo are taken out of payments as they arrive.
- The promo is 25% off the $8.99 shelf price ($2.25 a jar). If it is 25% off wholesale, it costs $1.35 a jar and the promo bill falls by about $6k.
- Halvard's $3.10 includes jars, labels and case-packing. Trial batches are billed inside the first order.
- Calloway's March 1 start date is written into the contract.

**The plan's own math, before anything goes wrong**
- 48 stores x 3 flavors = 144 shelf slots. At 6 jars per slot per week that is 864 jars a week, $4,666 a week at wholesale, about $20k a month.
- Landed cost is $3.10 + $0.30 = $3.40, so margin is $2.00 a jar. That is about $1,728 a week, or $7.5k a month at full velocity, before overhead and credits.
- Entry costs are about $57k: slotting $36,000, promo ~$15.5k, free opening fill 1,728 jars x $3.40 = $5.9k. Slotting alone ($36k) is more margin than the entire first 15,000-jar production run will ever earn ($30k).
- At full velocity the $57k takes about 33 weeks of invoiced sales to earn back. Cumulative break-even lands around late October, after your September target. September can look profitable month to month only if everything above holds and the deductions are booked as one-time.
- First production run: 15,000 jars x $3.10 = $46,500, with $23,250 due in January and $23,250 on delivery. Because the minimum is 5,000 per flavor, every reorder of all three flavors is another $46.5k.
- Calendar: order Jan 4, six weeks gives Feb 15, and the trial batch adds 2-3 weeks. That puts finished goods at Halvard at March 1-8 at best, not "late February."

## The Autopsy

**The strongest case for this plan:** A 48-store regional chain approached you, which is inbound demand rather than a cold pitch. You have a proven direct customer base, and chili crisp is a category with real pull. At the buyer's 6 jars per slot per week, Calloway is a $20k-a-month account against your current $9k, so "biggest account" is nearly automatic. The $2.00 jar margin (37%) produces about $90k a year from one account once entry costs are behind you. Halvard removes the kitchen as the ceiling on your growth. A winning regional chain also opens the door to other chains. Slotting, free fill and launch promos are standard retail entry costs, and a smart operator would say "pay the toll once and own the shelf." Each failure mode below has to beat this case.

Seven ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The cash ran out in late May, the first Calloway check netted to zero, and two of three flavors sat empty from late June
**Probability:** ~30% — the plan misses on its own arithmetic (about a $37k hole at the plan's own velocity), but a bridge from Calloway or a lender is possible, so it stays at 30 and not higher.
**Cause of death:** Prepaid 5,000-jar minimums against slotting and promo deducted from net-60 payments, with a second lot due before any net money arrived.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Jan): You ordered 3 x 5,000 jars on Jan 4. The $23,250 deposit left cash at $36,750, in the weakest month of the base business.
- Month 2 (Feb): Nothing to sell, and the $23,250 balance came due on delivery.
- Month 3 (Mar): About $13.5k cash after the balance. The free fill (1,728 jars) shipped, freight ran about $260 a week, and weekly replenishment of ~864 jars started in week 3. Every sale was on net-60.
- Month 4 (Apr): Velocity hit the buyer's 6, the promo ended Apr 26, and the buyer was pleased. Cash drifted to about $11k. Every dashboard you could see said success.
- Month 5 (May): The first remittance on May 14 netted $0: $4,666 gross against $51.5k of slotting and promo deductions that would take until about July 30 to absorb. The stock was running out around Jun 30 on a six-week lead, so the reorder was due about May 19: $23,250 deposit against $11k.
- Month 6 (Jun): You funded one flavor (a 5,000-jar lot is $15.5k, so $7,750 down). The other two flavors ran dry on the shelf from late June.
- Month 7 (Jul-Aug): Empty facings for about 11 weeks (net receipts only began in August). At the fall reset the buyer gave the two empty slots to another brand. In September Calloway was one flavor and about a third of the run rate, below your base business, and cumulative cash was still negative.
**The assumption that allowed it:** Net-60 and deductions are a delay, not a hole; the $60k covers "the first order." The plan's guard is none: the line of credit is already counted inside the $60k.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** You offered none, so I fall back to small food brands taking their first grocery chain, which tend to die of working capital before demand (weakly sourced, judgment). You differ in two directions: a live $9k-a-month direct business and a regional rather than national chain push the number down; no broker or distributor absorbing any of these terms pushes it up.
**First warning sign:** Visible today in a weekly cash forecast. In flight, the Jan 4 deposit leaves $36,750 against $46.5k still owed before any net receipt.

### 2. Finished goods landed March 1-8 at best, a failed trial pushed one flavor to late March, and the promo clock ran against empty shelves
**Probability:** ~20% — near-certain to slip 1-3 weeks on Halvard's own figures; the share is lower because a short slip alone is survivable.
**Cause of death:** A start date set from the six-week lead time alone, without the trial batch, written into a contract with no slip relief.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 0 (Dec): You signed Dec 15 for a Mar 1 start. Halvard schedules trial batches after the order, not before.
- Month 1 (Jan): Order on Jan 4. Trial batches arrived Jan 18-25; the third flavor's crunch and heat were off at kettle scale and needed a second trial, with sign-off Feb 8-15.
- Month 2 (Feb): Two flavors were scheduled to finish Mar 1-8; the third was scheduled for Mar 22-29. The "late February" date you gave yourself was already gone.
- Month 3 (Mar): Mar 1 arrived with no product. The 8-week promo clock and slotting terms still ran from the contract date, so you funded promo weeks you couldn't sell into, and the buyer filled the empty facings with a rival brand until the next reset.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "Late February delivery," the date you stated with the most certainty. It held only if the trial batch is invisible, and it isn't. The plan has no guard: nothing in it schedules a trial, and Halvard's lead time isn't in writing.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** A first run with a new contract manufacturer slipping by an unbudgeted ~40% is the base-rate norm (weakly sourced). Here the slip is built in before anything goes wrong. Your difference is that the chain may tolerate days of delay (unknown, and not in the paper you have), which pushes the number down.
**First warning sign:** Jan 22: fewer than all three trial batches signed off, or no written production slot from Halvard.

### 3. Compound: velocity ran at half, the deductions took until October to absorb, and the six-month jars started expiring into credits
**Probability:** ~15% — the buyer's 6 a week is an expectation, not data; I have no sourced base rate for a new-to-grocery condiment, and 6 is above what I'd assume for an unknown brand (judgment call, weakly sourced).
**Cause of death:** Two modes firing together. Low sell-through stretches the net-60 deduction hole, and the long lot-sizes meet a 26-week shelf life.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 3 (Mar-Apr): The promo-lifted weeks read near 5 per slot per week, and you read that as validation.
- Month 5 (May): Post-promo velocity fell to about 3, and replenishment orders dropped to ~430 jars a week. On May 19 you ordered the second lot on the promo read, paying the $23,250 deposit.
- Month 6 (Jun): At 432 jars a week the invoices absorb the deductions over about 22 weeks, so net receipts moved from August to October. A 5,000-jar flavor lot lasts 17 weeks at 6 per store, 26 weeks at 4, and 35 weeks at 3. At 3 per store-week, no lot sells inside its shelf life.
- Month 7 (Jul-Aug): A second lot arrived into a market that hadn't finished the first. Run-rate margin at 3 a week was $3.7k a month, so the $57k entry cost needed about 66 weeks to earn back.
- Month 8 (Sep): The first expiry credits landed at $5.40 a jar on jars you'd paid $3.10 for. The account was not profitable and not clearly the biggest.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That the buyer's 6 a week, and the promo weeks, predict the 6 a week after the promo ends. The plan's guard is none: nothing caps the expired-product credits or ties the reorder to post-promo data.
**First warning sign:** Calloway replenishment orders in the weeks of May 3-24 running below ~580 jars a week (4 per slot).

### 4. Calloway's paper let it take money back without a cap, and nobody on their side owed you a volume
**Probability:** ~10% — the terms as sent cap nothing and define nothing, but the draft isn't in front of me, so some of this may already be fixed.
**Cause of death:** Open-ended credits and chargebacks (damaged and expired product returned for credit, promo billed back) with no volume commitment, no term, and no exit.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 0 (Dec): You signed the terms as listed. Calloway holds Accept at best: the buyer "expects about 6," which is neither a purchase order nor a commitment.
- Month 3 (Mar-Apr): Credits for "damaged" included breakage in Calloway's warehouse and stores. The promo bill-back was computed on shelf price, not wholesale.
- Month 5 (May): The first remittances showed unitemized short-pays. A 5% credit rate takes your $2.00 jar to $1.73 per jar shipped, and each credited jar erases the margin on 2.7 sold jars.
- Month 6-7 (Jun-Jul): Reconciling cost 6+ hours a week. The buyer rotated, the new buyer added a "marketing fund" at reset, and you had no paper to push back with.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That a buyer who approached you will behave as a partner. The plan's guard is none: the contract draft is not yet read by anyone with CPG experience.
**First warning sign:** The deduction schedule isn't itemized in the draft, or the first remittance has a short-pay you can't tie to a line in the contract.

### 5. The vendor packet arrived after the signature and nothing in it had been priced or scheduled
**Probability:** ~10% — cheap items individually, but each waits on a third party's calendar.
**Cause of death:** The omission: product liability insurance naming Calloway, barcodes and case labels, warehouse appointment and pallet rules, and non-compliance fines. None of it is in your budget or timeline.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Jan): The packet arrived Jan 6. The insurance binder took 2-3 weeks to issue.
- Month 2 (Feb): Halvard's cases weren't built to Calloway's pallet and label spec.
- Month 3 (Mar): The first load was refused at the dock or accepted with fines deducted from invoices. You re-labeled by hand while the promo clock ran.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That supplying a grocery chain is "ship jars to the warehouse." The plan's guard is none.
**First warning sign:** The vendor packet is not in your hands before you sign Dec 15.

### 6. You both stopped cooking and started three other jobs; the one business that made money got the leftovers
**Probability:** ~10% — a mundane base-rate killer, but you are two people with no one owning Calloway.
**Cause of death:** Bandwidth. Halvard QA, Calloway's portal and reconciliations, out-of-stock emails from 48 stores, and the markets and online store all landed on two people.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 2 (Feb): Both of you were on trials, the contract and financing.
- Month 3 (Mar): Weekend markets still needed two people. Online ads were paused for lack of hours.
- Month 4 (Apr): One of you was out three weeks (illness). The base business slipped from $9k toward $6-7k, in the month you needed its cash.
- Month 5 (May): The base business had lost its momentum, and remittance disputes ate the freed hours.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That giving up the kitchen frees time; it moves the work to Halvard and Calloway. The plan's guard is none: no role is assigned.
**First warning sign:** Base-business weekly revenue under 80% of its trailing eight-week average for two weeks running, from March 8.

### 7. The frame's blind spot: what Calloway's buyer was optimizing was never on the page
**Probability:** ~5% — can't be mechanism-ized from three lines of terms; the adversary section resolves it.
**Cause of death:** The terms sheet explains why Calloway wins in every outcome ($36k slotting, $9.3k of free goods at wholesale, and a funded promo arrive before a jar sells through). It doesn't say why a 48-store chain wants 144 slots from a brand with no regional awareness, who is being displaced, or whether a private-label or category reset is coming.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1-3 (Jan-Mar): You didn't ask, because you were thrilled.
- Month 5-6 (May-Jun): The buyer sought sell-through data and cost detail beyond compliance.
- Month 8 (Sep): The fall reset decided without you.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That Calloway's goals line up with yours. The plan's guard is none.
**First warning sign:** The buyer won't say the reset calendar or who got displaced.

**Tie-break at ~10%:** three modes sit on the rung.
- #4 outranks #5: it fires silent in month 5 and the cash it takes is gone, while #5 fires loud at the first dock and costs a reschedule and a fine.
- #4 outranks #6: it is client-triggered, so working harder can't stop it, while #6 can be cut by dropping a market.
- #5 outranks #6: it is near-certain to be met in January-March and only a third party's calendar fixes it, while #6 needs two things to go wrong at once.

**Considered and cut:** freight cost creep (folded into #1), Halvard bumping your slot for a bigger client (folded into #2), single-account concentration after launch (folded into #4), buyer turnover mid-year (folded into #7), channel conflict with your own market and online prices (<2%, recoverable).

**Downgraded — guarded for now:**

|---|---|---|
| Recipe drift at co-packer scale | Trial batch you sign off before anything is scheduled | Blind taste of a production jar against your own jar: 2 of 4 tasters pick it as different on heat or crunch |
| Oil or jar quality failing before day 180 | Halvard's process gives a 6-month shelf life | Two quality-defect credits (not breakage) in one month |

## The Rebuild

**The revised plan:** Counter before Dec 15 with a phased launch. Lead flavor only, in all 48 stores, starting on delivery or the next reset after Mar 1. Flavors two and three enter later, once the post-promo data and your cash both support it. Trial batches for all three flavors move into November so sign-off precedes the signature. Entry costs fall from ~$57k to ~$19k (slotting $12k, fill 576 jars x $3.40 = ~$2.0k, promo ~$5.2k) and the first lot from $46.5k to $15.5k ($7,750 deposit). Margin at full velocity is $576 a week ($2.5k a month), $5k a month less than the three-flavor plan, which is the price of phasing: about $15k over three months against a $37k hole. Restate the goal as "cash-positive and profitable by September, with Calloway the biggest account if flavor two is in by August." Size is the vanity metric; cash is the real one.

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **Cash.** Check: ask Calloway AP in writing when invoicing starts, whether net-60 runs from delivery or receipt, how slotting and promo are deducted, and whether promo is 25% of shelf or wholesale; put the answers in a weekly forecast to Sep 30. Walk away if the confirmed peak shortfall is above $5k after committed credit, with no phased option. Falsifier: a written Calloway deferral of slotting over 12+ weeks or net-30, or a committed ≥$40k facility by Dec 12. Without one of those, the $37k hole stands.
2. **Timeline.** Check: Halvard's written production slot for the week of Jan 4 and a delivery date after sign-off. Walk away if the launch flavor isn't signed off by Dec 12 or Halvard won't commit to delivery by Feb 19. Falsifier: three trial batches signed off by Dec 12 and a written date on or before Feb 19. Anything else, and "late February" is false.
3. **Velocity.** Check: ask the buyer for weeks 9-16 sell-through on the last two new local or specialty condiment launches, plus the reset calendar. Walk away if there is no comparable and Calloway refuses the phased launch. Falsifier: comparables averaging under 4 units per store-SKU-week in weeks 9-16 (and the stock you can't sell within 26 weeks).
4. **Paper.** Check: full contract and vendor packet reviewed by a food or CPG attorney (a few hours). Walk away if credits stay uncapped or there is no exit clause after you've asked.
5. **The unknowns.** Check: ask the buyer who is being displaced, whether a private-label condiment is planned, and why now. Ask Halvard for non-use and confidentiality on your process and a no-private-label undertaking. Walk away if Halvard refuses non-use or Calloway confirms private-label chili crisp.

**Decision rule:** IF by Dec 8 Calloway has agreed in writing to a lead-flavor, all-48-store first phase with slotting at or under $12k and capped credits, AND the launch flavor's trial batch is signed off by Dec 12, THEN sign Dec 15 and order the lead flavor Jan 4. ELSE IF Calloway insists on all three flavors in all 48 stores, sign only if ≥$40k of additional credit is committed by Dec 12 and the slotting is deducted over 12+ weeks or net-30 is agreed. ELSE do not sign and ask for the next reset. Don't take the middle (three flavors in 16 stores): it keeps the 15,000-jar prepaid minimum while cutting velocity to a third, about 52 weeks of stock against a 26-week shelf life, so it inherits the worst of both. Risky capability (trial sign-off) is sequenced before the irreversible steps (signature, deposit).

**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** The horizon passed. We phased and it was a mistake: the buyer gave the two flavors' slots to a rival at the March reset; we chose the wrong lead flavor; and the base business grew without Calloway anyway. 

**What tips the balance:** A written Calloway commitment to defer slotting over 12+ weeks or pay net-30 cuts the hole below $15k and sends you to the full three-flavor plan. Written into the rule above as the ELSE IF.

**Consciously accepted:** The buyer's motive (#7). You can't buy that information, and the unknown stays unknown. Priced: if Calloway's motive is a one-reset experiment, the loss is capped at the phase-one entry cost of ~$19k.

**How the rebuilt plan dies:** Calloway says no to phasing and you hold the line, losing the account, and then the base business has to carry the next two years alone. Alternatively, the lead flavor is the wrong one, and the read after promo (3 weeks, May 11-24) is too early to decide flavor two. The fix isn't strictly dominant.

**Residual risk:** Velocity below 4. The buyer's discretion at every reset. Concentration: if flavor two lands, Calloway is about 60% of revenue on net-60 and a delisting is a shock.

## The Adversary

**Playing:** Calloway's category buyer and private-label team. Motivation: margin per shelf foot, funded trade spend, and a cheaper own-brand version of any item that proves itself. Resources: the shelf, store-level sales data, supplier payables, and the right to short-pay. Can't: invent your recipe overnight or deliver your brand equity. Best alternative: any condiment brand with broker backing, or a national chili crisp at similar margin.
**The kill chain:**
1. **Probe (Dec 15-Jan 15):** The vendor packet asks for ingredient sourcing, cost of goods and process detail "for compliance."
2. **Position (Mar 1-Apr 26):** The promo weeks give Calloway your velocity curve, and your jars sit off the main condiment set or at the bottom shelf.
3. **Strike (May 14-Jul):** Remittance variances, a new "marketing fund" fee at reset, a request for another promo you fund, or a price ask from $5.40 to $4.95 "to hold the slot."
4. **Entrench (Aug-Sep):** A private-label chili crisp at around $5.99 at the fall reset, built on your proven velocity.
**Detection opportunities:** Move 1: any request for COGS or process beyond allergen and ingredient statements before May 1. Move 2: shelf placement in six audited stores by Mar 20. Move 3: a remittance variance over 3% of invoice, or any fee line not in the contract, from May 14. Move 4: an announced private-label condiment line or a rival's new facings, checked Aug 15 and Sep 15.
**The move you'd never see coming:** Halvard, not Calloway, makes the private label. The co-packer holds your process, and nothing in the quote says it can't make a near-copy for someone else.
**What this means you should change:** Get non-use and confidentiality from Halvard in writing before the first deposit. Disclose only allergen and ingredient statements. Have a second retailer in conversation by August so Calloway isn't the only door.
