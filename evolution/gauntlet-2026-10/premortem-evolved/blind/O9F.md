# Premortem: Saltmarsh Clay second space (beginner wheel courses)
**Horizon:** Dec 25 · **Success was defined as:** "the new space covering its own costs by Christmas without pulling money from the original studio"
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure — "if this dies, this is what killed it" — not the probability the plan fails overall. They sum to ~100. **Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** about 19 in 20 as written, because the plan fails on its own numbers before any outside risk arrives.
**Assumptions I'm working from:** You gave no comparable past project, so the reference classes below are mine and weakly sourced (no dated search run). It is mid-May, about two weeks before the June 2 signing. Fit-out (10 wheels, tables, shelving, sink and clay trap, ventilation, gas piping) costs about $8,000 and is not inside the $9,400 a month. Kiln loan, insurance and utilities start at signing (about $3,500 a month before rent) and the instructor starts in August. Students pay at enrollment. The 60 member pieces a week and the 2–3 beginner pieces a week are finished pieces, so each needs both a bisque and a glaze firing. One 24 cu ft load holds 70 pieces of either kind. If any of this is wrong, correct me, because the cash and kiln math below leans on it.

## The Autopsy
**The strongest case for this plan:** Saltmarsh already has 30 people paying monthly, which proves Corvel Bay will pay for wheel time and firing. 140 people left their names at a free try-the-wheel booth, which is real interest from people who had a clay-covered hand in the thing you sell. Two months of free rent covers the fit-out period. A studio that teaches beginners feeds its own membership, so a second room that makes members out of strangers is how studios grow. The old electric kiln is dying, so a new, bigger gas kiln fixes a problem you have to solve anyway. A smart person looking at that sees a proven business, a warm list, a concession on rent and an asset that replaces a failing one.

Six ways this died, ranked by probability. Tie-break: #4 and #5 both sit at ~10%. #4 ranks above #5 because an unfilled cohort ends the plan with nothing to rescue it, while the overruns in #5 are survivable if enrollment holds.

### 1. Four full cohorts brought in $7,367 a month against $9,400 of costs, and the plan was never in the black
**Probability:** ~40% — the plan's own arithmetic, with no outside event needed
**Cause of death:** The plan compared $13,600 per course to $9,400 per month. $13,600 is the revenue of an eight-week cycle; $9,400 is one month of costs. Eight weeks of costs is about $17,350. Full cohorts bring in $13,600 per 8 weeks, which is $7,367 a month and 78 percent of costs. At 70 percent fill it is $5,157 a month; at 50 percent, $3,683. Break-even at $340 needs about 51 paying students at once, against 40 seats.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jun: Lease signed June 2 with a personal guarantee. Fit-out (about $8,000) and kiln, insurance and utilities began; savings dropped from $22,000 to roughly $14,000, then about $7,000 by the end of July.
- Aug: Rent of $4,100 started Aug 4 with the first cohorts. At 70 percent fill, cycle one brought in $9,520 against about $17,350 of costs.
- Sep: Savings reached about zero by the end of cycle one (late September). A full house would have delayed that to November, not prevented it.
- Oct–Nov: Money moved from the original studio to cover the new rent, which broke the second half of the success definition. The second cycle started with a worse cash position and the same math.
- Dec: By Christmas the new space covered between 40 and 78 percent of its costs, and you were carrying a lease with about $221,000 of guarantee left to run (58 paid months at $4,100 is $237,800 in total).
**The assumption that allowed it:** "With all four cohorts full, it should be in the black." You were the most certain here, and it is the first thing that fails.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Small studios and gyms that add a second site on a personal guarantee usually fail on the fixed-cost base being sized for full capacity, not on demand (my class, weakly sourced). Where yours differs: the error is arithmetic, not forecasting, which pushes the number up, not down.
**Existing guard:** none. The $22,000 cushion only delays it.
**First warning sign:** Today. A model with a month-by-month cash line shows it before June 2; otherwise the first rent bill on Aug 4 does.

### 2. The kiln could finish 105 pieces a week and the plan produced 140 to 180
**Probability:** ~20% — second own-math miss, and it only bites if enrollment works
**Cause of death:** One 24 cu ft kiln at three firings a week offers 210 piece-firings. Every piece needs two firings, so it finishes 105 pieces a week. Demand was 60 member pieces plus 40 students at 2–3 pieces: 140 to 180. That is 58 to 75 percent coverage, a backlog of 35 to 75 pieces every week. Giving members priority leaves room for about 45 beginner pieces a week, which is 15 to 22 students and $2,800 to $4,100 a month of tuition at $340 per 8 weeks.
**How it unfolded (counted from the first firing):**
- Month 1: The first loads were bisque-heavy and the unfired shelf grew by 35 to 75 pieces a week. Students who threw in week one saw nothing glazed by week four.
- Month 2: You chose between members and students. Members first meant beginners waited 4 to 6 weeks for finished pieces and stopped recommending the course. Students first meant members' work waited and cancellations hit the original studio.
- Month 3: The second cycle opened at 40 to 50 percent fill because the first cycle's students did not tell friends. The kiln, which was supposed to be the asset, was the ceiling on revenue.
**The assumption that allowed it:** One kiln on a two-day cycle covers all members plus 40 beginners. This is also the "plan worked and that killed it" mode: the fuller it got, the worse it broke.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Shared-kiln studios commonly run out of firing capacity before wheel capacity (weakly sourced). Where yours differs: your cycle time is short and you did the load math, which pushes the number down; but the math itself says the plan misses.
**Existing guard:** none. The plan states the firing cycle but never compares it to demand.
**First warning sign:** Within 10 days of the first firing, more than one load (70 pieces) waiting unfired on the shelves.

### 3. The gas meter could not be energized by Aug 4, the old kiln died first, and rent started anyway
**Probability:** ~15% — near-certain to happen on the utility's own dates, but it kills mainly through #1's cash condition (compound of timing and member loss)
**Cause of death:** The utility said 10–14 weeks from application, with the application filed June 2. That is Aug 11 at the earliest and Sep 8 at the latest, and the courses started Aug 4. Kiln delivery and a gas inspection add more weeks. Nothing in hand commits the utility to either date. Meanwhile the electric kiln at Saltmarsh was dying and not being replaced, so for the weeks in between there was no kiln at all.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jun–Jul: The application waited on the lease being signed. The old kiln failed in July (it was already dying), and members' 60 pieces a week had nowhere to go.
- Aug: Rent started Aug 4. Four cohorts began throwing 80 to 120 pieces a week and nothing could be bisque-fired. Dry pieces cracked in storage; two students asked for refunds.
- Sep: The meter went live around Sep 8, but kiln install and inspection took another one to two weeks. Five to six weeks of student work plus member work (700 to 900 pieces) sat waiting for bisque: 10 to 13 loads, about four weeks of kiln time before anyone saw glaze.
- Oct: Members who paid for wheel time and firing got neither for two months. Three to six left. The original studio's income fell while its owner was paying the new studio's rent from it.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "I'd apply the day the lease is signed" is the same as "the meter will be ready when the courses start."
**Existing guard:** none. No clause ties rent to energization and no fallback firing exists.
**First warning sign:** The utility's written date. A date after Aug 4 is certain; the question is whether you asked for it before signing.

### 4. The 140 names were browsers, and the cohorts filled to 40 percent
**Probability:** ~10% — a free fair sign-up sheet is the weakest form of demand evidence
**Cause of death:** The 140 names came from a free try-the-wheel booth. Nobody had seen a price or a date. Event leads for hobby classes commonly convert at 10–15 percent (weakly sourced, no dated search), which is 14 to 21 students against 40 seats.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jun: You emailed the list and 12 to 18 replied with interest, but few paid.
- Jul: Monday and Tuesday filled to 6 and 5; Wednesday and Thursday sat at 2 and 1. You cut the price to $280 to fill the room.
- Aug: Cohorts opened half full at a lower price. Revenue per cycle was about $6,000.
- Sep–Oct: With no evening cohort full, the instructor's hours stayed fixed; the revenue gap in #1 widened.
**The assumption that allowed it:** A free sign-up equals a $340 commitment.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Hobby-class conversion from event leads (weakly sourced). Where yours differs: the booth was local and hands-on, which pushes conversion up; the price is untested, which pushes it down.
**Existing guard:** the waitlist, which is inadequate because it carries no price and no deposit.
**First warning sign:** Fewer than 20 refundable deposits from the 140 within two weeks of the offer going out.

### 5. The fit-out ran over, nobody was staffed to load the kiln, and the owner stopped
**Probability:** ~10% — ordinary overruns and a bandwidth fight, a mundane killer
**Cause of death:** The $9,400 a month does not include the fit-out, the meter upgrade fee, kiln installation, a ventilation system for a gas kiln, a deposit, or student liability coverage. It also names an instructor but nobody to load and unload three firings a week, drive members' pieces across town, open the second room and cover the original studio on the same evenings.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jun: Fit-out ran 40 percent over its assumed $8,000 and took until mid-July.
- Jul–Aug: You were at the new space four evenings and loading the kiln on firing days. The original studio ran on goodwill.
- Sep–Oct: Members complained about open-studio hours and slow pieces. A loading error cost one glaze load.
- Nov–Dec: The tiredness you called "restlessness" in your message turned into a decision to close one of the two sites.
**The assumption that allowed it:** The plan priced rooms and machines but not the hours of the one person who runs both.
**Existing guard:** the $22,000 cushion, which is inadequate because #1 already spends it.
**First warning sign:** Fit-out invoices passing $9,000 before the end of June, or a week with more than 55 hours on clay business.

### 6. The first gas loads came out wrong for the members' glazes
**Probability:** ~5% — the frame's blind spot: I cannot see how the members' glazes behave in a gas kiln
**Cause of death:** The members' clay bodies and glazes were chosen for an electric kiln. A gas kiln fires in a different atmosphere and with different curves, and some glazes shift color or craze. The plan treats a kiln as a kiln.
**How it unfolded:**
- Sep: The first glaze loads came out with three of ten popular glazes off-color; two pieces cracked on cooling.
- Oct: Members whose work was spoiled stopped trusting the new kiln. You tested new glazes during the busiest weeks.
- Nov: Reformulating cost two more firing weeks, which fed #2's backlog.
**The assumption that allowed it:** Members' work moves between kilns unchanged.
**Existing guard:** none. No test firing is planned.
**First warning sign:** Test tiles from the three most-used member glazes fired in a gas kiln before the lease is signed.

**Considered and cut:** kiln-loan terms (folded into #1), lease clauses nobody read (folded into #3), friction between members and students over firing priority (folded into #2).

**Fatal flaw:** Yes, in the plan as written. Four full cohorts cannot cover $9,400 a month at $340, and the kiln cannot finish the pieces that the cohorts plus members produce. Neither flaw is in the idea of teaching beginners, and neither needs bad luck. Success by Christmas is not recoverable under any rebuild; I have moved that date below.

## The Rebuild
**The revised plan:** Do not sign on June 2 on these terms. Run two pilot cohorts (20 seats, same price and format) in the existing studio starting the week of Aug 4, after fixing the electric kiln so the pilot's work gets fired. Keep the new space only as an option: ask the landlord for rent that starts when the gas is energized and a guarantee capped at 12 months of rent ($49,200). Re-cut the new space's economics before signing, so that it breaks even at 70 percent fill of what the kiln can actually fire. Success moves from "covering its costs by Christmas" to "covering its costs by Mar 31 of the following year".

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. The utility date in writing. Ask the utility whether it will accept an application against a signed option or letter of intent, and get an energization date in writing by May 26. Walk away if the landlord will not tie rent start to that date. Falsifier for "gas will be ready for Aug 4": any written date after Aug 4. Expect one.
2. A priced demand test. Email the 140 names with price, dates and a refundable $100 deposit; count by May 26. Walk away below 20 deposits (about 14 percent). Falsifier for "the waitlist is demand": fewer than 20 paying names by May 26.
3. Real kiln numbers. Ask two local studios or the kiln vendor how many of your kind of pieces fit a bisque load and a glaze load, and log your members' actual pieces per week for three weeks. Walk away if verified capacity is under 120 finished pieces a week and neither a second kiln nor a cap is planned. Falsifier for "one kiln is enough": demand above capacity on the logged numbers.
4. A cash model with every cost. Write 12 months with real quotes, kiln loan start date and instructor start date. Walk away if cash reaches zero before Mar 31 at 70 percent fill. Falsifier for "the cushion covers it": the model shows zero before the new horizon.
5. Glaze test tiles. Fire the three most-used member glazes in a gas kiln by Jun 30. Walk away from sharing a kiln if more than one fails and members will not switch.
**Decision rule:** IF by May 26 the landlord has agreed in writing to rent that starts at energization and a capped guarantee, AND at least 20 of the 140 names have paid a deposit, AND the utility has given a written energization date, AND the model breaks even at 70 percent fill with a firing plan for 320 piece-firings a week, THEN sign on June 2 (or an extended date). ELSE do not sign: run the pilot from the week of Aug 4 and decide again on Oct 1. On Oct 1, sign for a January opening only if at least 18 of the 20 pilot seats paid, at least 6 graduates committed to monthly membership, and the same landlord, utility and firing conditions hold; otherwise keep one studio, replace the kiln, and run two course cohorts there each quarter. The pilot comes before the guarantee because it tests demand and teaching without a five-year commitment.
**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** It is Dec 25 and you didn't sign, and it was a mistake. The landlord leased the space on June 9 and nothing in Corvel Bay came near the price. The 140 names cooled and only 14 ever paid for anything. The old kiln died in July regardless, so you spent the first months on a replacement.
**What tips the balance:** All four conditions of the IF branch met by May 26, in particular rent starting at energization with a capped guarantee and 25 or more deposits. That flips the call to sign June 2; the decision rule above already says so.
**Consciously accepted (at most one):** Losing this specific space. Cost: three to six months of searching and possibly higher rent, which is a few thousand dollars against $237,800 of exposure.
**How the rebuilt plan dies:** The pilot sells to the warmest names in the existing room and overstates demand for a colder, farther location; running beginners in the existing studio also takes evening wheel time from members. The pilot is strictly better than signing blind, but it is not proof.
**Residual risk:** Demand outside the 140 names, the owner's own hours across two sites, and a landlord who will not move.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** The nearest rival that teaches beginners, such as a rec-center or community-college ceramics program 15 minutes away. Motivation: students and members; a competitor announcing a second site is a visible chance. Resources: an established course calendar, lower prices, a working kiln. Cannot do: match your membership culture or your wheel time. Best alternative: do nothing and let your kiln problem do the work.
**The kill chain:**
- Jun 20 (probe): they see your second-space announcement and the fair sign-up list and learn your price and Aug 4 start.
- Jul 10 (position): they post a fall beginner series at $295 with a firing-included guarantee, starting the week of Aug 25.
- Aug 11–Sep 8 (strike): while your meter is pending, students and members hear nothing about their pieces; the rival offers a free trial open studio with one-week firing to anyone from Saltmarsh.
- Oct 15 (entrench): they bundle holiday gift-making classes and members' firing at a subscription price.
**Detection opportunities:** A post or flyer with a fall series at $295 or less (Jul 10). Three or more members or students asking about outside firing (Aug 25). A fall in member count from 30 to 27 or fewer (Oct 15).
**The move you'd never see coming:** The rival offers your members firing-only service while the electric kiln is dead, so your 30 members never need to come back, and the original studio is the casualty.
**What this means you should change:** Fix members' firing first and tell them the date. Price the pilot against $295, not $340, and put the firing promise in writing.
