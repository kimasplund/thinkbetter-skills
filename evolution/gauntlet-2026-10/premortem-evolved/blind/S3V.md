# Premortem: Saltmarsh Clay second space (8-week beginner wheel courses, Corvel Bay)
**Horizon:** Dec 25 · **Success was defined as:** "the new space covering its own costs by Christmas without pulling money from the original studio"
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure ("if this dies, this is what killed it"), not the probability the plan fails overall. They sum to ~100. **Overall chance this misses its success definition by Dec 25:** ~90%, mostly because of the arithmetic in #1.
**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- You gave no past project to compare against, so the base rates below are judgment calls and labeled as such.
- Each cohort meets once a week for 8 weeks, so the four cohorts run Monday to Thursday in parallel. Rounds start Aug 4, Sep 29 and Nov 24, and students pay at enrollment.
- Fit-out (ten wheels, shelving, a gas-kiln hood and piping, clay-trap sinks, electrical) is not in your $9,400. I assumed $15,000 to $25,000. Please replace this with a real quote.
- The $9,400 starts when rent starts (Aug 2) and the $4,100 rent is flat for five years. The personal guarantee is uncapped and does not burn off.
- Member fees at the original studio are already in that studio's income. Moving their firing across town earns the new space nothing.

## The Autopsy
**The strongest case for this plan:** You already have 30 members paying monthly, so local appetite for clay is proven, not hoped for. A free try-the-wheel booth produced 140 names, which is a warm list most startups never get. Beginner courses are the funnel that feeds memberships, and the original studio's fixed costs are already covered, so every graduate who joins is close to pure margin. Two rent-free months give you time to fit out. A kiln you own also fixes the original studio's dying kiln. A smart advocate would say the downside is bounded (about $22,000 of savings) and the upside is a second site with a pipeline.

Seven ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The $13,600 was an 8-week number compared with a one-month cost, so the space lost money even when every seat sold
**Probability:** ~30% — the plan misses its own target on its own inputs. The share is held at 30 because price and cohort count could still have been fixed before signing.
**Cause of death:** $13,600 per round is about $7,360 a month (8 weeks is 1.85 months). Against $9,400 that is a $2,040 monthly loss at 100% fill, and break-even needs ~128% fill, which ten wheels per night cannot supply. At 75% fill the monthly loss is about $3,880.
**How it unfolded:**
- June: You signed on June 2 with the sentence "comfortably in the black" never rerun as a monthly figure. The five-year personal guarantee carries about $246,000 of rent, and the kiln loan sits on top of it.
- August: Rent started Aug 2. Round one enrolled and $9,500 to $13,600 arrived in a lump, so the bank balance looked healthy while the monthly run-rate was already negative. That lump is why nobody looked at the run-rate.
- October: Round two's lump covered September's and October's costs, and the same cash-balance reading kept the problem invisible.
- November: Round three's enrollment ran into the holidays. Costs from Aug 2 to Dec 25 were about $45,100 against at most $40,800 of revenue at 100% fill (about $30,600 at 75%).
- December: The new space covered roughly 55% to 75% of its costs. Fit-out had already used most of the $22,000 cushion, so the gap was met from the original studio's account. The success definition broke in December, and the lease stayed on through year five.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "four full cohorts against $9,400" was a like-for-like comparison.
**First warning sign:** visible today. A monthly P&L at 100% fill shows -$2,040. After signing, the first warning is August's net, which arrives after the point of no return.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** second sites of membership studios usually die on the fixed-cost ramp, not on teaching quality (judgment, weakly sourced). This plan differs in that $9,400 of fixed cost sits on only 40 seats per round, which pushes the share up.

### 2. The 140-name waitlist was a free-booth sheet, and it did not convert at the rate round one needed
**Probability:** ~20% — a name on a sheet at a free event is Accept-level commitment (agrees, then silence does the killing), and the price was never tested.
**Cause of death:** Round one needed 40 paid seats, which is 29% of the list. Three rounds by Dec 25 need 120 seats, which is 86% of the list with no new marketing. A realistic conversion from a free-activity sheet is 5% to 15% (judgment, weakly sourced).
**How it unfolded:**
- June: You emailed the list. About a quarter opened it, and a handful asked about dates.
- July: By July 7 about 10 had paid. The Harbor Days names were already weeks old and mostly lacked a weekday-evening habit.
- August: Two cohorts were merged and two were cancelled. Rent started on schedule, and refunds for the cancelled nights went out of the $22,000.
- September: Round two had no list left to sell to. Word of mouth from a single cohort of 10 is thin.
- December: Average fill was about 35% to 50%, and the monthly loss was $5,000 or more.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a sign-up sheet measures willingness to pay $340.
**First warning sign:** fewer than 20 paid seats by May 26, or fewer than 28 by July 7.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** free-event sign-up lists convert at single-digit to low-double-digit rates (weakly sourced). This list is stronger than most because it came from a hands-on activity in your own town and your members vouch for you. It is also weaker than most because it is a month stale, and its price was never tested. Net: roughly neutral, so the number stays at ~20.

### 3. (Compound) The meter slipped past Aug 4 just as the original electric kiln died, so for weeks no kiln anywhere could fire anything
**Probability:** ~15% — the plan's own 10 to 14 weeks from June 2 lands Aug 11 to Sep 8, already after Aug 4, and the dependency was never confirmed with the utility.
**Cause of death:** a dependency with a lead time over two weeks, treated as one week, combined with a decision not to replace a kiln already known to be dying.
**How it unfolded:**
- June: You applied for the meter on June 2. The utility wanted the lessee's account details and a load calculation, so the clock started two weeks late.
- August: Round one began Aug 4. Students threw pots and nothing could be fired. Pieces stacked on shelves with no date for the first bisque.
- September: The original kiln's element failed around mid-September, since the plan assumed it would last while the new one ran. Thirty members had nowhere to fire for about six weeks. Four to six of them cancelled, which is lost income at the studio that pays your salary.
- October: The meter went live around Oct 14, which is 19 weeks and consistent with a 2x slip. The kiln then needed commissioning and a gas inspection, so the first bisque fired in late October.
- November: Round one's students, who paid in August, had gone two months without a finished pot. Refund requests arrived and word of mouth turned negative just as round three was being sold.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the utility's range was a commitment and that the old kiln would hold until the new one ran.
**First warning sign:** no written energization date from the utility by June 16, or any irregular firing at the old kiln.
**Existing guard:** none.

### 4. The classes filled and the kiln could not keep up
**Probability:** ~10% — the plan counted loads, not firings per piece. Members make ~60 pieces a week and 40 beginners add 80 to 120, so 140 to 180 pieces a week need two firings each (bisque, then glaze).
**Cause of death:** capacity of 3 loads x 70 = 210 piece-firings a week is 105 finished pieces a week. The shortfall is 35 to 75 pieces every week. Even at 3.5 cycles a week, capacity is 122 finished pieces. (Glaze loads usually hold fewer pieces than bisque loads, so the real figure may be worse; I could not verify that from the plan.)
**How it unfolded:**
- September: After a repricing and a better-organized sales push, round two filled. The kiln was live and the three-load schedule ran.
- October: Backlog grew by 35 to 75 pieces a week. By week 4 of the round, glaze turnaround had stretched past two weeks.
- November: Students' pieces waited four to six weeks. Members' pieces were bumped for students' (or the reverse), and either group had a grievance.
- December: The pieces students wanted as holiday gifts were not ready on Dec 24. Re-enrollment and member retention both dipped at the exact moment the model needed them.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a load is a piece count rather than half of a piece's journey.
**First warning sign:** a bisque queue above 50 pieces by week 3 of the first full round.
**Existing guard:** none. The plan has no second kiln or outsourcing option.

### 5. The fit-out and the permits were never priced, so the room cost more and opened later than the plan said
**Probability:** ~10% — the plan lists rent, loan, insurance, utilities and an instructor, and nothing for the room itself or the city.
**Cause of death:** a gas kiln needs a hood or ventilation, gas piping, a clearance and inspection, usually a use-permit and fire-marshal sign-off for class occupancy, plus ten wheels, clay-trap sinks, electrical and shelving. I assumed $15,000 to $25,000.
**How it unfolded:**
- June: Quotes arrived above what the two rent-free months could absorb, and fit-out began.
- July: The city asked for a change-of-use review for class occupancy. The inspection slot was three weeks out.
- August: The room opened late. Rent started Aug 2 regardless of readiness, and about $5,000 to $10,000 of the cushion was already spent.
- September: Savings fell below $10,000 with no revenue stable yet.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "fit it out" is a free-rent activity rather than a five-figure budget line with a permitting step.
**First warning sign:** written quotes above $12,000 by May 26, or no permit confirmation by then.
**Existing guard:** none.

### 6. The owner ran out of hours
**Probability:** ~10% — a mundane base-rate killer. You run the original studio, fit out a room, teach or supervise four evenings, and drive a load across town three times a week.
**Cause of death:** the only person who can load, fire and unload a gas kiln is also the only person who can be in two places. There is no backup.
**How it unfolded:**
- August: Adrenaline carried the first weeks.
- September: Evenings at the new space overlapped with member needs at the original. Load, fire and unload took about 10 hours a week of your time on top of everything else.
- October: Illness cost one firing day. Backlog from #4 was already growing, so this added to it.
- November: Hours were above 55 a week for two months. Motivation fell, which is the usual months 2 to 4 failure for second sites.
- December: Decisions became reactive, and the January decision (whether to extend) was made tired.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the owner's hours are a free input.
**First warning sign:** two consecutive weeks above 55 hours, or any missed firing day.
**Existing guard:** a part-time instructor, which covers teaching but not the kiln.

### 7. The frame's blind spot: nobody in this plan acts
**Probability:** ~5% — something outside the frame that I can't yet turn into a mechanism. The plan contains no rival, landlord, regulator or customer who responds to anything. The adversary section tests this.
**Cause of death:** unresolved. The adversary section below names the likeliest candidate.
**How it unfolded:**
- Unknown. The scenario here would be some actor reacting to your move in a way the plan never imagined.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the market stands still while you build.
**First warning sign:** a rival listing or price you didn't expect, by the tripwire dates below.

**Where the numbers tie:** #4, #5 and #6 all sit at ~10, so each pair needs a written tie-break.
- #4 above #5: #4 is partly arithmetic I can already do from your own numbers (105 finished pieces against 140 to 180), while #5 depends on quotes I have not seen.
- #4 above #6: the kiln shortfall happens even with a flawless owner, whereas #6 needs a stressed or sick one.
- #5 above #6: #5 fires before the doors open, and #6 fires only after months of strain, so #5 has more chances to occur.

**Considered and cut:** rent-escalator clause (folded into #1), kiln loan terms or cross-default (folded into #1), August seasonal trough (folded into #2), landlord objecting to the gas hood (folded into #5).

## The Rebuild
**The revised plan:** Do not sign on June 2 as written. Presell the first round at your price before signing, negotiate the lease so that rent starts when the gas meter is live (and the guarantee is capped and burns off), keep the old kiln alive or contract outside firing as a bridge, and rebuild the schedule to about seven cohorts including weekends. At $340 and 70% fill, seven cohorts earn about $9,000 a month, which is break-even and not profit, so reprice toward $380 or trim the fixed costs. Open on the date the meter is live, not Aug 4.

**What changed and why:**
| Change | Failure mode it closes | What it costs | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landlord: rent starts 60 days after meter energization, guarantee capped at 6 months of rent and burning off at month 12, 2+3 year term with exit | #1, #3, #5 | Possible rent increase of $200 to $400 a month, or the landlord says no (which is itself the answer) | Launch-Blocking, agreed in writing by May 26 or the signing moves |

**Response type for the most dangerous mode (#1):** prevent. The signature is the entry path, and the changes above close it before it happens. Each fix costs less than the roughly $2,000 to $4,000 a month it prevents, except the weekend cohorts (more teaching hours), which are justified by the irreversibility of the guarantee.
**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **Rerun the break-even.** Check by building a monthly P&L with revenue = paid seats x price / 1.85 and costs = $9,400 plus fit-out amortized. Walk away if it shows a loss at 70% fill with the cohorts you can actually staff. Falsifier: fewer than 20 paid seats by May 26.
2. **Get the utility's energization date in writing.** Ask the utility for a written estimate on the lessee's application, using the lease LOI. Walk away if the written date is later than Sep 8 with no firing bridge. Falsifier: no written date by June 16.
3. **Price the room.** Collect three written quotes and a permit confirmation. Walk away if the total fit-out exceeds $12,000 above what the landlord contributes, or if the use permit is not available within six weeks. Falsifier: quotes above $12,000 by May 26.
4. **Convert the waitlist to paper.** Presell with a refundable deposit rather than a reply. Walk away if fewer than 20 pay.
5. **Insurance binder in hand.** Request the binder for a gas kiln and class activity before the signature. Walk away if it excludes either.

**Decision rule:** SIGN IF the landlord agrees in writing to the capped, burning-off guarantee and rent starting at energization, AND at least 20 seats are prepaid by May 26, AND the monthly model breaks even at 70% fill with the cohorts you can staff, AND a firing bridge is in place. ELSE do not sign on June 2: run a pilot course at the original studio, and sign by Sep 1 for a January start.
**If the decision rule lands on delay — the reverse autopsy:** it is now Dec 25 and we waited, and it was a mistake.
- A rival opened a beginner series in the autumn and took the list you would have converted.
- The landlord leased the space to someone else, and the next site cost 20% more.
- The old kiln failed anyway in October, and without a new one members left, which is what the bridge was meant to prevent.
**What tips the balance:** 28 prepaid seats by June 1 plus a landlord who accepts the cap makes the ELSE branch moot, so sign.
**Consciously accepted:** I leave one risk unmitigated, the gas-kiln safety incident, because a licensed fitter and an inspection are already the guard and the residual odds are low. The priced cost is the incident-response insurance premium you already carry, which is why it sits in the downgraded table.
**How the rebuilt plan dies:** the fixes introduce a new weakest joint, the landlord. If the landlord says no to the energization-linked rent, you either sign the bad lease anyway or lose the site, and sunk effort tempts the first. Also, the weekend cohorts rest on the same untested demand as #2.
**Residual risk:** the waitlist may simply be small. If 20 seats never arrive, no lease structure fixes that.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** the nearest other provider of beginner wheel classes in town, assumed to be a community arts center or another studio with a working kiln. They gain from your gap year: they have a kiln, an existing audience, tolerance for thin margins, and a best alternative that costs them almost nothing. What they can't do is match your members' sense of ownership.
**The kill chain:**
- June: Probe. They see your lease announcement and your Harbor Days photos, and note the Aug 4 start.
- July 7: Position. They post a beginner series with an early-bird price near $280 starting the same week.
- Aug 4 to Sep 8: Strike. They message the same free-booth names with a "fired and ready in two weeks" promise, aimed at your kiln gap.
- Oct: Entrench. They offer your members open-kiln firing at a low per-piece fee while your old kiln is down.
- Nov: Lock in. A holiday gift voucher targets the same families your round three needed.
**Detection opportunities:** (1) a rival listing or price appearing by July 7; (2) waitlist replies citing another class, any time June to Sept; (3) a member mentioning another studio's firing price during the kiln gap; (4) your instructor being approached.
**The move you'd never see coming:** they don't compete for your students. They take your members, because the old kiln is dying and you chose not to replace it. The members are the 30 paying relationships, and they leave first.
**What this means you should change:** bridge the firing gap before anything else, and don't announce an Aug 4 date until the meter has one in writing.
