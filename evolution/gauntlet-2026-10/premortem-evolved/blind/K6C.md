## The Autopsy

**The strongest case for this plan:** A regional chain came to you, which means a buyer already likes the product and is not being talked into it. Each jar costs $3.40 landed and sells to Calloway for $5.40, a 37% contribution margin that does not depend on your own labor. At the buyer's pace, 144 shelf positions sell 864 jars a week, about $20k a month, more than double today's $9k, so "biggest account by September" is close to guaranteed on volume alone. Halvard turns a kitchen-limited business into one that can fill a chain, and its 50% deposit is gentler than full prepayment. The $8.99 shelf price sits inside the premium-condiment band, and an 11-week runway from now to signing leaves time to prepare. A smart advocate would say that the downside is a manageable fee and the upside is a business that stops being a weekend job.

The most firmly stated claim in the brief, "deliver to the warehouse in late February," was attacked first. It fails on your own figures: January 4 plus the 2-3 week trial sign-off plus the 6-week lead time puts finished goods at Halvard between March 1 and March 8, before freight, receiving, or reaching a single store (see #2).

Seven ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The cash ran out at the second production run, because Net-60 and the slotting deduction meant Calloway paid nothing until the stock it had sold was already gone
**Probability:** ~30% — the arithmetic is on the page: about $93k went out before the first usable Calloway cash, against $60k of capital. A reference class (small food brands moving from direct sales to grocery run short on the second production run, not the first) points the same way, weakly sourced. The disanalogy: your 50/50 deposit split is gentler than prepayment, which pushes the number down, while Net-60 plus netted slotting pushes it up more.
**Cause of death:** The first run (15,000 jars, $46,500) took 78% of your capital. The success case, with stores selling at the buyer's pace, forced a second $46,500 order before any Calloway cash arrived.
**How it unfolded:**
- January: the $23,250 deposit went to Halvard on January 4, leaving $36,750 of the $60k. Winter market sales were below the $9k average.
- March: Halvard's balance of $23,250 plus about $4,500 of freight came due on delivery, leaving about $9k.
- March to May: Calloway's first invoices were about $4,666 a week. The $36,000 of slotting and about $9,300 of promo bill-backs consumed roughly the first 9.7 weeks of invoices, so the first remittance in early May showed $0 and a deduction schedule.
- May: sell-through at the buyer's pace meant the first run was gone around July 1. A six-week lead time put the reorder date in early to mid May. The deposit was $23,250 and the account held about $9k.
- June: you funded one flavor out of three. Two flavors stocked out in late June.
- July: the first usable Calloway cash arrived around July 21, after the stockouts. The buyer had already logged the out-of-stocks ahead of the summer reset.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a signed retail account funds itself the way a market stall does, with cash arriving as jars sell.
**First warning sign:** the cash schedule in December, which nobody built, and then the first remittance advice in early May.

### 2. March 1 was written into the Calloway agreement, but the party that controlled the date, Halvard, was never bound to it
**Probability:** ~20% — the delay itself was near-certain by your own numbers, but it kills only through the buyer's reaction and the sunk deposit. Reference class: first runs with a new co-packer slip more often than not (weakly sourced). The disanalogy: your plan misses March 1 with zero slippage, which pushes the share above what the class alone would give.
**Cause of death:** An agreement dated to March 1 for 48 stores sat on top of a supply chain whose best case was already past that date, with no matching date in the Halvard order and no stated remedy from Calloway for lateness.
**How it unfolded:**
- December 15: signed, with March 1 in the agreement.
- January: the order went in on January 4. The trial batch took the full three weeks and one resample, so the run was scheduled for January 25.
- February: Halvard confirmed finished goods for about March 8. Nobody told Calloway until late February, because there was no contractual date to tell them about.
- March: Calloway reset the shelves on schedule and put up empty shelf tags. Jars reached the warehouse around March 12 and the stores around March 22.
- April: the buyer, who had tied the launch to the March reset, put the launch on probation. The 8-week promo clock and the first invoice both moved, and the balance already paid to Halvard could not.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "6 weeks" and "2-3 weeks" were commitments rather than typical figures, and that being first in line at Halvard was not a position anyone had asked about.
**First warning sign:** the written finished-goods date from Halvard, which could have been requested in October, or a second trial round in early January.

### 3. Even at the buyer's pace, the entry costs pushed payback past September
**Probability:** ~15% — the arithmetic on your own terms misses the date, but a lenient reading of "profitable" (a monthly view) would not call it a failure. The weakly sourced pattern is that grocery entry costs take longer to recover than founders expect.
**Cause of death:** $51,200 of up-front costs ($36,000 slotting, $9,300 promo, $5,900 of free jars at landed cost) set against a $2.00 margin per jar.
**How it unfolded:**
- Late March to mid-May: the 8 promo weeks sold about 6,900 jars, contributing $13,800 before entry costs. After those costs the program stood at about -$37,400.
- Mid-May onward: full-price weeks added about $1,730 each, so payback needed another 21.6 weeks. Started March 1, that is September 27. Started March 22, it is mid-October.
- September 30: the program was still about $4,000 short of recovering its entry costs, at full buyer pace and before the 60-day cash lag.
- September: the books showed the strongest Calloway month yet, and the whole business was still below break-even for the quarter after kitchen rent and line interest.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that wholesale margin per jar describes the business, when the fixed entry costs describe it.
**First warning sign:** the cumulative program line in mid-May, which was already worse than the model assumed if the promo was billed on shelf price.

### 4. The buyer's "about 6" was an expectation, not data, and the unsold inventory carried 78% of the capital
**Probability:** ~10% — most velocity shortfalls are booked under #3 (slow payback) and #1 (cash), so this rung holds only the cases where stranded stock and credits were the proximate killer. The share of new regional specialty SKUs that run under buyer forecasts is weakly sourced.
**Cause of death:** Expired and unsold jars credited back at full wholesale while $46,500 sat in a 6-month-life product.
**How it unfolded:**
- April: weeks 1-3 averaged about 3.5 jars per slot per week. The weakest flavor ran about 2.
- May to June: Calloway's warehouse orders reflected store pulls, but you were reading warehouse orders, not store scans, and read the slowdown as a reordering lag.
- July: the buyer cut the weakest flavor's facings at the summer reset. About 3,700 jars were left, with five weeks of life.
- August: a 30-day pull date made that stock unsellable at Calloway, and the expired product on shelves was credited back at $5.40 a jar. The markets could absorb some jars, about 800 a month at best, and displaced kitchen output.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a buyer's volume expectation behaves like a purchase order.
**First warning sign:** store scan data by week 3 (week of April 12), or a four-store shelf trial before signing.

### 5. The slip and the cash gap compounded through the one cash source you had
**Probability:** ~10% — the two modes are the most likely pair to co-occur, and the interaction is the mechanism, not just both happening.
**Cause of death:** A late delivery landed exactly on the weakest cash point, and the fix consumed the hours that fed the only fast-cash channel.
**How it unfolded:**
- Early March: finished goods slipped to March 8 and the balance and freight left about $9k. The farmers-market lull gave no cushion.
- Late March to April: late shipments, receiving appointments, store-level out-of-stocks and the first deduction codes took two to three days a week. The first market weekends of the spring season were thin or skipped.
- April to May: market and online revenue fell from the assumed $9k run-rate to about $6k. (This is the weakest link in the chain: plausible at about 60%, since the drop depends on how much of the season the two of you actually missed.)
- May: the first remittance came back $0 and the reorder deposit was out of reach.
- June to July: the stockouts of #1 followed, with less cash in hand to absorb them.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the two of you are free to run a 48-store launch and still run the markets.
**First warning sign:** weekly market and online revenue below about 75% of the same week a year earlier in the weeks of March 29 and April 12.

### 6. A 48-store account arrived with obligations that were never in the brief
**Probability:** ~10% — vendor paperwork and deductions are routine for any chain, but nothing in the brief priced them. Reference class unavailable, labeled a judgment call.
**Cause of death:** Unpriced obligations: product liability insurance with Calloway as additional insured, UPC barcodes on the label, a recall plan, an invoice portal, deduction reconciliation, and a price gap against your own channels.
**How it unfolded:**
- December: Calloway's vendor packet (not in the brief) listed insurance, UPC and recall requirements. The label artwork was already sent to print without a UPC.
- January: a reprint pushed the Halvard run back about a week.
- March to June: promo bill-backs and damage credits arrived as coded deductions. Nobody reconciled them. By June about 3% of invoice value (weakly sourced estimate) had been paid without dispute.
- Spring: market and online customers found the $8.99 shelf price below your own price (assumed $11-12) and moved their purchases to Calloway at a $2 margin instead of a roughly $8 one.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the contract terms you were sent are the whole cost of the account.
**First warning sign:** the vendor packet itself, requested in October, or the first remittance with deduction codes you cannot match to the contract.

### 7. The model of the deal was built from a term sheet, and what the shelf actually looked like was never seen
**Probability:** ~5% — a frame blind spot, not a mechanism I can trace yet. Flagged as a judgment call.
**Cause of death:** Store-level execution in 48 stores: which aisle the jars sat in, who restocked them, how many stores were out of one flavor at any time. Warehouse-level data hid it.
**How it unfolded:**
- Week 2: spot visits would have shown jars in the wrong aisle next to $4 condiments and no one assigned to restock.
- Weeks 3 to 8: a quarter of stores averaged half the pace of the rest, and the average still looked acceptable.
- Summer: the weak stores dragged the average toward #4.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that velocity is a property of the product rather than of 144 separate shelf positions.
**First warning sign:** a week-2 visit to eight stores.

**Tie-break (the three modes at 10%):** #4 outranks #5 because #4 is a standalone cause with a larger direct loss ($46,500 of inventory), while #5 leans on a link at about 60% plausibility. #4 outranks #6 because #6 is routine and recoverable, while #4 is not recoverable once the order is paid. #5 outranks #6 because #5 can end the business through cash, while #6 mostly drains margin.

## The Rebuild
**The revised plan:** Do not sign the agreement as drafted on December 15. Before then: ask Calloway for a four-store, four-week shelf trial in November with your own jars; start Halvard's trial batch now; build the cash schedule; and ask for five changes to the terms (below). Sign, with a start date tied to Halvard's written finished-goods date, only if the decision rule passes.

**What changed and why:**
| Change | Failure mode it closes | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Launch-Blocking: Four-store, three-flavor shelf trial, November 9 to December 6, own-kitchen jars, weekly store scans from the buyer. Decision by December 10. | #4, #3 | About 330 jars at landed cost, roughly $1,100, plus about 20 hours |
| Launch-Blocking: Start Halvard's trial batch now and get a written finished-goods date for an order on January 4. Decision by November 20, or the start date moves to April 1. | #2, #5 | A trial batch fee (get a quote); if Halvard requires a signed order first, the $23,250 deposit moves earlier |
| Launch-Blocking: Calloway's start date set to Halvard's written finished-goods date plus 3 weeks, in the agreement, with no lateness fines before it. | #2, #5 | 2-5 weeks of Calloway sales, about $3,500-8,600 of contribution |
| Launch-Blocking: Slotting phased (for example one-third at launch, one-third at week 8, one-third at week 16) or waived on the trial flavor; promo capped at 4 weeks or a dollar ceiling; Net-30 on first orders. | #1, #3 | Negotiating capital; the risk Calloway says no. These asks are the point of the decision rule |
| Launch-Blocking: Credits for expired or damaged product capped at an agreed percent of sales, with 60-day minimum dating at pull. | #4, #6 | Same as above |

| Launch-Blocking: UPC, insurance quote, recall plan and Calloway's vendor packet in hand by December 10, before the artwork goes to print. | #6 | $1-2k a year (weakly sourced; get quotes) |
| Track: Weekly store scans, deduction ledger, and a store-visit routine in week 2. | #4, #6, #7 | About 3 hours a week |

**Response labels for the most dangerous modes:** #4 is **limit**, because the likelihood cannot move without data: the trial and the capped credits contain the loss. #1 and #5 are **prevent**: the funding and the start-date change close the entry path.

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **Velocity.** What: sell-through at $8.99 without promo. How: the November shelf trial, weekly scans from the buyer. Walk away if the average is below 4 jars per flavor per store per week; between 4 and 5, renegotiate to a smaller footprint. Falsifier of "6 a week": fewer than 5 average over the last three trial weeks.
2. **Halvard's date.** What: written finished-goods date and trial sign-off. How: request now. Walk away if finished goods fall later than March 12 for a January 4 order. A date later than February 26 moves the start to April 1. Falsifier of "late February": any written date after February 26.
3. **Cash peak.** What: week-by-week cash with Halvard run 1 and run 2, Calloway deductions and Net-60. How: a spreadsheet from your own figures. Walk away if committed funding does not cover the peak need (about $93k as written, against $60k) with a $10k margin, unless the renegotiated terms bring the peak under what you hold. Falsifier of "$60k is enough": peak need above cash plus committed credit.
4. **Terms on paper.** What: written purchase orders for the first 12 weeks, deduction definitions, delisting notice, any late-shipment fines. How: Calloway's vendor agreement, read in full. Walk away from uncapped credits or any fine not disclosed.
5. **Vendor paperwork.** What: insurance with Calloway as additional insured, UPC, recall plan. How: quotes by December 1. Walk away if insurance cannot be bound by December 10.

**Decision rule:** Sign on December 15 and place the Halvard order IF (a) the November trial averaged at least 5 jars per flavor per store per week, AND (b) Halvard committed in writing to finished goods by February 26 or Calloway's start date is at least 3 weeks after Halvard's written date, AND (c) committed funding plus the renegotiated cash terms keep peak need below money in hand with a $10k margin by February 1. ELSE: do not place the Halvard order. Run a 12-store pilot from your own kitchen starting in April, and go to Halvard after 8 weeks of store scans at 5 or better. If your kitchen cannot make about 900 jars a month, decline now and revisit at Calloway's fall reset.

**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** The horizon passed and we did not do it, and it turned out to be a mistake. Calloway gave the shelf to a competing chili crisp at the March reset and the next category review was a year away. The terms were the best on offer, and a second chain wanted the same fees later. The own-kitchen pilot capped at 12 stores because of hours, and the buyer lost patience.
**What tips the balance:** a trial average of 7 or more jars per store per flavor, with Calloway agreeing to phase the slotting over 16 weeks, moves the call to sign for all 48 stores on the original footprint, with standby funding committed by February 1.
**Consciously accepted (at most one):** single-source dependence on Halvard for all three flavors. A failure there would cost six to eight weeks of supply, about $28-37k of invoiced sales and $10-14k of contribution, against the cost of qualifying a second co-packer (a second trial batch and new minimums). Accepted until the first reorder cycle completes.
**How the rebuilt plan dies:** a four-store trial in Calloway's most favorable stores can overstate pace across 48. The five asks together can cost the deal, since the buyer may say "March 1 or nothing." The standby funding could cost more in fees than the cash it provides if drawn for long.
**Residual risk:** Calloway remains about 70% of revenue at launch, the unit pace is still unproven at 48-store scale, and retailer deductions remain a recurring leak.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** a better-funded chili-crisp brand with national distribution and a broker. It gains your 144 facings in a premium condiment set and a category relationship at Calloway. It has trade-spend budgets, free fills, and can pay slotting without cash stress. It cannot move before Calloway's next category review and cannot copy a local founder story. Its best alternative is a different regional chain, which costs it a year.
**The kill chain:**
- January to February 2027, probe: the rival's broker learns of the March reset from the buyer and asks for a meeting.
- March 22 to May 16, position: during your 8 promo weeks the rival funds a deeper temporary price cut on adjacent jars and takes an end-cap in some stores.
- June to July, strike: your cash trough from #1 stocks out two flavors. The rival offers to cover the gap with no slotting, ahead of the July reset decisions.
- August to September, entrench: the rival holds the facings you lost, and your expired-product credits make the account look weak.
**Detection opportunities:**
- A buyer request for a competitive bid or your out-of-stock log, February to June.
- A rival price at or below $7.99, or an end-cap in 10 or more stores, in the weeks of April 18 and May 2.
- A store-brand chili crisp appearing in the set, or a buyer request for your specs or cost breakdown, at the June and September resets.
**The move you'd never see coming:** Calloway sources a store-brand version using the sell-through data your launch generated, possibly from a co-packer like Halvard, which already holds your recipe and specs.
**What this means you should change:** add confidentiality and non-use of your recipe to the Halvard contract; ask Calloway for a 60-day notice before delisting or launching a competing store brand; and set a hard rule that no flavor goes out of stock.
