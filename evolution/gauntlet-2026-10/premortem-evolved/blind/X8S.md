# Premortem: Tidewell x Calloway Markets (48 stores, 3 flavors, March 1 start)
**Horizon:** Sep 30, 2027 · **Success was defined as:** "by September, Calloway is our biggest account and the business is profitable"
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure, meaning "if this dies, this is what killed it". They are not the probability the plan fails overall. They sum to ~100.
**Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** ~65% as written. Most of that comes from the first two modes, which are arithmetic on your own terms.
**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- You gave no past launch to anchor on, so the reference classes below are mine and are marked where weakly sourced.
- Your average jar sells for about $11 direct, so $9k a month is roughly 800 jars a month across all channels.
- The 25% promo is 25% off the $8.99 shelf price, so you fund $2.25 a jar sold. If it is 25% off your $5.40 wholesale, it is $1.35 a jar. Your terms don't say which.
- The slotting fee is one-time, not annual.
- Net-60 runs from the invoice date. Slotting and promo bill-backs are netted against your invoices as Calloway pays them.
- Calloway replenishes weekly as the free opening case sells through, so paid invoices start in about week 3.
- Halvard makes only the Calloway product. Your kitchen and markets keep supplying the core business, which covers its own costs and your pay but adds nothing to the Calloway float.
- Halvard's 5,000-jar minimum applies to every order, and the deposit is not refundable once the run is scheduled.
- Stores are live about March 9–16 (explained under mode 3). The buyer's 6 jars per store per flavor per week is a forecast, not a purchase order.

## The Autopsy

**The strongest case for this plan:** A 48-store regional chain is offering all three of your flavors a shelf, and chains don't do that for products that look weak. The buyer has category data and still expects 6 jars per store per flavor per week. At that rate you sell 864 jars a week, about $20k a month of wholesale revenue. That is more than twice your whole business today. You keep $2.00 a jar after Halvard and freight ($5.40 - $3.10 - $0.30), about $7,500 a month of contribution at run rate. Slotting and promo are netted from invoices rather than paid up front, so none of the fee cash leaves your account on day one. Halvard removes the hand-cooking ceiling, and a Calloway shelf is proof you can show the next chain. A smart advocate would say this is how a $9k-a-month farmers-market brand becomes a real one, and the terms, while heavy, are not unusual for grocery.

**The assumption you stated with the most certainty, attacked first:** "Deliver to the warehouse in late February." Your own inputs don't produce that date. Ordering January 5, the trial batch adds 2–3 weeks before Halvard schedules you (scheduled Jan 19–26). Six weeks of lead time then puts finished goods at March 2–9, after the March 1 start and before any warehouse-to-store transit. Late February only works if the trial batch runs in parallel with production, and your terms say it comes first. That is mode 3 below. The date survives only if the trial is done before the order.

Seven ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The deductions and net-60 held your cash hostage, and you could not fund the first reorder
**Probability:** ~30%, because this is your own terms run forward and fires even if every other mode stays quiet.
**Reference class:** small specialty-food brands entering grocery tend to run short of working capital before the retailer's money starts arriving. This is weakly sourced, and I have no dated base rate to quote. **Disanalogy:** your slotting is deducted, not prepaid, which pushes the number down. Halvard's 50% deposit and 5,000-jar minimum are prepaid cash, which pushes it up. Net, the number holds.
**Cause of death:** $60k of savings and credit line against about $102k of Halvard cash through mid-June, with no net Calloway dollar until early August.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jan: the first order is 15,000 jars at $3.10, $46,500. The deposit of $23,250 left on Jan 5 and cash fell to $36,750.
- Feb: the $23,250 balance and about $4,500 of freight were due at delivery. By the end of February you held about $9,000, including every dollar of remaining credit line.
- Mar–Apr: sales ran on plan, 864 jars a week. Invoices started in week 3, but Calloway netted $36,000 of slotting and about $15,550 of promo bill-backs against them. The first payments, due mid-May, were wiped out. The cumulative deductions weren't covered until about week 13 of invoicing, so the first net dollar landed in early August.
- May: the reorder had to be placed by about May 4 to land before the shelf emptied in late June. Its deposit was $23,250 against $9,000 in the bank. You were $14k short on the day, and the full second bill ($51k with freight) put the trough near -$42k by mid-June.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a deal with deductions instead of cash fees is a deal with no cash problem.
**Existing guard:** none. The $25k credit line is already inside the $60k.
**First warning sign:** the 13-week cash forecast on Jan 5, the day of the deposit, shows the low point under $10k by Feb 27 and negative by May.

### 2. The shelf took fewer jars than the buyer forecast, and 15,000 jars outlived their date
**Probability:** ~20%, because 6 jars per store per flavor per week is about 4.6 times your entire current volume, and selling through 15,000 jars inside a six-month life needs an average of 4.0.
**Reference class:** new specialty and local condiments in regional grocery often settle below the buyer's opening forecast. This is weakly sourced, with no dated figure. **Disanalogy:** 48 stores and an 8-week promo push your early velocity up. The cliff when the price goes from $6.74 back to $8.99 pushes the full-price rate down, and you have no sampler on a shelf the way you do at a market. Net, the number holds.
**Cause of death:** Halvard's minimum bought 15,000 jars against a demand that was never measured, and the shelf life ran out before they sold.
**How it unfolded:**
- Mar: promo-price sales ran around 4 per store per flavor per week. The buyer was satisfied and you read it as confirmation.
- May: the promo ended May 4–11. Full-price velocity fell to around 2.5. By then the 15,000 jars were paid for, so no data could have changed the quantity.
- Jun–Jul: stock didn't move. About 10,400 jars sat after eight weeks, roughly 40 weeks of cover against a 26-week life. Calloway asked for a promo extension or fewer facings, and near-date jars began coming back as credits at $5.40.
- Aug–Sep: the first lot crossed its six-month date. About 3,900 jars were unsold, $13k at cost. Calloway's wholesale ran about $8.4k a month, still below your $9k core. Calloway had paid $0 net by Sep 30, and your cumulative Calloway position was about -$47k with the write-off.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the buyer's forecast is a measurement.
**Existing guard:** none. The six-month life and the 5,000 minimum work against you here.
**First warning sign:** a week-3 scan average under 4.0 (week of Mar 29) at promo price, or any week of full-price velocity under 3.0 in mid-May.

### 3. The jars reached Calloway's dock after March 1 and the onboarding clock ran past the reset
**Probability:** ~15%, because the schedule already misses on your own numbers and the open question is only whether the slip is a nuisance or a kill.
**Cause of death:** a trial-batch-first rule on a first order, scheduled in January, with nothing in the plan for a failed trial.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jan 5: the order went in. The trial batch took 2–3 weeks, and your first chili crisp came back with the crisp softened or oil-heavy. The recipe needed a second pass.
- Late Jan–Feb: the second sign-off pushed scheduling to about Feb 9–16, and the six weeks ran to Mar 23–Apr 6. Halvard's holiday backlog made a further week plausible.
- Mar 1: Calloway's set went in without you, or with a shortened fill. Your terms don't say whether the promo runs from March 1 or from first sale. If it is calendar-dated, you funded weeks with no product on shelf.
- Apr: the buyer moved your space to the next vendor in the category, and the opening case, slotting, and promo timing were all renegotiated from a weaker position.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "6 weeks" means six weeks from the order date.
**Existing guard:** none.
**First warning sign:** Halvard's written run date on Jan 5 is later than Jan 12, or a trial sample fails your own tasting before the order is placed.

### 4. Compound: the plan worked at 6 a week, the reorder could not be funded, and the shelf emptied
**Probability:** ~10%. It needs mode 1's cash gap together with demand at or above the forecast, which is half the demand outcomes.
**Cause of death:** the success case. A 6-week lead time and a $23,250 deposit met a shelf that sold at full buyer velocity.
**How it unfolded:**
- May 4: velocity held at 5.5–6 even after the promo. The reorder was due and the bank held $9,000.
- Late May: you borrowed $14k from family and credit cards and placed the order June 1. At six weeks the delivery landed July 13. Stock had run out around June 22.
- Jul: three weeks of empty or partial shelf. Calloway's system flagged the gaps, and the buyer's reset left room for one fewer flavor.
- Aug–Sep: net money from Calloway started in August, just after the delisting decision. You were neither the biggest account nor profitable.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that winning on velocity is the safe case.
**Existing guard:** none. Nothing in your plan schedules a reorder.
**First warning sign:** weeks of stock on hand (warehouse plus stores) under 9 with no funded order placed, checkable from about Apr 6.

### 5. Calloway's paper let it walk away and keep the credits
**Probability:** ~10%, because the terms you listed contain no Calloway commitment at all.

**How it unfolded:**

- May–Jun: six hours a week went into disputing deductions, and the promo bill-back ran against all units sold, not only the ones you shipped.
- Jul: a category review dropped two flavors. Calloway owed nothing for the cut, and the $36,000 of slotting was already deducted.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that wanting you in every store is a commitment to keep you there.
**Existing guard:** none in the terms you listed. I haven't seen the supply agreement, which may be better or worse.
**First warning sign:** the first remittance advice, about mid-May, with credits or deductions that don't match the signed schedule.

### 6. The core business thinned while both founders worked Calloway

**How it unfolded:**

**Existing guard:** none.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** Calloway's category buyer and private-label team. They gain a local brand at 40% shelf margin ($8.99 against $5.40), with the supplier funding promo, credits, and slotting. They hold daily scan data you don't see, and a 48-store chain can lose one chili crisp with no cost. They can't make a brand people trust quickly, and their best alternative is established chili-crisp brands already on shelf, or a private label at lower cost.
**The kill chain:**
1. **Dec 15–Jan, probe:** sign on Calloway's paper with no purchase commitment, and ask for your weekly velocity and your co-packer's name.
2. **Mar 9–16, position:** stores fill with 1,728 free jars. Calloway holds the opening case and starts netting $36,000 against your invoices.
3. **Late Apr–May, observe:** the promo ends, and the buyer reads full-price velocity before you do and asks for an extension, a lower price, or a smaller set.
4. **Jun–Jul, strike:** at the summer reset, Calloway cuts to one or two flavors, or prices a private label off your velocity, with nothing owed back.
5. **Aug–Sep, entrench:** the private label takes the facings.
**Detection opportunities:** (1) a request for your co-packer's name or recipe, any time before Jul 7. (2) The buyer sees velocity before you do, so you need weekly scan data in the contract by Dec 15. (3) A request for a price cut or promo extension, from May. (4) A facing cut or set change at the June 1 or Aug 1 review. (5) A Halvard request to make a similar product for any retailer, any time.
**The move you'd never see coming:** Calloway has your recipe-quality trial batch's specs, and Halvard, or another co-packer, makes a "Calloway Kitchen" chili crisp at about $4.20 wholesale, built from your three flavors and your sell-through. It needs no hostile act, just a buyer comparing your price to a cheaper copy.
**What this means you should change:** get weekly scan data and the delisting protection in the contract, and give the recipe only under a Halvard agreement that bars private-label work.
