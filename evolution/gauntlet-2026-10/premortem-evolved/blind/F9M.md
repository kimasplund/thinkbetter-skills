The plan has four serious problems, and three of them can't be fixed by working harder. Together they mean that "all 14 schools running in January and the year-two money released" is very unlikely as written. Most of this can still be fixed by renegotiating with Arbelle before the vote, so I'd treat the October 8 vote as "accept with changes" rather than yes or no.

I'm assuming your current four-school program is already funded, so your $150k reserve is available only for the expansion. I'm also assuming Arbelle's $600k a year is meant to cover the $50k a month.

**1. The year-two target is probably out of reach (the biggest problem)**

- You need a 15-point advantage over the comparison group. At your current schools you get about 12 points after a full October-to-April year.
- The new schools would have about 13 weeks (January 12 to mid-April), with a late start, new coordinators and brand-new tutors. Expect a smaller effect than 12 points, not a larger one. Hitting 15 would be a result far better than your best.
- Because the effect is measured against a comparison group, a small number of kids per school makes the result noisy. That cuts both ways, but the threshold is set above your demonstrated performance.
- Even if the program works, it can fail the test. Then you would have spent $600k of year-one money and lost the second $600k, with the expansion half-built.
- Ask Arbelle to change the condition before you accept. Options:
  - Tie release to implementation milestones: schools running, dosage delivered, tutor retention.
  - Use a threshold you've shown you can meet, such as a positive, statistically significant effect, or growth measured against your own baseline.
  - Start the new schools in the fall and measure the spring benchmark after a full year.
  - Make the year-two release depend on a later benchmark, such as the following winter.
- If they won't move, the real question for the board is whether you would want the year-one money knowing year two is unlikely.

**2. Cash flow: the reimbursement timing breaks your reserves**

- Coordinators start November 1 at about $50k a month. The Nov-Jan quarter closes January 31, and Arbelle pays about 45 days later, around mid-March.
- By mid-March you will have spent about $225k (4.5 months) and received nothing. Your $150k reserve runs out around the end of January, and you are about $75k short in mid-March.
- The first payment (about $150k) arrives mid-March. The next gap looks the same: Feb-Apr closes April 30 and pays around mid-June, so you'd be about $225k out of pocket again just before it arrives.
- That means you permanently need about $225k of float and have $150k. This assumes no overruns, and there will be some: recruiting, background checks, training, materials, and the cost of a late payment or a disallowed expense.
- Also, every dollar of reserve you spend leaves nothing for the existing four schools.
- Fixes, in rough order of preference:
  - Ask Arbelle for an advance, such as the first quarter up front, or monthly or quarterly payment in advance.
  - Ask for payment on invoice within 15 to 30 days.
  - Line up a bridge: a line of credit, a program-related investment, or a bridge loan from another funder, in place before the vote.
  - Slow the ramp so spending doesn't start in November.
- Confirm what counts as an allowable expense and what happens if a report is rejected or late.

**3. The district MOU won't be done by January 12**

- Your last MOU took about five months. The expansion needs one covering 10 schools, using school space and student data.
- Five months from October is March. Knowing the people helps, but they have their own legal review, data-privacy review and approval calendar, and the holidays are in the way.
- Ten principals being excited is good, but principal enthusiasm isn't the same as district approval, and the district can still say no or slow you down.
- You also can't hire coordinators in November and place them in schools with no MOU. They would be paid for two or more months with nothing to do, which makes the cash problem worse.
- Fixes: start the MOU conversation this week, before the board vote, and ask the district what the real timeline is in writing. Ask whether the existing MOU can be amended instead of replaced, and whether a staged approval (some schools first) is possible. Make the grant's start date depend on the MOU.

**4. Tutor supply: 175 more is a very large jump**

- You'd go from about 70 to about 245 tutors, 3.5 times your current base, from one source.
- Harmon's spring semester starts January 20, but you plan to start tutoring on January 12. Your tutors may not be enrolled, registered for practicum or on campus until after you start, and recruiting in November and December runs into finals and winter break.
- Practicum placements are also set by the school of education and its faculty. Check whether Harmon can place 175 more students, since that is likely about as many as the program has.
- Retention matters as well. Tutoring twice a week for 400 kids is about 800 sessions a week, and any tutor who drops out leaves a child without a session. Education students also have a fixed term and leave in May.
- Fixes: ask Harmon (the education dean or practicum office) now whether they can commit to a number. Add other sources: other colleges, AmeriCorps-type programs, retirees, community volunteers, high schoolers, paid tutors. Start fewer kids per school at first, or tutor in pairs.
- If the pipeline is weak, a realistic version is a smaller January start (for example 4 to 5 of the new schools) with the rest in the fall.

**Other points worth checking**

- **Coordinator hiring.** Hiring 10 people starting November 1, when the vote is October 8, means offers have to go out almost immediately, and the pool at that time of year is thin. Also check whether the $50k a month includes all costs (salaries, benefits, training, supplies, background checks, evaluation). Does $50k a month cover 10 coordinators and the other costs?
- **Organizational capacity.** You are going from 4 schools to 14 and from 160 kids to about 560 (40 x 10 new schools plus 160). The management layer (a director of programs, a data person, someone for tutor training) is probably not in the $50k a month. Many nonprofits stumble when they more than triple in a year.
- **Quality.** Your 12-point result depends on tutor training, supervision and consistency. Scaling quickly with new tutors may dilute it, which makes issue 1 worse.
- **Comparison group.** Find out who picks the comparison group, how it's matched, and how the district benchmark data reaches Arbelle. A mismatched group could work for or against you. That needs a written definition in the grant agreement.
- **Dosage.** Twice a week, 40 kids per school: confirm that's feasible given school schedules and the number of tutors on site at any one time.
- **What happens after year two.** If the grant ends, is there a plan to sustain 14 schools?
- **Reporting burden.** Quarterly expense reports on 10 additional sites is real work for your finance staff.

**What I'd bring to the board**

1. A recommendation to accept the grant only with changes: an advance or faster payment, a different (or later) year-two metric, and a start date that depends on the MOU.
2. A phased plan, for example: start the MOU now, hire coordinators when the MOU is nearly done, start a first wave of schools in January or February with the tutors you can actually place, and open the rest in the fall. Phase it so spending follows signed approvals.
3. A written commitment from Harmon on tutor numbers, and a second source of tutors.
4. A cash plan showing the monthly balance with the bridge financing in place.
5. A clear answer to this question: if the year-two money is not released, can the organization absorb that, or does it have to wind the 10 schools down? Board members should vote with that downside in front of them.

Arbelle made the offer because it wants this to work. Funders often prefer a funded pilot with realistic terms to a grant that fails. Showing them these four numbers (the $75k gap, the five-month MOU, the 13-week window against a 12-point benchmark, and 175 tutors) is a strong basis for a conversation about changing the terms.
