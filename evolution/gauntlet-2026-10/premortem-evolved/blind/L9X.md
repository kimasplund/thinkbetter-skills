Short answer: not as written. I wouldn't sign on June 2 with this plan. The idea may be sound, but the schedule and the numbers have problems that each could sink it, and the lease terms leave you exposed if they do.

**1. The timeline doesn't work**
- You apply for the meter upgrade on June 2, and it takes 10-14 weeks. That puts the earliest gas on about August 11 and the latest around September 8.
- Courses start the week of August 4. Even in the best case, the kiln can't run for the first cohorts. Their pieces can't be bisqued or glazed, which hurts a beginner course badly.
- The kiln also needs delivery, installation, venting and likely a gas inspection and commissioning after the meter work. Count on more delay.
- The two free months end around August 2, so rent starts before you can fire anything.
- Because the original kiln is dying and you won't replace it, your existing members have no firing at all until the new kiln works. That puts your current income at risk.

**2. Even a full house doesn't cover costs**
- $13,600 is what four full cohorts bring in for the whole 8-week course, not per month. 8 weeks is about 1.85 months, so that's roughly $7,350 a month.
- Your fixed costs are $9,400 a month. At 100% capacity you lose about $2,000 a month.
- Break-even on courses alone needs about 51 students per 8-week block ($9,400 x 1.85 / $340). Your capacity is 40 (4 cohorts x 10 wheels).
- This assumes cohorts run back to back with no gaps and no dropouts. Real schedules have breaks between blocks and some no-shows.
- "Covering its own costs by Christmas" therefore can't happen with this model, however good the demand is.

**3. The waitlist is weaker than it looks**
- 140 names from a free try-the-wheel booth is interest, not commitment. Typical conversion from a sign-up sheet to paid enrollment is perhaps 10-20%. That's roughly 14-28 students, or about 1-3 cohorts, not 4.
- Test it before you sign. Email the list, offer a deposit or early-bird price, and see how many pay. Fifteen to twenty deposits would be a real signal.

**4. The kiln can't handle the firing load**
- Every piece is fired twice, once for bisque and once for glaze. Count piece-firings, not pieces.
- Demand: members at 60 pieces a week, plus 40 beginners x 2-3 pieces = 80-120 pieces a week. That's 140-180 pieces a week, or 280-360 piece-firings.
- Capacity: 3 firings x 70 = 210 piece-firings a week, and that assumes every load is completely full.
- You'd be short by 25-40% or more. Add the driving time to move members' work between studios, which delays everything.
- Mid-course, beginners wait weeks to see their work. That drives dropouts and bad reviews.

**5. The cash cushion is thin and the downside is large**
- $22,000 covers about 2.3 months of $9,400 in costs. You haven't listed fit-out, wheels, shelving, ventilation, deposits or the kiln down payment. Those could eat much of it before opening.
- Run August to December. That's 5 months of costs, about $47,000. Even if you earned $20-30k in that stretch, you'd be well into your savings and probably into the original studio's money, which you said you want to avoid.
- The personal guarantee on a five-year lease at $4,100 a month is about $246,000 of exposure, secured by you personally. That is the biggest risk. The plan's other weaknesses all become your personal debt.

**What I'd do instead**
1. **Don't sign until the dates work.** Apply for the meter now, or ask the landlord to let you apply before signing. Make lease start, and rent, conditional on gas being live. Or move the first cohorts to October and November.
2. **Renegotiate the lease.** Ask for 3 years, or a 5-year term with a break clause at 12-18 months. Ask for a capped or burning-off personal guarantee, and rent-free time that runs from when gas is connected.
3. **Fix the economics.** Either raise the price (at $340 for 8 weeks, you're probably underpriced; check against local classes), add weekend or daytime cohorts, or add revenue such as open studio memberships, workshops, and parties or corporate events. You need roughly $10k+ a month in realistic revenue, not $7k at perfect fill.
4. **Fix firing.** Consider a second kiln, or replace or repair the old electric one. Or lower the member volume you take on, charge firing fees, or limit beginners to a fixed number of pieces. Check kiln size and loading again: 70 pieces in 24 cu ft is a plausible number only for small, well-packed pieces.
5. **Validate demand cheaply.** Run a pilot of one or two cohorts at your existing studio before committing to a new lease. This tests price, conversion and retention at almost no cost.
6. **Set a stop rule.** For example: if fewer than 25 paid students by a given date, or cash falls below $10,000, you stop and don't draw on the original studio.

**Assumptions I made.** The $9,400 includes the kiln loan payment from August. The $340 is per 8-week course. Cohorts run back to back. If any of these is different, the numbers shift, but I don't think the main conclusion does.

**Bottom line:** the market interest and your instinct to grow are good signs, but this version has a schedule that can't be met, a revenue model that can't break even even at full capacity, a kiln that's undersized for the plan, and a personal guarantee that turns a miss into a major personal loss. Rework those four things first, then sign.
