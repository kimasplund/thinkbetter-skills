Short answer: not as written. The demand is real, but this deal would likely run you out of cash around May, miss the March 1 date, and leave Calloway unprofitable on a cumulative basis through September. It can be fixed with negotiation. Here is the math.

Assumptions: 48 stores x 3 flavors = 144 store-flavors. The buyer's 6 jars/week means 864 jars/week. The 25% promo is billed against the $5.40 wholesale price ($1.35/jar). If it is 25% of the $8.99 shelf price, it is $2.25/jar and everything below gets worse. I'm ignoring your existing business's cash flow. I'm assuming Calloway pays for jars as they're replenished.

**1. Per-jar economics are fine. The upfront costs are the problem.**

- Contribution per jar: $5.40 - $3.10 - $0.30 freight = $2.00 (37%).
- Slotting: 144 x $250 = **$36,000**.
- Free opening fill: 144 x 12 = 1,728 jars. That's about $5,900 of landed cost, or about $9,300 of revenue you don't collect.
- Launch promo: 864 x 8 weeks = 6,912 jars x $1.35 = about **$9,300** (about $15,500 if it's off shelf price).
- Total upfront give-ups: about **$51,000**. At $2.00/jar, you need about 25,600 jars to earn that back. That is about 30 weeks at the buyer's own forecast, so roughly October at best, even if the forecast holds.

**2. The timeline does not hit March 1.**

You plan to order in the first week of January. The trial batch adds 2-3 weeks before Halvard schedules you, and then production takes 6 weeks. That puts finished goods in early-to-mid March, plus transit. Late February only works if the trial batch happens before you order. Ask Halvard now whether the trial can run in December, right after signing, or even before. Also ask Calloway what a late start costs you (penalties, a lost slot, a reset date). Don't promise March 1 until Halvard confirms in writing.

**3. Cash is the real killer.**

- January: 50% deposit on 15,000 jars (3 x 5,000 minimum) = $23,250.
- Late February: balance of $23,250 plus about $4,500 freight.
- That's about $51,000 out before the first jar is on the shelf. You have $60k including the full credit line, leaving about $9k.
- Money in: Net-60 means March invoices are paid in May at the earliest. The $36k slotting and the promo credits are deducted from those invoices. At roughly $3,500/week of net invoicing during the promo, deductions eat about the first 10 weeks of invoices. Expect near-zero actual cash from Calloway until roughly June-July.
- Reorder: 15,000 jars minus the free fill lasts about 15 weeks at forecast, so you'd run out in mid-June. With a 6-week lead time and a deposit, you must reorder in early May. That's another $23k deposit, when you have about $9k and no Calloway cash.
- Estimated peak funding need is about $95-100k against $60k available. The gap is roughly $35-40k. If you draw the full credit line, you'll also pay interest on it. If sales are slow, the gap is smaller but you're stuck with inventory. If they're fast, you stock out and risk delisting.

**4. The September goal.**

- Biggest account: yes. About $20k/month at forecast versus your $9k total.
- Profitable by September: no. Over March-August at forecast (about 22,500 sold plus the free fill), revenue is about $121k, less promo, slotting, and about $82k of product and freight cost. That nets to roughly breakeven to slightly negative (about -$6k) before returns, interest, and your time. After the promo, the run-rate is about $7,500/month of contribution. That is real, but it comes late.
- Sensitivity: the 6 jars/store/week figure is the buyer's hope, not a commitment. A new chili crisp in a regional chain may sell at half that, 3 jars/week. Then the upfront costs take more than a year to recover, and unsold product nears expiry (6-month shelf life, and retailers usually want much of it left on delivery). Expired and damaged product is credited back to them, so slow sell-through costs you twice.

**5. Other risks.**

- Nothing in the terms commits Calloway to volume, but you're committed to Halvard's 5,000-per-flavor minimums. If one flavor lags, you're stuck with 5,000 jars.
- Quality and consistency change when moving from your own kitchen to a co-packer. The trial batch matters. Make sure the product matches what people buy at the markets.
- Check your current cost per jar. If you make it for less than $3.10, the co-packer deal costs you margin.
- Capacity: running two of you through a 48-store account while still doing markets and online is a lot of work.

**6. What I'd do instead of signing on December 15.**

Counter, don't decline. Priorities in order:

1. **Slotting.** Ask to waive it, cut it to a fraction, or limit it to one or two flavors. Or tie it to performance (for example, deducted over 12-18 months, or refunded if they delist you within a year).
2. **Phase the rollout.** Start with 10-15 stores, or one or two flavors, then expand on proven velocity. This also cuts the minimum run and the cash need.
3. **Start date.** Push to April 1 or a rolling store-by-store start. That fits the trial batch plus lead time.
4. **Payment.** Net-30 instead of Net-60. At minimum, don't offset slotting and promos in a way that leaves you with no payments for months.
5. **Promo.** Cap it (fewer weeks, or Calloway shares the cost) and define the 25% base in writing.
6. **Returns.** Cap credits for expiry and damage (for example, a percent of sales), and set minimum remaining shelf life at delivery.
7. **Halvard.** Ask for a smaller first run, a 30/70 deposit split, or a lower minimum. Confirm the trial batch can start in December.
8. **Funding.** If you do the full deal, you need another $40k or more: a larger line of credit, a purchase-order or invoice financing, or a small investor. Line it up before you sign.

Rule of thumb: don't sign unless your plan survives sales at half the buyer's forecast and Calloway paying 90 days after you ship.

This is a good account to want and a bad deal to sign as is. A Calloway deal with phased stores, reduced slotting, and Net-30 could hit your September goal. Please have a lawyer review the contract for exclusivity, delisting, and chargeback clauses before you sign.
