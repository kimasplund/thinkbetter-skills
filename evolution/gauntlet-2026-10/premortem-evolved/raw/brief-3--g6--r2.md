# Tidewell x Calloway Markets: how this deal dies

**Horizon:** Sept 30, 2027 · **Success was defined as:** "by September, Calloway is our biggest account and the business is profitable"
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure, meaning "if this dies, this is what killed it". They are not the probability that the plan fails overall, and they sum to about 100.
**Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** ~70% as written. The "biggest account" half probably lands if the buyer's velocity is real. The "profitable" half is the weak one: even at the buyer's own number the Calloway account is still about $8-9k underwater cumulatively on Sept 30 (arithmetic below).
**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- All 48 stores take all 3 flavors, so there are 144 store-flavor slots.
- The 25% promo bill-back is 25% of the $8.99 shelf price ($2.25 a jar) on units sold in the eight weeks. If it is 25% of your $5.40 wholesale ($1.35), the promo cost drops by about $6,200.
- Calloway invoices as stores reorder and nets the slotting fee out of those invoices until the $36,000 is cleared. Net-60 runs from the invoice date.
- Your existing $9k a month covers its own costs and your pay. LOC interest and Halvard's trial-batch fees are not in your numbers, so I left them out.
- Halvard makes the Calloway volume; your kitchen keeps serving markets and online. A second run of the same size is needed once the first sells through.
- Your average realized price today is about $10 a jar, so you sell roughly 900 jars a month. I use this only to size the step up.
- I could not pull live category data (chili crisp shelf prices, typical grocery velocity). Nothing below is category data, and the numbers that depend on the buyer's 6 jars a week are his, not mine.

## The Autopsy

**The strongest case for this plan:** A 48-store chain came to you, and it wants all three flavors at once, which an unknown brand rarely gets. At $8.99 shelf and $5.40 wholesale the retailer keeps about 40%, so Calloway has every reason to make it work. You keep $2.00 a jar after co-packing and freight (37% of wholesale). At the buyer's own velocity the account is 864 jars a week, about $20k a month of wholesale revenue, more than double your entire business today, which makes "biggest account" nearly automatic. You already have proven demand at markets and online. Co-packing takes you out of the kitchen, and a regional chain on the shelf is a proof point for the next chain. A smart person who believes this is not naive. The demand signal is real and the unit margin is positive.

**Your own numbers first.** I attacked the assumption you stated most firmly, the calendar ("order the first week of January, deliver late February"), and then ran the rest of the arithmetic.
- **Calendar.** Jan 5, plus 2-3 weeks of trial sign-off, plus 6 weeks of production puts finished jars at Halvard on Mar 2-9. Freight, warehouse receiving and store distribution come after that. "Late February" needs seven weeks from order, and your own numbers give eight to nine. The calendar does not survive.
- **Unit economics.** Landed cost is $3.40 ($3.10 plus $0.30 freight), so you keep $2.00 a jar. At 6 jars per store per flavor per week that is 864 jars a week and $1,728 a week of contribution, about $7,500 a month.
- **Cost of entry.** Slotting is 144 x $250 = $36,000. The free opening case is 1,728 jars, exactly two weeks of the buyer's own sell-through, so Calloway pays for nothing until week 3. That is $9,331 of forgone wholesale. The promo is 6,912 jars x $2.25 = $15,552. Total entry cost is about $60,900, which is 35 weeks of perfect contribution. Break-even on the account lands in early November 2027 at the buyer's velocity, and the account is still $8-9k underwater on Sept 30.
- **Cash.** The minimum is 15,000 jars. That is $46,500 for jars plus $4,500 freight, $51,000 out by early March, leaving about $9k of your $60k. Net-60 with slotting and promo netted against invoices means the invoices from week 3 to about week 14 (mid-March to late May) pay out near $0. The first net Calloway cash lands around Jul 30. The first 15,000 jars are only 17 weeks of supply, so the second order's $23,250 deposit is due around May 25 and the balance in July. Outlay by mid-July is about $102k against $60k, a $42k gap.
- **Scale.** 864 jars a week is roughly four times everything you sell today, and Calloway would be about 70% of revenue.

Seven ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The account sold through at the buyer's own number, and the second Halvard deposit came due before Calloway had paid a dollar.
**Probability:** ~30% — arithmetic, not judgment: about $102k leaves your accounts by mid-July against $60k available, and no net Calloway cash arrives before about Jul 30.
**Cause of death:** The deal was a five-month working-capital loan of roughly $100k to a grocer, funded by two people holding $60k. Existing guard in the plan: the $25k line of credit, which is spent by March.
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Small specialty-food brands entering a first regional chain are commonly starved by working capital before demand (a widely repeated pattern; weakly sourced, I have no dated figure or source). Where you differ: you have $60k committed and a paying $9k-a-month base, which pushes the number down. Against that, a fourfold weekly volume step in one month with two people and a six-week lead time pushes it up. Net, it stays on top.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jan: The $23,250 deposit went to Halvard in the first week, plus the trial-batch fees nobody had priced. Savings dropped to about $11k.
- Feb-Mar: The balance and freight ($27,750) left on delivery, with the credit line fully drawn. You held about $9k. Stores filled from free cases, and the first two weeks of sell-through earned nothing.
- Apr: Invoices ran about $4,700 a week on paper and arrived as $0 on the remittance, because the $36,000 slotting fee and the promo bill-backs were netted against them. The plan had no column for a zero-cash quarter.
- May: Scan data showed the 864 jars a week were real. Halvard wanted $23,250 by about May 25 to avoid empty shelves in July. You had $9k less rent and ingredients for the markets.
- Jun-Jul: You tried to split the order and ask for terms. The run was not scheduled, stores began running out in early July, and the buyer re-awarded the empty facings to another brand. The first net Calloway payment landed Jul 30, three weeks after the shelves emptied.
- Sept: Calloway was not your biggest account. It was a delisting notice and a $42k hole.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that a signed order from the biggest customer you have ever had is revenue, when it is a loan you make to that customer.
**First warning sign:** The shortfall is already visible today in the terms. The first in-flight sign is the Feb balance wire leaving about $9k against a late-May deposit, and the Mar-Apr remittance advice showing $0 net due.

### 2. Velocity landed near 3 jars per store per flavor per week once the 25% discount ended, the $36,000 slotting fee was never earned back, and half the run was heading for its pull date.
**Probability:** ~20% — the 6-a-week figure is a forecast by the party collecting $36,000 and a funded promotion whether or not it holds, and the first read is inflated by the discount.
**Cause of death:** Shoppers at a regional grocery paid $8.99 for a product they had never seen, and the buyer had no obligation to keep it. Existing guard: the buyer's own estimate, which is not a commitment (the terms contain no minimum purchase and no listing term).
**Reference class and disanalogy:** New grocery items are widely said to be delisted at high rates in their first year (weakly sourced; I cannot give a figure, source or year). Where you differ: proven market and online demand pushes the number down. The market shopper is not the 48-store shopper, and the eight promo weeks hide the true $8.99 price, which pushes it up. Net, it stays at 20%.
**How it unfolded:**
- Mar: The product sat at $6.74 under promo. The first two weeks sold from free cases and looked busy.
- Apr: The promo held velocity around 5 to 6 a week. On Apr 26 the price went back to $8.99, a 33% increase, and velocity fell to about 3.
- May: The week-8 review showed about 3 a week. With no listing term to honor, the buyer cut the weakest flavor and the weakest stores. The slotting for those facings had already been deducted and was not refundable.
- Jun-Jul: At 3 a week, about half the 15,000-jar run (roughly 7,500 jars) was still unsold. The code dates, set by a late-February production, approached Calloway's pull window. A six-month life runs out in late August, and grocers commonly pull earlier (check Calloway's rule). Expired and damaged credits at $5.40 a jar started coming back.
- Sept: Slotting ($36k), promo ($15.6k) and credits sat on top of an unsold half-run. Cumulative loss on the account was in the $50-60k range.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "about 6 a week" measures the shopper, when it is the counterparty's forecast.
**First warning sign:** Scan data for weeks 3-4 (around Mar 22). If velocity at 25% off is below 6, the unpromoted number will be lower.

### 3. The Mar 1 start was missed on the plan's own arithmetic, and the two-week slip landed on top of the cash wall.
**Probability:** ~15% — the slip itself is close to certain on your numbers, but it kills only when stacked on #1 or when Calloway enforces the date, and the terms say nothing about penalties.
**Cause of death:** The plan priced Halvard's six weeks and left out the 2-3 weeks of trial sign-off stated in the same sentence. This is the compound entry, narrated as the interaction of the slip and the cash wall in #1. Existing guard: none; the terms have no slack and no remedy for lateness.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jan: The order went in the first week. Halvard required trial batches for three flavors before scheduling, and sign-off came Jan 19-26 at best.
- Feb: The six-week clock started after sign-off. Finished goods came off the line Mar 2-9, not late February.
- Mar: Calloway's reset happened on its calendar. The terms did not say whether the eight promo weeks start on Mar 1 or on first delivery, and Calloway applied them from its own reset date. Jars reached shelves Mar 16-23. You funded promo weeks that had no stock behind them, and the buyer's first impression was a half-filled launch.
- Apr-Jun: Every invoice and every Calloway payment moved two weeks later, which pushed the first net cash to mid-August. Halvard's second-run deposit date did not move. The slip ate the buffer the cash wall needed, so #1 arrived earlier and larger.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that "six weeks" was the whole lead time.
**First warning sign:** By Jan 26, trial sign-off incomplete for all three flavors, or a Halvard start date after Jan 19.

### 4. Both founders were consumed by Calloway, and the $9k-a-month base that funds everything drifted down.
**Probability:** ~10% — two people, a roughly fourfold volume step, and no named owner for the Calloway account; a base-rate killer, not a plot twist.
**Cause of death:** The base business paid the kitchen rent and your pay, and nobody was staffed to keep it running while the new account absorbed the same hours. Existing guard: none.
**How it unfolded:**

- May-Jun: That shortfall widened the deposit gap in #1 by roughly the same amount each month, and the unresolved question of who runs Calloway full time came up with no one named.
**The assumption that allowed it:** that the new account would be absorbed in spare hours.
**First warning sign:** Two consecutive weeks of market and online revenue more than 20% below the trailing average, from March.

### 5. The 15,000-jar Halvard run did not match the signed-off trial batch, and the deposit was already gone.

**Cause of death:** Recipe sign-off at pilot scale, with no written tolerance, was treated as the spec for a run made in one week. Existing guard: the trial batch and balance-on-delivery. It covers taste, not scale variance, and gives leverage only if the contract defines acceptance.
**How it unfolded:**

- Feb: All three flavors ran in the same week.
- Mar: At the dock, one flavor showed oil separation and a softer crisp. Halvard called it within process. The founders held the $7,750 balance and disputed it.

### 6. Nobody owned the paperwork and the deductions, so onboarding items slipped and credits and bill-backs were taken that nobody checked.

Nine weeks remain before Dec 15, so trial batches can finish before you sign, not after. Place the production order on Jan 4 for a written delivery date of Feb 15 or earlier, which leaves two weeks of slack against Mar 1. Do not produce more than about three weeks before first delivery, because the six-month shelf life starts at production. If the terms do not move, launch with two flavors in all 48 stores and add the third at the mid-year reset. Two flavors cut slotting to $24,000, the Halvard minimum to 10,000 jars, and the mid-July peak outlay to about $68k, roughly $8k over what you have as written and about covered if slotting is capped at 25% of each invoice.

**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** The horizon passed. We held back on the Dec 15 signing, and it was a mistake.

## The Adversary

**Playing:** A funded national chili-crisp competitor with a broker at Calloway and a trade-promotion budget. Motivation: keep or take the facings next to yours. Resources: it can fund deeper promo than your capped $15.6k, and it has data on Calloway's shoppers you lack. What it cannot do: discount indefinitely, because deep Calloway pricing leaks to its other accounts. Its best alternative is paying trade funds elsewhere at a similar cost.
**The kill chain:**
1. Probe (Dec 15 - Feb): the broker learns from the planogram that a regional newcomer holds three facings from Mar 1, and the competitor's rep prices your jar and asks the buyer for the next review date.
2. Position (Mar 1 - Apr 26): it buys a feature or end-cap at about $6.99 on its flagship, next to your $6.74 promo price.
3. Strike (Apr 26 - May): as your 25% ends and your price returns to $8.99, its feature continues and your velocity drops. It pitches the buyer on a reset with guaranteed velocity and larger slotting that it pays.
4. Entrench (Jun - Jul): it uses any out-of-stock on your shelves to claim the facing.
**Detection opportunities:**
- Probe and position: the competitor's feature or price in Calloway's weekly circular and store audits from Mar 1. Threshold: a feature at $6.99 or less in 2 of the first 4 promo weeks.
- Strike and entrench: at the Apr 26 review, the buyer asks for a deeper discount, more promo funding, or declines to share scan data. In a Jun 14 store audit, 10% or more of your slots are empty or refilled.
**The move you'd never see coming:** Calloway's buyer uses your 8-week scan data and a common co-packer to specify a private-label chili crisp. Your agreement with Halvard has no non-use or confidentiality clause, and the retailer knows your $5.40 and can shelve its own at $5.99.
**What this means you should change:** Get the recipe confidentiality and non-use clause in the Halvard contract before the trial batches go out on Oct 30. Hold promo money to the capped $15.6k and answer a competitor's feature with demos, not extra discount. Keep your own scan data and a strong direct channel as a fallback.
