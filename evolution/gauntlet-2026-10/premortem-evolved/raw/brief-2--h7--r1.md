# Premortem: Ledgerwood Reading Partners, 4 to 14 schools on the Arbelle grant
**Horizon:** August 31, 2027 (past the mid-April benchmark and the summer release decision) · **Success was defined as:** "all 14 schools running in January, and the second-year money released next summer."
**Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** ~90%, on the plan as written.
**Note on the percentages:** they are shares of failure ("if this dies, this is what killed it"), not the probability the plan fails overall. They sum to ~100. Where two failures would hit together, the share goes to whichever one is the first, binding cause.
**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- The Oct 8 vote is still ahead of you. If it has passed, everything under the Rebuild becomes a set of amendment requests to a signed agreement, and the dates below are unchanged.
- Dates run 2026 to 2027. Arbelle's first quarter closes Jan 31 and pays about Mar 16; the second closes Apr 30 and pays about Jun 14.
- The $50k a month covers the coordinators and the rest of the expansion budget as you described it. It does not include tutor background checks, fingerprinting, insurance, extra finance and HR time, or evaluation work, because you did not mention them.
- The $150k of reserves is your only cushion for the existing four schools as well as the expansion, and there is no credit line today.
- You have no written commitment from Harmon State and no written confirmation of Arbelle's reimbursement rules or of who builds the comparison group.
- Your reference class is your own record: a five-month MOU for four schools, and +12 points after a full October to April year.

## The Autopsy
**The strongest case for this plan:** Arbelle chose you because the model works. A +12-point edge over comparison students after one year of tutoring is a strong result, and 160 kids is enough to take seriously. The model scales on a simple ratio: about 2.3 kids per tutor today (160 kids, 70 tutors) and the same ratio for the expansion (400 kids, 175 tutors). The tutor supply is a renewable one that already pays you in practicum credit rather than wages. All ten principals want you in. You know the district office, so the process that took five months the first time should be faster the second. At roughly $1,500 per child per year the cost is defensible, and $1.2M is a rare chance to jump from a local program to a district-scale one. Every failure below has to beat that case, not a strawman.

Six ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The district MOU for ten schools was still unsigned on January 12, and then through March
**Probability:** ~30% (rare to see it fail on a date, but your own five-month MOU is the reference class and the plan gives this one about three months)
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Your four-school MOU took about five months. This one differs three ways. You know the people now (pushes the number down). It may be an amendment to an existing agreement, which could be faster (down). It covers ten schools and student data at once, in a queue where Arbelle's deadline means nothing to the district (up). Net: the date is unlikely to hold.
**Existing guard:** none. The plan has no MOU line, no filing date and no district-side owner. Principal enthusiasm is not a guard, because principals do not sign the MOU.
**Cause of death:** January 12 was set by Harmon's calendar and Arbelle's quarters, not by the district's.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Nov): Ten coordinators started Nov 1 with nothing to coordinate. The MOU package for ten schools went to the district in the second week of November. The district treated it as a new program and sent it to legal and to its data-privacy review.
- Month 2 (Dec): The review sat through finals week and the holiday break. The coordinators spent December recruiting tutors for schools they were not allowed to enter.
- Month 3 (Jan): January 12 came with zero to three schools approved. Principals could not override the district. The tutors who were trained had nowhere to go.
- Month 4 to 5 (Feb to Mar): Approvals arrived school by school, the last around mid-March (Nov filing plus the five-month reference). Tutoring began at most new schools in late March.
- Month 6 (Apr): The benchmark landed after three to five weeks of tutoring at most new schools. See #2.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "All 10 principals have told me they're excited to have us" was treated as access. Excitement costs a principal nothing, so it is Comply, not Endorse. The signature that matters belongs to the district office, which has said nothing on paper.
**First warning sign:** No written district signature date by Oct 31, and fewer than four school approvals by Dec 15.

### 2. The second-year bar of +15 points was out of reach on the plan's own arithmetic
**Probability:** ~20% (it would be near-certain if the plan reached April, but it only gets the credit when #1, #3 and #4 did not already kill the plan first)
**Reference class and disanalogy:** Your four schools earn +12 after a full October to April year, about 25 effective weeks at two sessions a week, so roughly 50 sessions. A new school starting Jan 12 gets about 11 to 12 effective weeks to a mid-April benchmark after holidays and testing weeks, so roughly 22 to 24 sessions: under half the dose. Scaling the effect with dose gives about +5 to +6, against a bar of +15, which is 25% above your best result. Gains are not exactly linear in dose, and fresh sites with lower baselines have more room to improve, so I would not trust a single number. But no reasonable adjustment closes a gap of this size. Programs usually look weaker when they scale to new sites and new staff; I give that as a pattern, weakly sourced.
**Existing guard:** none. Nothing in the plan flags the gap, and the comparison group is undefined: who picks it, from which students, and measured how.
**Cause of death:** The grant converted a result earned with a full year of dosage into a threshold applied to a quarter-year of dosage.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 to 2 (Nov to Dec): The board accepted the benchmark clause. Nobody asked Arbelle who builds the comparison group or what happens at 10 points.
- Month 3 to 5 (Jan to Mar): Late starts (#1) and a thin tutor bench (#4) cut the dose further. By March the new-school sessions were running at a pace the old schools reached in December.
- Month 6 (mid-Apr): The benchmark showed +4 to +8. The $600k stayed unreleased.
- Month 7 to 10 (May to Aug): Year-two coordinators had been hired against money that was never promised. You either carried ten salaries on reserves or laid off the people you had just trained.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "Our kids outscore comparison students by about 12" was read as the capacity to deliver 15 anywhere, in any dose.
**First warning sign:** A six-week interim reading check at new schools on Mar 1 showing gains under 60% of what the old schools showed at the same dose.

### 3. Compound: the MOU delay met payment in arrears, and cash ran out in late January
**Probability:** ~20% (the cash gap is arithmetic and the compound is the likeliest pairing, but a bridge loan or advance closes it if arranged in time)
**Tie-break against #2 (both ~20%):** #2 sits above because it binds even if every other date holds and it is irreversible. #3 can be closed with a bridge facility or a payment advance.
**Existing guard:** none. The $150k of reserves is the guard, and the arithmetic shows it is too small.
**Cause of death:** A grant paid quarterly in arrears 45 days after quarter close makes you the bank for about 4.5 months of spending.
**The arithmetic:** Spending runs $50k a month from Nov 1. The first reimbursement arrives about Mar 16, so you will have spent about $225k by then. The reserve is $150k. The shortfall is about $75k, before any existing-program cost and before anything the plan left unpriced (below). The same $225k float returns just before the Jun 14 payment.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Nov): The first $50k went out. Background checks, fingerprinting, tutor training materials, extra payroll and finance time, and insurance for 175 new volunteers had never been budgeted, so about $20k to $40k landed on reserves in the first three months.
- Month 2 (Dec): Reserves fell to about $50k. Ten coordinators were being paid and could not enter schools (#1).
- Month 3 (Jan): Reserves hit zero in the last week of January, six weeks before the first reimbursement. Arbelle reimburses on submitted expense reports, so anything disallowed was lost as well.
- Month 4 (Feb): The Feb 15 payroll was due with no cash. The choices were to lay off coordinators and break the 14-school promise, delay pay at the four schools that earned the +12, or take a loan on terms set under pressure.
- Month 5 (Mar): The money arrived on Mar 16 but the damage was done. The four existing schools had their staff cut or distracted for six weeks.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "Arbelle pays" was treated as "Arbelle funds," when timing makes it a loan from you.
**First warning sign:** A 13-week cash forecast that includes the existing program and shows reserves under $100k by Jan 31. It can be built this week.

### 4. The tutor pipeline missed Harmon's calendar and never reached 175
**Probability:** ~15% (it kills when the supply never forms, not when it forms late; the MOU delay, which makes a late supply tolerable, is counted in #1)
**Existing guard:** the 70 current tutors and your Harmon relationship. Neither is a written commitment for 175 more.
**Cause of death:** Recruiting and training were scheduled for November and December, the two months students are least available, for a practicum that only exists once the spring term starts on Jan 20.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Nov): Recruitment competed with the end-of-semester crunch. Interest was real but sign-ups were few, because no credit could be promised until the spring registration.
- Month 2 (Dec): Finals, then the break. Training sessions and fingerprinting appointments needed students who had left town.
- Month 3 (Jan): Jan 12 was eight days before the term started. Practicum credit attaches to enrolled students, so only the volunteers could start. A bench of 60 to 80 trained tutors was a third to a half of the 175 needed.
- Month 4 (Feb): With the term underway, class timetables fixed which afternoons each student could tutor. Sites ran at 5 to 8 tutors instead of 17 to 18, and kids per school was cut to hold the 2.3 ratio.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "We have about 70" was read as proof that 245 is available from the same pool.
**First warning sign:** Fewer than 90 signed-up tutors by Dec 15, or no written practicum commitment from Harmon by Dec 1.

### 5. The January start worked and the organization did not scale with it
**Probability:** ~10% (it only fires if #1 through #4 are avoided, but it is the usual way scaling nonprofits lose their quality)
**Existing guard:** none. No deputy, no program director and no second layer of management appears in the plan.
**Cause of death:** The program went from 160 to 560 kids, 4 to 14 schools, and 4 staff sites to 14 with the executive director as the only integration layer.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (Nov): Ten coordinators were hired in about three weeks, which is a recruiting sprint at any size of nonprofit, and were trained by people who also ran the existing schools.
- Month 2 to 3 (Dec to Jan): The ED's weeks went to MOU follow-up, hiring, the grant report and the cash problem. Visits to the original four schools went down.
- Month 4 (Feb): At the old schools, session completion fell and two coordinators resigned. Nobody had the time to notice.
- Month 5 to 6 (Mar to Apr): The new schools ran on inconsistent training. The program Arbelle thought it was buying, the one that produced +12, existed at four schools and a lower-fidelity version at ten.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "More schools" was priced as more coordinators, not as more management.
**First warning sign:** Session completion at the original four schools dipping under its Oct to Dec baseline in any month from Feb, or the ED cancelling two or more existing-site visits in a month.

### 6. The yardstick moved: the comparison group changed without any failure on your side
**Probability:** ~5% (a judgment call; the reference class cannot cover this)
**Existing guard:** none. The comparison group is not defined in anything you described.
**Cause of death:** This is the frame's blind spot. I cannot name the exact mechanism, only that the 15-point comparison depends on a group Ledgerwood neither selects nor controls. If the district placed its own tutoring or extended-day help in the comparison schools in January, their scores rose and your edge narrowed with no change in your program.
**How it unfolded:**
- Month 3 (Jan): The district rolled out an intervention for comparison students. Nobody told you.
- Month 6 (Apr): The gap came in at +9 or +10 on a real +12 effect. The adversary section plays this out.
**The assumption that allowed it:** "The comparison group stays untreated" was never written down by anyone.
**First warning sign:** Any district announcement of new tutoring or extended-day support at schools outside your 14, by Jan 31.

**Considered and cut:** a principal changing schools mid-year (folded into #1, and in the table below), coordinator hiring slipping past Nov 1 (folded into #5), Arbelle changing terms after signing (<5%, recoverable).
**Downgraded — guarded for now:**

| Mode | Guard that holds it down | Signal that re-upgrades it |
|---|---|---|
| A principal withdraws or leaves a new school | Ten principals, so the plan survives losing two | Any principal turnover or a competing offer reported by Nov 30 |
| Harmon changes practicum-credit rules or cohort size | The existing 70-tutor arrangement | Any change in Harmon's spring practicum policy before Dec 1 |
| Tutor quality at new sites falls below the old sites | The training model works at four schools | A fidelity check at any new site scoring under the old sites' baseline |

**Most likely:** #1, the highest feasibility among the high scorers. It depends on one external office, it has a five-month record against it, and nothing in the plan schedules it.
**Most dangerous:** #3, not #1 and not #2. It is the only mode that threatens the four schools that already work, and its first warning (cash under $100k by January) arrives only weeks before the point of no return (a missed Feb 15 payroll). #2 costs you $600k of upside but arrives in April and leaves the existing program intact; #3 can end it.
**The hidden assumption:** Modes 1, 3 and 4 all die unless the same belief holds: that four outside clocks (the district office, Harmon State's semester, Arbelle's quarters, and the district's benchmark calendar) will run at Ledgerwood's tempo. #2 dies on the same root, because the benchmark date and the late start both come from clocks you do not set. The Jan 12 date was set by none of them.
**Fatal flaw:** Yes. The plan accepts three terms that are each survivable alone but not together: a benchmark bar 25% above your best full-year result, measured on under half the dose; funding that arrives after you have spent 1.5 times your reserves; and a start date no outside party has agreed to. Accepting the grant in this form is the flaw. The program and the funder are not.

## The Rebuild
**The revised plan:** Accept Arbelle's offer in principle, but do not accept the terms as written, and do not hire ten coordinators on Nov 1. The structure becomes two waves. Wave 1 is the 2 to 4 new schools whose approvals are in hand by Dec 15, opening on Jan 12 or the first Monday after their signatures, with the first quarter funded in advance or covered by a bridge facility. Wave 2 is the remaining 6 to 8 schools opening in September, so they have a full October to April year of dosage when the benchmark is measured. The benchmark becomes either a re-based target (such as a gain-per-session standard matched to your current +12) or a measurement taken in April of year two for the wave 2 cohort. Hires are tied to signatures: three coordinators on Nov 1 to run the MOU, recruiting and training, then one more per school as each approval lands. A deputy takes day-to-day charge of the existing four schools before any expansion hiring.

**What changed and why:**

| Change | Failure mode it closes | What it costs | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ask Arbelle, before signing, for Q1 funded in advance or monthly advances. If refused, secure a bank line of at least $150k with a commitment letter by Oct 31 | #3 | Interest on a drawn line, perhaps $5k to $10k, against the risk to a program with 160 kids | Launch-Blocking, decision Oct 31 |
| Re-base the second-year test: gain per session matched to current schools, or measurement in April of year two for the wave 2 cohort, plus a written definition of the comparison group and evaluator | #2, #6 | Possible friction with Arbelle, and a delay in the release | Launch-Blocking, decision Nov 15 |
| File one MOU package now, with 10 signed principal letters, and ask the district for a written signature date by school | #1 | A week of the ED's time and a visit | Launch-Blocking, decision Oct 31 |
| Hire coordinators in tranches: 3 on Nov 1, then one per school on MOU signature | #1, #3 | A slower ramp, but it saves roughly $30k to $35k a month of idle salary | Launch-Blocking, decision Nov 15 |
| Written Harmon State practicum agreement: spring registration list by Dec 1, credit rules, and training done in the first two weeks of term (Jan 20 to Feb 3) | #4 | A first tutoring start for wave 1 at roughly Feb 1 rather than Jan 12 | Launch-Blocking, decision Dec 1 |
| Add a second tutor source (other colleges, AmeriCorps, community volunteers) for wave 2 | #4 | Recruiting time of about 0.25 FTE through May | Fast-Follow, by Mar 1 |
| Hire a deputy director for the existing four schools; cap new-site weekly visits by the ED | #5 | About one salary, in the range of $70k to $90k a year | Fast-Follow, by Dec 15 |
| Biweekly update to Arbelle's program officer, and a request that the district share its intervention calendar | #6, adversary | A few hours a month | Track |

Every cost above is smaller than the mode it closes. The deputy is the one close call, because $80k a year is against a 10% share. I keep it because the thing at risk is the four schools that produced your only evidence.

**Responses to the most dangerous modes:** #3 is prevent (advance or bridge) and limit (hiring tied to signatures, so a stall cannot burn more than three coordinators' pay). #2 is prevent (re-basing the metric) and limit (year-two staff offers contingent on release, with a stated end date of August).
**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **District timeline.** Check: a written signature date, by school, from the district office. How: meet the contact this week and submit the package. Walk away from a January start if: fewer than four school approvals have a signature date before Dec 15. Falsifier: fewer than four signed by Dec 15 means the Jan 12 start for all 14 was never possible.
2. **Grant paper.** Check: payment mechanics (what counts as an allowable expense, how late payment is handled), the comparison group definition and who builds it, and what happens between 10 and 15 points. How: read the draft agreement and put the questions to the program officer in writing. Walk away from the second-year clause as drafted if: the release stays a single pass or fail at +15 with no re-based option and no advance.
3. **Cash.** Check: a 13-week forecast with the existing program, the unpriced costs and the Arbelle lag. How: finance lead builds it by Oct 20. Walk away if: committed liquidity (reserves plus a signed line) is under about $225k, or there is no commitment letter by Oct 31. Then limit to three coordinators.
4. **Harmon State.** Check: a written count of spring practicum students for new tutors. How: a letter from the department chair with a number. Walk away if: fewer than 90 committed by Dec 1. Falsifier: fewer than 90 signed by Dec 15 means 175 is out of reach this year.
5. **Principals.** Check: one-page letters with a room, a time slot and a named staff contact for each school. Walk away at any school lacking a letter by Nov 15; it drops to wave 2.
**Decision rule:** IF by Oct 31 the district gives a written signature date for at least four schools AND Arbelle agrees to advance Q1 or a bank commitment of $150k or more is in hand, AND by Nov 15 Arbelle agrees in writing to a re-based or later year-two test, THEN accept, hire three coordinators on Nov 1 and open wave 1 on Jan 12 (or on signature). ELSE accept only a reduced first tranche, hire no coordinators beyond the three, and move all openings to September. The risky capability (the MOU) is tested before the irreversible step (the ten-coordinator payroll).
**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** We scaled back, and it turned out to be a mistake. (1) Arbelle read the counter-proposal as hesitation and moved the money to another applicant. (2) The district signed all ten MOUs in November, which makes the shrinkage look like timidity. (3) A rival provider moved into the schools we deferred.
**What tips the balance:** A signed MOU covering at least eight schools by Nov 15 plus Arbelle's written advance. In that case the ELSE branch is dropped and the full January start goes ahead on the original terms for those schools.
**Consciously accepted (at most one):** #6, the comparison group moving. No money is spent against it; the exposure is up to $600k of year-two release, already reduced by the re-based test, and the leftover cost is a few points of measured effect.
**How the rebuilt plan dies:** The rebuild now depends on Arbelle accepting a restructured timeline and metric, and a funder that wanted fast scale may read the counter as a smaller commitment. Wave 2 also needs its own MOUs and a fall tutor supply that is just as untested as the spring one. The fixes shift the risk to negotiation; they do not remove it.
**Residual risk:** The district's approval pace stays outside your control, and your best evidence is one 160-kid program.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** An incumbent literacy provider (assumed; I do not know who works in Fennick) that holds district relationships and wants the same schools and Arbelle's attention. Its resources are standing contracts and the district's goodwill. It cannot block your MOU openly or misstate your results. Its best alternative is to bid for Arbelle's next cycle.
**The kill chain:**
- Probe (Oct 2026): It asks the district office how outside-program MOUs are processed and suggests batching them into one annual cycle. That reinforces the five-month reference.
- Position (Nov to Dec): It offers three of your ten principals a free January pilot. Principals say yes to both, since excitement costs them nothing.
- Strike (Jan to Feb): When your start slips, it mentions "delays" to Arbelle's program officer and offers an alternative.
- Entrench (Apr to Jun): It cites your benchmark results next to its own and presents itself as the safer partner for year two.
**Detection opportunities:**
- Probe: the district changes its MOU process or asks you new questions; visible Oct to Nov.
- Position: a principal mentions another provider by Nov 30.
- Strike: the program officer begins asking about delays you did not report, or the check-in cadence changes; visible by Feb 15.
- Entrench: Arbelle asks for results by school before April.
**The move you'd never see coming:** Nobody has to attack you. The district itself adds tutoring or extended-day help in the comparison schools in January, which raises their scores and narrows the 15-point gap. There is no villain and no notice (#6).
**What this means you should change:** Get the comparison group and its owner written down before signing, give the program officer your own timeline updates every two weeks so nobody else narrates your delay, and ask the district for its intervention calendar before January.
