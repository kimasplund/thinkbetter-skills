**Short version:** I wouldn't accept the plan as written. The grant is worth pursuing, but five problems each break the "all 14 schools in January, year-two money next summer" definition of success. All five can be fixed by negotiating with Arbelle and Fennick before you commit. I've assumed the $50k/month is the whole expansion cost and that your existing four schools are funded separately. I've also assumed the vote hasn't happened or can still be revisited.

**1. The district MOU is the critical path, and the timeline ignores it**
- Your last MOU took about five months. Even if it takes half that this time, 10 schools can't be signed, onboarded and staffed by January 12. Knowing the people helps, but it doesn't change their review calendar.
- Principal enthusiasm doesn't count here. The district office decides, and you have no commitment from it.
- Without an MOU you can't use school space or student data. That means no tutoring and no benchmark data to measure.
- Questions to ask now: Will Fennick treat this as an amendment to your existing MOU or a new one? Will it sign all 10 schools at once? What is the real calendar, including board or legal review? Can you get a written target date?

**2. The tutor pipeline is thinner than it looks**
- You need about 175 more tutors, which is 2.5 times today's roster, and they come from one source: Harmon practicum students.
- Harmon's spring term starts January 20, so a January 12 start has no tutors. Students also can't be recruited or trained during winter break, so "recruit and train in November and December" is mostly a recruiting plan, not a trained workforce.
- Practicum placement is probably tied to course enrollment and faculty capacity. Ask Harmon how many education students exist per semester. Your 70 may already be a large share of them.
- Practicum tutors typically leave after one semester. You'd need to re-recruit around 245 tutors every term.
- Fixes to explore: a second source such as other colleges, AmeriCorps or community volunteers; paid tutors; a smaller cohort; or a start in late January or February.

**3. Cash flow breaks your reserves**
- Spending is $50k a month from November 1. Arbelle pays quarterly in arrears, about 45 days after the quarter closes.
- The November-January quarter closes January 31, so the first payment lands around mid-March. By then you will have spent roughly $225k (4.5 months), against $150k in reserves.
- The gap doesn't close later. Each payment is always about 4.5 months behind spending, so you carry a permanent float of about $225k. You'd be short by roughly $75k or more, and that assumes nothing else draws on the reserves.
- Spending in the first months may also exceed the average, because hiring and training costs come early.
- Fixes to ask for: an upfront advance (for example one quarter), monthly or quarterly-in-advance payments, or a line of credit secured against the grant award letter. Don't accept the grant until this is solved.

**4. The year-two trigger is probably unreachable as designed**
- You need new schools to beat the comparison group by 15 points on the mid-April benchmark. Your existing schools get about 12 points after a full October-April year, with mature sites and experienced staff.
- New sites that start in January get about 3 months of tutoring, not 6 or 7, with new coordinators and first-term tutors. A realistic expectation is well under 12 points, not above it.
- Even a true 12-point effect carries sampling noise at 40 kids per school, and the comparison group may not match your students.
- So you could run a good program and still lose $600k. Because the money is all-or-nothing, you should treat year two as unlikely, not as a given.
- Fixes to negotiate: a threshold your own data supports (for example 8 to 10 points, or a growth measure), proportional release instead of all-or-nothing, a trigger based on a full year, a one-year delay of the test, or a sliding scale by school. Also agree in writing on who builds the comparison group and how.
- Your 12-point result is a strength, so show Arbelle the data. Funders often prefer a realistic, evidence-based target to an aspirational one.

**5. The scope, sequencing and management risk**
- Going from 4 to 14 schools in one step means 10 new coordinators hired within weeks. Coordinators start November 1, so you'd recruit, vet and hire them almost immediately after a vote.
- Your management and quality control would be stretched across 10 new sites with new staff. Quality is what produced your 12-point effect, so protect it.
- Consider a phased rollout: for example 3 to 4 schools in the first cohort (those with signed MOUs and tutor supply), with the rest in the fall. Ask Arbelle whether the funding can be restructured that way. You also lose the benchmark test less badly if the first cohort is stronger.

**What I'd do before the vote**
1. Call the district office this week for a realistic MOU timeline and ask for it in writing.
2. Ask Harmon for a firm tutor commitment and the spring calendar, and line up a second tutor source.
3. Ask Arbelle for an advance or monthly payments, a phased schedule, and a revised year-two trigger. Build a month-by-month cash model to show them.
4. Take the board a revised version: a staggered start, a trigger tied to evidence, a financing plan, and a clear list of conditions that must be met before spending begins. For example, hire coordinators only for schools with a signed MOU.
5. If Arbelle won't move on the payment terms or the trigger, ask the board to decide explicitly how much risk of loss it will accept. The worst case is spending your reserves and losing the second-year grant.
