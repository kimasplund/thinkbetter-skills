Your draft is close to workable, but it has three weak points: the east lateral closure hits the orchard growers hardest, the Dunmere agreement may bar the closure, and the board has conflicts of interest that could sink ratification. Here is how I would fix those and get a plan both camps will stick to.

**1. Check the Dunmere agreement before anything else**

The $40,000 a year is paid for seepage from the east lateral that recharges the town's wells. Closing that canal on August 1 may breach the 1997 agreement, or cost you the payment. It would also hand Dunmere a grievance. Have counsel read the agreement this week. Then talk to the town early. They may accept a reduced seasonal flow, or a late-season recharge pulse, as long as the wells are protected. Don't let the board discover this after it votes.

**2. Separate the two real interests**

- Orchards need water they can't replace: enough to keep the trees alive, plus the post-harvest water that sets next year's fruit buds. A fallowed row-crop field comes back next year. A dead tree takes years to replace.
- Row-crop growers want fairness under the bylaws, flexibility, and no one gaming the system.

These don't conflict as much as the positions suggest. Cherries are harvested early, so the orchards' critical need comes after harvest, and some of it falls in late summer and fall. Row crops need most of their water in midsummer. That gives you room to trade on timing instead of fighting over shares.

**3. A plan that keeps equal shares but adds options**

- Keep the 55% equal base allocation. This honors the bylaws and avoids a contested vote.
- Let each farm bank part of its allocation for a post-harvest window. Orchards could reserve a share for September to October. This isn't priority. It is a scheduling choice open to every member.
- Keep in-district trading, but add guardrails. Open-price trading in a shortage can produce spikes that push small growers out and let a few large members accumulate water. Options are a price cap or collar, a minimum-use or no-speculation rule, and a public log of trades. Orchards will pay for water they need, and row-crop growers who fallow will be glad to sell. A trading market is probably the best way to meet the orchards' need without breaking equal shares, so long as it is not abusable.
- Add a voluntary fallowing or lease-back program. Growers who plan to fallow, such as Orrin with half his hay, get paid by the district or by buyers for the water they release. This moves water toward the trees without taking it from anyone by force.
- Replace the fixed August 1 date for the east lateral. Tie it to conditions: a review date, and a rule that keeps the lateral open for post-harvest deliveries to orchards on it. A 9% conveyance saving is real, but check what share of the east-lateral users it would strand. If the orchards on that canal are cut off, your plan fails for them whatever their allocation. If the lateral must close, pipe or pump alternatives, or exchanges with other turnouts, might serve them.

**4. Fix the process problems**

- **Conflicts of interest.** Orrin is chair, farms 3,400 acres, and plans to fallow half. Open trading would let him sell water. Neil Benning has backed him, but he leases land from Quillon Land Partners, which now owns it. That affects who holds the vote, and perhaps who has standing as a grower. Neither of these is necessarily improper, but they will be raised. Have both disclose, and consider having them recuse or at least have counsel say whether they need to. Have someone other than Orrin chair that discussion.
- **Ratification.** If any part of the plan departs from equal shares, it needs an acreage-weighted member vote. Quillon owns the Benning acreage, so Quillon's vote may count there, not Neil's. Find out who holds the vote on each parcel before you design anything. Where possible, design the plan so it doesn't depart from equal shares (the banking and trading approach above is meant to do this). Get a legal opinion on whether it does. If it does need a vote, count the votes ahead of time.
- **Compliance.** Both groups will stick to a plan that measures use and has visible, agreed consequences. Install or verify meters, publish weekly use by farm, and agree a trigger for review in advance: for example, if the allocation changes or the reservoir drops below a set level, the board revisits within two weeks.

**5. Before March 18**

1. Counsel reviews the Dunmere agreement and the bylaws on equal shares, trading and voting.
2. Get actual numbers: east-lateral acreage by crop, orchard post-harvest demand, and how much water growers would sell and at what price.
3. Meet the orchard group and the row-crop group separately, then together. Ask each what they need to be able to live with the plan, and what they would give up.
4. Circulate the draft early and invite changes, so members feel they shaped it.
5. Bring the board one package with two or three options and the cost of each, instead of a single draft.

**Assumptions.** I don't know the bylaw wording, the details of the Dunmere agreement, or how the east lateral serves orchards compared with row crops. Each of those could change the advice above, so check them first.
