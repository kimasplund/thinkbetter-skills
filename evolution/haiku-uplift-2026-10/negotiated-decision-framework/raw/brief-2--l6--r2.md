The short answer: your draft will be hard to get through as written, mainly because the east lateral closure lands on the orchard growers at the worst time for them, and it may trigger the acreage-weighted member vote. The fix is to split the problem into pieces that each side can accept. Several facts in your message also need checking before March 18.

**1. The east lateral closure is the biggest risk.**
Cherry bud set for next year's crop happens in the summer, after harvest. An August 1 shutoff cuts water in exactly the window the orchard growers say matters most, and most of them are on that lateral. Expect them to read the plan as "priority for row crops, orchards pay for it." A 9% reduction in conveyance losses is a real saving, but it is a district-wide number that falls on a few farms.
- Ask the orchard growers to name the earliest date their trees can go without water after harvest, and check that against your bud-set timing with an extension agent or an irrigation specialist.
- Options to put to the board: close the lateral later (after bud set), close a different segment, or keep it open and take the loss-reduction elsewhere (lining, valve or scheduling changes).
- If you keep the closure, pair it with compensation or a reserved water block for east lateral parcels.

**2. Check whether the plan needs a member vote.**
Any departure from equal shares needs a member vote weighted by acreage owned. Closing one lateral for some farms and not others is arguably a departure, and a reserved orchard block certainly is. Get the bylaws read by counsel now, so you know whether the board can adopt a plan on March 18 or whether the vote has to come first. If a vote is needed, the March 18 date is probably a planning deadline, not the adoption date.

**3. Voting weight and conflicts need sorting before any vote.**
- The Bennings sold their land to Quillon Land Partners and lease it back. Acreage "owned" may now belong to Quillon, not to Neil Benning. Confirm who holds the title and the votes, and whether Quillon is a member entitled to vote.
- Neil Benning's position is also a conflict question, since he is a tenant on land he no longer owns and is a processing-tomato grower with a direct stake in trading rules. Get a written conflict-of-interest opinion and record any recusal before the vote.
- Orrin Castellane says he will probably fallow half his hay. If he is selling or trading the water he won't use, open trading may benefit him more than anyone, and that should be disclosed. A fallowed field can also be counted differently under the entitlement formula. Check how the 55% is calculated on fallowed acreage.

**4. Dunmere's $40,000 may not be safe.**
The 1997 agreement ties the town's money to seepage from the east lateral that recharges its wells. Closing the lateral early cuts that seepage. Read the agreement for any seepage minimum, termination clause or payment condition before you treat the $40,000 as budgeted. The loss of conveyance losses may be cheap for the district but expensive for Dunmere, which could mean a dispute or a lost payment.

**5. Trading needs guardrails, but it is the easiest part to agree on.**
Open trading at any price can move water to the highest bidder, who is often the grower with the most cash, and it can leave orchards short after harvest. Consider: a trading window that closes before bud-set, a price cap or an approved-price list, and a rule that trades cannot reduce a parcel below its protected minimum.

**A plan both groups could accept (to test with them):**
- Keep equal percentage cuts (55%) as the base for all acreage, which honors the bylaws.
- Add a small reserved post-harvest block for tree crops, funded by a modest, equal percentage adjustment or by the conveyance savings, so row crops take a slightly larger cut to pay for it.
- Make the east lateral closure conditional: schedule it after bud set, or delay it until the Dunmere agreement and the bud-set timing are checked.
- Adopt trading rules with a window and guardrails, as above.
- Get the vote question answered by counsel first, then run the vote, with conflicts recorded.

**Before March 18:**
1. Counsel reads the bylaws, the 1997 Dunmere agreement, and the title records for the Bennings/Quillon parcels.
2. Meet the orchard growers and row-crop growers separately, then together, to test the reserved-block idea.
3. Get bud-set timing from an extension specialist.
4. Confirm the conveyance-loss estimate (9%) with an engineer, since the number drives the lateral decision.

**Assumptions I made:** I took the 55% as the same for every farm, that the bylaws require the acreage vote for any change to equal shares, and that Dunmere's payment depends on lateral seepage. Each of these needs checking against the actual documents. I have not seen the bylaws, the agreement, or the title records.
