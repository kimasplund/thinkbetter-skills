Winning the February 12 vote is the smaller problem. The bigger risk is that a vote you win on a thin margin leaves you with a shrinking school, a broken promise to the Halloran family, and a lower-school community that feels it was run over. Here is how I would approach it, starting with the facts that could sink the plan before it reaches the board.

**Check three things before anything else**

1. **The 1958 gift language.** The plaque says the land was given "for the education of the children of Brennock." Ask counsel whether that wording, or the deed itself, restricts sale or requires a reversion, a cy-pres petition, or notice to the Halloran heirs or a successor foundation. If there is any doubt, a quiet legal opinion now is far cheaper than a challenge after the sale closes. Vellacott's letter of intent may also have a diligence or title contingency you need to know about.

2. **The Ardelle pledge.** The $800k still to come is for the north-campus outdoor learning program. If the campus closes, that money may be restricted to a use that no longer exists. Talk to Ardelle's program officer before the vote, not after. Ask whether they would redirect the balance to the south campus or to an agreed successor program. If they say no, the sale's real proceeds are $800k lower than the headline.

3. **The south-campus conditions.** The 700 ceiling is not the only condition. Traffic marshals, the 9pm event limit, and the Sycamore Lane agreement were written for a campus that carries the gym and its current load. Adding 230 K-5 students means more drop-off traffic at the same entrance, with younger children arriving and leaving at fixed times. Ask the facilities consultant and a traffic engineer to model peak drop-off, and find out whether the planning approval requires a new hearing for the lower-school building. The neighbours who fought the gym will notice.

**What the numbers say, and what they do not**

The $13.5M sale is not yet money. It is a signed letter of intent. The $8M building is a plan, not a budget line. The $1.1M deficit is real and needs solving, but closing a campus is one of several ways to solve it, and it is the one that is hardest to reverse. Before the board votes, ask whether the same savings could come from a smaller set of changes: staffing ratios, a tuition or aid review, a capital campaign that uses the Ardelle money on its current terms, or leasing or selling part of the north site while keeping a K-5 presence there. Put these options next to the sale on one page so trustees see a real choice, not a yes-or-no on the only plan on the table.

Also, the vote on February 12 comes before re-enrolment results are due on March 1. Your plan assumes 92% of families return. If you vote before you know that number, you are voting on a forecast. Consider asking the board to approve the sale in principle, conditional on re-enrolment reaching a stated threshold by March 1, with a final vote after that. This gives trustees a way to say yes without betting the school on one date.

**Getting ten of fifteen trustees**

Trustees will mostly vote on three questions: is the deficit real, is closing the north campus the least bad fix, and will the school survive the next five years. Lead with the deficit and the enrolment trend, which nobody disputes, and then show the options. Do not lead with the sale price. A sale price invites the question of who gets to decide the use of the land, which is the question you most need to avoid being the first to answer.

Identify the trustees who are most likely to be swing votes, and meet each one before the meeting. Ask what would make them comfortable. Expect that some will want the Halloran question answered, some will want the teachers' position addressed, and some will want the Ardelle money settled. If you can give a clear answer to each of those, you are likely to have the votes. If you cannot, it is better to learn that in January than in the room.

**Keeping parents and staff with you**

Lower-school parents are reacting to two concrete things: the commute, and little children sharing a campus with teenagers. Both can be addressed in the plan rather than argued away. Options include a dedicated lower-school wing with separate entrance and play space, staggered schedules, a transport offer or shuttle between campuses for the first year, and a written commitment on supervision and separation. Make the commitments specific, with dates and named owners, so parents can hold you to them.

For teachers, the worry is jobs. Say early and in writing what happens to every role: which positions move, which are cut, which are protected for a stated period, and how severance or redeployment works. A teacher who knows the terms is much more likely to stay engaged than one who hears rumours. Offer teachers a seat in the implementation planning for the lower-school building, so they help shape the move rather than only receive it.

Tell families and staff before the board sees the full plan, so the vote is not the first time they hear it. Hold listening sessions at both campuses, record the concerns, and respond publicly to the common ones. Parents who feel heard are far more likely to re-enrol, and re-enrolment by March 1 is the number your budget depends on.

**Suggested sequence**

- Now to early December: legal review of the 1958 gift and the Vellacott letter; talk to Ardelle; have the consultant and traffic engineer model the south campus with 640 students.
- Early December to January: meet swing trustees; present the options page; hold parent and staff sessions; publish the lower-school plan.
- January 10: re-enrolment contracts go out, with the commitments above in writing.
- Before February 12: decide whether to ask for an in-principle vote with a March 1 condition, based on what the legal review and Ardelle conversation show.
- March 1: re-enrolment results. Final decision and sale only if the threshold is met.

The plan you bring to the board should be one that a skeptical trustee can defend to a parent, and that a parent can accept even if they disagree. That is what turns a 10-vote majority into a school that stays together after the vote.
