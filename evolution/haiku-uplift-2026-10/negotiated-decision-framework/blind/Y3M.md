Your draft has three problems that will decide whether it sticks, and each one can be fixed before March 18.

**1. The August 1 east lateral closure is the weakest piece.**
- It hits the orchard growers hardest. Many of them are on that lateral, and the post-harvest water they say matters most is exactly what an August 1 closure removes. For cherries, harvest is typically done by July, so a closure just after it cuts off the bud-set irrigation. Equal shares on paper won't help them if the water can't physically reach them.
- It may breach the 1997 Dunmere agreement. You get $40,000 a year for seepage that recharges the town's wells. Read the agreement before the meeting to see whether it requires minimum flows or notice, and talk to Dunmere early. Losing the payment or starting a dispute could cost more than the roughly 9% conveyance saving is worth.
- Alternatives to test: close the lateral later, such as after a September bud-set irrigation. Or keep it running at a reduced schedule. Or deliver orchard water through a short, scheduled post-harvest window and close it after that. Ask your operations staff what the 9% saving is worth in acre-feet and dollars, and what a September 15 closure would cost by comparison. That turns a fight over principle into a number.

**2. Make equal shares the default and add a voluntary, priced way to depart from it.**
You don't need to give the orchards priority. Pure equal shares with unrestricted trading lets whoever can pay buy the water, and it hands a windfall to anyone fallowing. Priority for the orchards would depart from the bylaws, which triggers the acreage-weighted ratification vote and gives the row-crop side a veto. A middle design that stays within equal shares:
- Everyone gets 55%, but members can choose when to take it. Each farm splits its allocation into in-season and post-harvest portions. Orchards bank water for the post-harvest window. Row-crop farms use theirs earlier. This costs nobody anything.
- Trading is allowed inside the district but with guardrails. Options are a published price or a cap, or a district-run exchange where offers are posted openly. Price caps are a harder sell, but with a transparent exchange the orchards can bid for post-harvest water and row-crop growers who fallow can sell it. The aim is that nobody can claim a private deal squeezed them.
- Hold a small reserve, perhaps 3 to 5% of supply, for permanent-crop emergencies. A tree-loss hardship pool is easier for row-crop growers to accept than priority, because it is limited and applies to anyone with perennial crops, not just the cherry growers.
- Revisit mid-season. Set a review date, such as June 1 or after the basin authority updates its allocation, so that if the 55% turns out to be wrong, the plan adjusts by rule and not by another fight.

**3. Deal with the conflicts of interest openly, now.**
- Orrin is chair, strongly favors open trading, and says he'll fallow about half his 3,400 acres of hay. That means he could have a lot of water to sell under the rule he's promoting. That may be entirely innocent, but others will see it. Ask him to disclose his expected sales position, and consider having someone else chair the vote on the trading rules, or at least have the board adopt a written recusal or disclosure practice.
- Neil Benning has backed Orrin throughout. His 2,900 acres were sold to Quillon Land Partners and leased back. If a plan departing from equal shares goes to an acreage-weighted vote of owners, Quillon's vote, not Neil's, counts for that land. So find out what Quillon wants. They may care about the land's value over the lease term, and they may not share Neil's view. Check also whether Neil's board seat and the lease affect his standing under your bylaws.
- Check what the bylaws say about who counts as an owner, how the weighting works, and whether trading rules or the lateral closure count as "a departure from equal shares." Your counsel or the bylaws will answer that, and it decides whether you need the ratification vote at all. A plan built to stay within equal shares may avoid it. Be careful, though, that you aren't engineering around a vote that members expect to have.

**Process to get to March 18**
1. Spend this week on facts. Get the water numbers by lateral and crop, the cost of different closure dates, the Dunmere terms, and the bylaws language.
2. Meet the orchard growers and the row-crop growers separately first. Ask each: what is the one thing you must have, and what can you live with? Orchards will probably say post-harvest water and protection against buy-outs. Row-crop growers will probably say no priority and bylaw fidelity. Those overlap more than the public positions suggest.
3. Bring the board one package, not three options, with the facts attached: equal shares, a flexible timing split, a transparent exchange, a small hardship reserve, a later or negotiated lateral closure, and a mid-season review.
4. Get commitments in writing in the adopted plan: what happens if the allocation changes, how trades are reported, and what the penalties are for violating the plan. People stick to plans they helped shape, can verify, and know will be reviewed.
5. If a ratification vote is needed, count the votes ahead of time using acreage owned and talk to the large owners, including Quillon, before the board meeting.
