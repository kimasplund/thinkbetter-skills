Short version: keep equal shares as the base, which protects you on the bylaws and the ratification vote. Then fix the two parts of your draft that quietly break it, the August 1 closure and unregulated trading. Use what the board already has in hand to give the orchard growers what they actually need, which is water after harvest, not a permanent priority.

## What I'd change in the draft

**1. The August 1 east lateral closure is the biggest problem.**
Your orchard growers' core argument is that post-harvest water sets next year's fruit buds. Cherries come off by roughly June to July, so the water they care about is exactly the August and September water. Many of them sit on the east lateral, and you're proposing to shut it right then. As written, the plan is "equal shares" on paper and a cut aimed at the orchards in practice. They'll see that, and it gives them a good reason to demand priority and take it to a weighted vote.

It also puts the Dunmere agreement at risk. The $40,000 a year and the 1997 terms cover seepage from that lateral that recharges the town's wells. I haven't seen the agreement, so check whether an early closure triggers a reduction, a notice requirement or a breach. Get the district's attorney to read it before March 18. If it does, the 9% conveyance saving may cost you more than it's worth.

Options, from least to most disruptive:
- Keep the lateral running at reduced flow through the post-harvest window, and close it later (mid-September or after the bud-set period).
- Close it on August 1 only if the orchards on it can be served another way, such as a delivery point or an interim turnout. I'd need an engineer's view on whether that's feasible.
- If the closure stays, publish the conveyance-loss math and show who gains from the 9%.

**2. Open trading at "whatever price they agree" will not hold in a 55% year.**
In a shortage, an unregulated market sends water to whoever can pay most, and tree-crop growers who can't fallow will be bidding against each other. That is the orchard growers' fear, and it's reasonable. Row-crop growers also lose if the price spikes and a few big sellers set it. Trading is worth keeping because it lets people who will fallow anyway sell to people who can't. But it needs some structure:
- A district-run exchange or posting board, so every trade is visible and recorded.
- Either a price ceiling or a published reference price, set before the season.
- Meter and report every transfer, so you can see whether the conveyance losses and the east lateral are really being managed.
- A rule that trades are for use within the season and can't be carried over or moved out of the district.

**3. Let members shape their 55%, not just receive it.**
Equal shares can still be flexible on timing. Let each member schedule their allocation across the season, with a floor and a ceiling on monthly draws. A cherry grower can then hold back water for August and September. Row-crop growers can front-load. This costs the district little and gives the orchards most of what a priority would. It also doesn't depart from equal shares, so it shouldn't need the weighted ratification vote. Confirm that with counsel and your bylaws.

**4. Create a pooled "post-harvest reserve" funded by willing sellers.**
Some members will fallow regardless, and the board chair has said he may fallow about half of his hay. Offer a voluntary program where the district pays a fair price for water from fallowed ground, and that water is reserved for permanent-crop needs in the bud-set window. Funding can come from assessments, or from members who want the water paying into the pool. The orchards get a path to extra water without a legal priority. Row-crop growers get paid for water they weren't going to use. Nobody's entitlement is cut.

## The people and the politics

**Orchard growers.** Their stated position is priority. Their real interest is surviving a bad year without losing trees, and in particular getting water after harvest. Items 1, 3 and 4 give them that. Ask them to put numbers on it: acres, how much water per acre in the bud-set window, what a lost year costs. A specific, small ask is easier to grant than a general demand for priority.

**Row-crop growers.** They want the bylaw promise honoured and don't want to fund someone else's crop. The plan above keeps equal shares, keeps trading and pays fallowers. Their concession is that trading has a ceiling and the lateral stays open a bit longer.

**Orrin Castellane (board chair).** Be careful here. He's firmly for equal shares and open trading, and he's likely to have surplus water to sell, since he's considering fallowing half of 3,400 acres of alfalfa. That is a personal financial interest in how trading is priced. It doesn't make his views wrong, and I'm not suggesting bad faith, but if the orchards believe the chair is writing rules that profit him, they'll resist any plan he's seen to drive. Protect him and the process:
- Have the board adopt a short written disclosure and recusal practice before the vote, covering anything where a member's own water, sales or leases are affected. Apply it to every board member.
- Consider having a different board member, ideally one acceptable to both camps, run the discussion on trading rules.
- Talk to Orrin privately first and explain this as protecting him.

**Neil Benning.** He has backed Orrin at every meeting, and the Bennings sold their 2,900 tomato acres to Quillon Land Partners and lease them back. Two things follow:
- Who owns the water entitlement, and who decides how it's used? If Quillon owns the land, Quillon may hold the entitlement and the acreage-weighted vote. Neil may be voting on the board but not holding the votes that count at ratification. Check the records and the lease terms. If Quillon would be a major vote in any ratification, it is a stakeholder you need to talk to directly. Its interests (lease economics, selling water, long-term land value) may differ from Neil's.
- Neil may have limits on his own flexibility under the lease. Ask him, don't assume.

**The weighted vote is the real power map.** It counts acres owned, so the large landowners decide any departure from equal shares. Before you meet anyone, list the members by owned acres and work out who actually controls a majority. Orchard growers are probably many farms on fewer acres, so they can win the board's sympathy and still lose a ratification vote. That is why they may be more open to a package than to a fight, and why you shouldn't count on the vote threat alone. A plan that stays within equal shares and doesn't trigger the vote is also the one that can't be ambushed by it.

## How to get it adopted and make it stick

1. **Before March 18, hold one-to-one conversations** with the orchard group's leaders, Orrin, Neil, and Quillon if it holds the entitlement. Ask each what a bad outcome looks like and what they could accept. Don't bring a draft yet.
2. **Bring data to the board**: the Dunmere terms, the cost of closing the lateral at August 1 versus a later date, the number of acres and volume needed in the bud-set window, and the trading price history if you have it.
3. **Present a package, not a menu.** The pieces depend on each other: equal shares, timing flexibility, a regulated exchange, a post-harvest reserve and a later or conditional closure. Each camp gives something in exchange for something. If it comes apart item by item, the orchards lose the protection and the row-crop growers lose nothing, which is not a deal.
4. **Make commitments explicit and checkable.** Meter deliveries, publish monthly use and trades, and set a mid-season review (for example, after the June cherry harvest and again in late July) where the board can adjust the reserve or the closure date. Agree in advance what triggers a change, such as reservoir level, actual inflows or the basin authority revising the 55% figure.
5. **Say what happens if someone breaks the rules.** Use graduated consequences, such as a water charge against next year's allocation for overuse, so compliance doesn't rest on goodwill.
6. **Record the decision and the reasoning** in the minutes, and note who agreed to what, so the plan isn't reopened every time the weather changes.

## What I'd want to confirm first
- The Dunmere agreement's actual terms on the east lateral.
- What the bylaws say about allocation timing and about trades. This decides whether the flexible-timing and reserve ideas need a weighted vote.
- Who holds the entitlements on the Benning land, and the lease terms.
- A rough count of acres and water needed by the orchard growers after harvest. I've assumed it's a small share of the total, which is what makes this solvable. If it's large, the conversation gets harder and you may need a different tradeoff, such as a modest, time-limited, compensated priority.

My confidence is fairly high that the structure above is the right direction, and lower on the details. They depend on the Dunmere terms, the entitlement ownership and the actual post-harvest volume, all of which you can check in the next few weeks.
