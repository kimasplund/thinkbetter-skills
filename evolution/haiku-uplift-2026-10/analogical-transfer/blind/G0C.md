**Recommendation:** Don't adopt the emergency-department model as proposed. Do adopt the parts of it that fit your problem, and design the pilot to test those parts.

**1. Triage reorders a queue but doesn't shorten it**

You receive about 640 filings a month and close about 590, so you are short by about 50 a month. Triage can't change that.

- Category 1 cases would be heard in three weeks. Under a fixed deficit, that speed comes out of the waits of categories 4 and 5.
- "As capacity allows" means that category 5 cases may never be heard while the deficit lasts. Category 5 wait times would grow without limit, not stay at 14 months.
- An ED can use this approach because most patients are low-acuity and are treated quickly once seen, and because the queue clears overnight. Your queue doesn't clear. It has run a deficit for two years.

The committee should be told this plainly. Any pilot that doesn't also address throughput or demand will show better numbers for the favored categories and worse numbers for the rest. That is a reshuffle, not an improvement.

**2. The urgency signal is weak**

- In an ED, urgency is judged clinically. Here it comes from a free-text box that 70% of tenants complete. Once urgent cases are heard sooner, filers will write to the category they want. The 30% who skip the box, who may be the least resourced, would be sorted into the slow lanes by default.
- The dispute is about money held by the landlord, and the harm of waiting is mostly financial. A tenant owed a deposit is hurt by delay in a fairly uniform way, except for real hardship (eviction, homelessness, no money for a new bond). Hardship does exist and is worth identifying. But the categories need defined, checkable criteria, not a judgment of how compelling the story reads.
- Delay favors the party holding the deposit. About 85% of claims are brought by tenants. A system that deprioritizes "ordinary" claims quietly rewards landlords who wait.

**3. Your data points to a different problem: scheduling**

- About 60% of cases settle or withdraw, mostly in the four weeks before the hearing date, after notices go out. Parties act when a deadline is close.
- Today the deadline is about 14 months away for a new filing, and it only becomes real near the end. That is the same behavior as in any queue where the date is fixed at filing.
- Roughly 40% of cases need an actual hearing, about 256 a month. That is well below the 640 filings and probably within what six adjudicators can do, if the wasted slots were refilled.

So the better levers are:

- **Overbook hearing slots.** Use the known settlement pattern, as airlines and clinics do, so that late settlements don't leave empty slots. Set the overbooking rate from your own data on settlement timing.
- **Move the forcing event earlier.** Hold a short early-resolution step (a case conference, mediation call or required response from the landlord) within a few weeks of filing. Because parties settle when a deadline bites, a nearer deadline should bring settlements forward and shrink the pool that reaches a hearing date.
- **Don't set a hearing date at filing for every case.** Allocate dates later, closer to when a hearing is realistic.
- **Fast-track simple cases.** An undefended claim, or one where the facts are agreed and only the amount is in dispute, can be resolved on the papers or in short hearings. This is the genuinely ED-like part: streaming by complexity, which isn't the same as streaming by urgency.

**4. What to borrow from the ED**

- A rapid front-end review within 48 hours is a good idea, but its purpose should be streaming and early contact with the parties: undefended, simple, contested, or complex, plus a hardship flag.
- Flow metrics, such as time to first contact, time to disposition and the share of slots used.
- Senior early decision-making, with an adjudicator or senior officer reviewing the stream decision.

**5. Pilot design**

*Scope*
- Apply it to new filings only. The 9,400 legacy cases need a separate plan, such as a dated backlog drive, and shouldn't be left to "as capacity allows".
- Split the pilot into arms. For example, filings by week of receipt go alternately to the proposed five-category triage or to the streaming and early-resolution model. Or use stepped introduction by case type, with the rest as a control. The aim is to compare, not to run one design and call it a success.

*Guardrails*
- Set a maximum wait for every category, with automatic escalation as cases age. "As capacity allows" should not be allowed to mean never.
- Check the urgency criteria. Sample and audit the triage decisions, and test whether category assignment tracks the free-text wording or the facts.
- Handle the 30% with no free text by a neutral default, or ask the question at triage by a short call.

*Measures*

A 14-month wait means hearing times won't move much within a pilot's first year, so use leading indicators:
- Time to first contact with both parties.
- Settlement rate and timing (share settling more than four weeks before the hearing date).
- Share of hearing slots used.
- Triage accuracy and consistency between officers.
- Median wait by category and by landlord or tenant filer.
- Appeals or complaints.
- Backlog growth, which is the headline measure. It should stop growing at 50 a month.

*Decision rule*

Pre-register what result would justify continuing, changing or stopping the pilot before January.

**6. Points to check before the meeting**

I'm assuming your closure rate is about 590 a month and that the 60% settlement figure is typical of recent years. Please check these:
- Hearing capacity per adjudicator and how many hearings a month the 40% share represents.
- How long hearings take, which tells you whether fast-tracking is feasible.
- The age profile of the backlog: how many cases are already settled in practice but still open.
- Whether the tribunal can add capacity or change the rules (for example, requiring the landlord to lodge the deposit, or interest on withheld deposits, which would reduce the incentive to delay).

**Suggested message to the committee**

"The ED model is right that we should assess cases early and stream them. It is wrong to assume that urgency ranking will fix a backlog that exists because we receive more than we can decide. We should pilot front-end streaming, earlier settlement pressure and overbooked scheduling against the proposed urgency categories, with guardrails on maximum waits and a pre-agreed test of success."
