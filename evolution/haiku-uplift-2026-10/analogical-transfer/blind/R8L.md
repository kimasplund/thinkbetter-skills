The emergency-department idea is worth piloting, but not in the form Dr Messer has described, and the pilot should be designed to show whether it helps at all. Triage changes who waits and for how long. It does not add hearing capacity, and your figures point to capacity as the root problem.

**Where the ED comparison holds**

| Emergency department | Deposit tribunal |
|---|---|
| Acuity: how fast delay causes harm | Urgency: how fast delay causes hardship |
| Waiting room queue | Hearing list |
| Patients reassessed while waiting | Filings re-categorised when facts change |
| Rapid first review | Review of every new filing within 48 hours |

**Where it breaks down**

1. **Capacity arithmetic.** Intake is about 640 a month and the backlog grows by about 50 a month, so you are clearing roughly 590 cases a month. At that rate 9,400 open cases is about 16 months of work, roughly consistent with the 14-month median. Triage reorders the queue but cannot shorten it unless throughput rises. If category 1 (three weeks) takes a large share of filings, every category below it waits longer, and category 5 ("as capacity allows") may in practice never be heard. Before any target times are published, the committee should work out how many hearing slots each month each category would absorb.

2. **The urgency signal is weak.** An ED assesses clinical state. Here the input is a self-reported free-text box that about 30% of tenants leave blank, and the stakes are similar in most cases, since the landlord holds the deposit until a ruling or settlement. Expect most filers to describe their case as urgent, so categories will drift upward. Structured questions work better than free text: whether the tenant has moved out, rent owed or an eviction notice, any landlord deadline, the deposit amount, and whether hardship can be documented. Those answers can also be audited.

3. **Fairness across parties.** About 85% of claims come from tenants. A system that ranks by claimant urgency could be seen as favouring one side, so the pilot should check outcomes for landlord-initiated claims as well.

**The larger lever in your figures**

About 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn before hearing, and most of that happens in the four weeks before the hearing date, after the notice has gone out. That wastes adjudicator time and leaves prepared slots empty. Options to test alongside triage:

- Schedule a settlement or mediation contact at a fixed point after filing and before the hearing notice, so notices go only to cases still live.
- Book hearing slots with a modest overbooking allowance based on your measured late-settlement and no-show rate, with a plan for days when more parties attend than expected.
- Release slots from withdrawn cases back to the list straight away.

Moving even a share of late settlements earlier would free capacity faster than reordering the queue.

**Pilot design**

- Do not switch the whole intake in January. Assign new filings to triage or to the current process by alternating weeks or random draw, so the effect can be separated from the backlog trend.
- Fix the measures before starting: median and 90th-percentile time from filing to hearing, by category and overall; share settled before hearing and how far ahead of the hearing date; empty slots; cases waiting beyond their category's target; and reopened or appealed decisions.
- Watch the ageing of category 5 each month. If it passes a set limit, a capacity rule is needed.
- Each month, have a second officer re-score a sample of categorisations to measure consistency.
- Write a re-categorisation rule (for example, when either party supplies new information) and log every change.
- Run it for at least six months. Filing-stage measures such as triage speed and category mix will show within weeks, but hearing waits will take more than a year to show, so report them in stages.

**What to tell the committee**

- The ED approach suits the problem of ordering work by urgency, and a controlled pilot is reasonable.
- On its own it will not cut the 14-month wait. That needs throughput above 640 a month or fewer cases entering hearing lists unnecessarily.
- Pair triage with the settlement-timing changes, which target the largest source of waste.
- Compare the pilot against a control group and report results by category before any change becomes permanent.

Assumptions: the 640 monthly intake and the net 50-a-month growth are steady, and "grown by roughly 50 a month" means net of cases closed. I have not seen the claim form or current hearing-slot numbers, so the capacity point is a calculation to check against real data rather than a settled finding.
