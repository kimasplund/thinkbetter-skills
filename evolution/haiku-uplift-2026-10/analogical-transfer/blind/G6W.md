Ines's library model fits the logistics well: loans, a holds queue, renewals and late fees are all things a library already runs. Where I would change her rules is at the points where the thing being lent is alive, because a returned jar is not a returned book.

Assumptions: I don't know your local food-safety rules or your donor agreements, so the points below are a starting position to check, not legal advice.

**1. Never reshelve a returned jar.**
This is the change I would make first. Last spring's kefir loss came from mixing outside material into shelf jars, and a returned jar carries an unknown history: it may have been fed with other flour, kept next to other cultures, or left too long. Make returns go to a quarantine fridge. A volunteer checks smell, rise and appearance, and the jar is either discarded or fully re-propagated from a verified reference before it can circulate again. Make this a written rule so nobody has to decide case by case.

**2. Separate the archive from the circulating copies.**
Donors asked that their original lines be kept going, and a loan system with no backup means one bad loan can end a line permanently. Keep a small reference stock of each culture that never leaves the building. Freeze it, or dry it into flakes where the culture tolerates that, and check viability on a set schedule. Circulating jars are copies that can be replaced from the reference. This protects the donor promise better than anything else you can do, and it is the single change most likely to reduce Teodor's load over time.

**3. Lend copies, not mother cultures.**
Consider whether a borrower takes a starter portion with written feeding instructions rather than a full jar, or a dried sample they revive at home. That shortens the time a fragile culture is out of your control. It also makes the replacement charge easier to justify, because the loss is one portion, not a whole line.

**4. Rethink the replacement charge and the late fee.**
A flat replacement charge for a dead or spoiled jar will teach people to hide problems and to avoid borrowing fragile cultures. Charge only for clear neglect or misuse, and treat spoilage that can't be attributed to the borrower as a normal cost of the commons. The €3 a week late fee is fine for keeping the queue moving, but a jar that is four weeks overdue is also a jar that is being neglected, so consider a welfare check or a return call before the fee starts.

**5. The 1890s rye needs a different plan.**
80 people in a queue with four-week loans is about six years for the last person. Either multiply that line into more copies from the reference stock, or let the holds queue run in parallel across several volunteer-held copies. Also be honest with members that home copies drift. The home rye already smells and rises differently after a few months, so "the same starter" needs a lineage note: which reference it came from and when. Without that, a member's home rye and your rye can diverge and no one can say which is the original.

**6. Feeding responsibility.**
Renewals should depend on the borrower confirming they have fed the culture, not only on the calendar. This turns a loan into a maintenance commitment, which is what keeps the jar alive, and it reduces the burden on Teodor.

**On the membership cards.**
Three weeks is enough time to make the card generic. Print the name, the library-style loan terms and a contact line, but leave out any promise about preservation or lineage until the reference-stock and quarantine rules are agreed. Those rules are the things members will actually rely on.

**Priority order, if you can only do three things before March:**
1. Quarantine returns, with no direct reshelving.
2. Set up a reference stock that never circulates.
3. Write the lineage note for the rye and the other popular cultures.

Everything else (holds, fees, renewals) can be adjusted after you have run it for a season.
