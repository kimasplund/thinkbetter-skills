Ines's library model gets a lot right: one jar per borrower, a holds queue, and returned jars going back on the shelf all make sense for a shared collection. The places I would change are the ones where a culture differs from a book, which is that it changes while it is out, it can die or be contaminated, and it needs regular feeding. Here is what I would change before the cards are printed.

**1. Stop returned jars going straight back on the shelf.**
A jar that comes back has been out with someone who may have fed it differently, may have mixed it with something from home, or may have let it sit. Before it re-enters circulation, it should be checked, fed, and held for a short period (for example one or two feeding cycles) in a quarantine shelf. Last spring's kefir loss came from exactly this kind of mixing, so a returned jar should never be topped up or combined with anything else, and the rule should say so in writing.

**2. Keep a master line at the commons, separate from the loan jars.**
Families asked that their original lines be kept going, and the rye volunteers' home portions already smell and rise differently after a few months. That is drift: the circulating jars are slowly becoming different cultures from the ones donated. The commons should keep a master stock of every line that is never lent, and the loan jars should be refreshed from the master on a schedule (for example every few months, or whenever a jar comes back from a long loan). Lending then never becomes the only copy.

**3. Replace the flat €3-a-week overdue fee with a feeding-based rule.**
A fine makes sense for a book that is simply late. For a living culture, the real harm is a jar going unfed, and a fee does not stop that. A 4-week loan with two renewals is up to 12 weeks out, which is long enough to kill some cultures. I would ask borrowers to report a feed or send a photo at each renewal, and charge the replacement fee only for clear neglect or contamination, not for a jar that dies despite care. "Dead or spoiled" needs a written definition, or every return becomes a dispute at the desk.

**4. Deal with the queue for the 1890s rye.**
80 people want one culture, and with two jars at most per line and up to 12 weeks per loan, a queue like that could run for many months. Options to consider are: dried or frozen portions (sourdough starters can usually be dried, which gives borrowers a backup and reduces demand for live jars), a second master of this line at a different volunteer's house or site (kept separate, so a single contamination cannot wipe it out), or a shorter loan limit for the most-requested lines so the queue moves. I would check the drying and freezing methods against a food-safety reference before promising them to members.

**5. Plan the feeding load, not just the rules.**
Teodor is already at his limit, and every quarantine check, master refresh, and returned-jar inspection adds work. The lending model moves feeding to borrowers during loans, which helps, but only if the commons has a clear schedule for the work that stays in-house. Name one or two volunteers for the quarantine and master work and write down their hours before March.

**What I would keep:** one jar per borrower, the holds queue, and the four-week loan with renewals. Those work for a shared collection, and they are simple enough for members to understand.

**Assumptions I made:** I do not know the current size of each line, how quickly each one drifts, or whether the commons has space for a quarantine shelf or a second site. If any of those are different, the rules above would need adjusting. Before the cards go to print, I would check the feeding schedule and the quarantine rule with whoever has run the live cultures longest.
