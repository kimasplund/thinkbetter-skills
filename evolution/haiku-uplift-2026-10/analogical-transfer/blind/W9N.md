Short answer: the electricity analogy holds for the goal (cut demand at the right moment) but not for the mechanism. As proposed, I would not put the programme in front of the board for next summer unchanged. Some parts are worth keeping, and I would take a redesigned version to the board.

**Where the analogy breaks**

1. **Lag.** A power peak is balanced in minutes, so an hourly price and a call on a few hours' notice can work. Here a release takes about 3 days to reach the Lower intake and up to 5 days to reach the tail. A shortage called on Tuesday cannot be fixed by Tuesday's reductions. Growers must be told days ahead, and the call has to rest on a forecast, not on a live reading.

2. **Return flow.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. So if the Upper orchards cut diversions by 1 m³, only about 0.6 m³ reaches the Lower intake. Any payment for "water saved" overstates the benefit to the tail by roughly two-thirds if it is based on diversions at the headgate. The payment should be tied to the downstream effect, or at least adjusted for the 40% return.

3. **Location.** The shortfall falls on the tail of the Lower Canal, yet the programme pays growers wherever they are. Paying Upper Canal orchards for cuts that mostly stay in the river and reach the tail at 60% is poor value. The Lower Canal's annual crops (barley, hay, grass seed) are probably the cheaper and more flexible source of reductions, but they are also the ones that suffer most at the tail.

4. **Crop flexibility.** Apple and cherry orchards are perennial. Cutting irrigation for a season can damage trees, so the price needed to get reductions from them may be high and the uptake low. Barley and hay can be cut back more easily. This matters for who enrols and what it costs.

5. **Baseline gaming.** A "below your own three-year average" baseline rewards growers who inflated use in past years, and it pays for reductions growers would have made anyway in a dry year. Any baseline needs checking against metered history and against what the water right allows.

6. **Measurement.** The programme needs reliable diversion metering for every enrolled grower. I do not know whether you have it. Without it, the payments cannot be verified and disputes will follow.

**What I would put in front of the board**

- **Keep the signal, change its timing.** An hourly price is least useful here because no one can act on it within the 3–5 day lag. A daily or multi-day outlook (projected tail shortfall and the reduction needed, published by a fixed lead time) is more useful.
- **Pay for availability as well as delivery.** A fixed payment for committing to reduce by a set volume when called, plus a per-m³ payment for verified reduction, is more reliable than a pure per-m³ scheme and easier to budget.
- **Adjust for return flow and location.** Either pay on measured gauge gain at the Lower intake, or apply a stated factor (for example 0.6) to Upper Canal reductions. Start with the Lower Canal, where the shortage lands, and widen only if the numbers justify it.
- **Pilot before scaling.** Run a limited pilot in one summer with pre-set measures: volume saved, change in tail deliveries, cost per m³ delivered to the tail, and grower take-up. Decide on scale-up after the season.
- **Check the legal basis first.** Confirm that the board can pay for reductions, that reduced water can legally be left in the river, and how any traded or banked volume is treated under the water rights.
- **Consider non-price levers.** Rotation or scheduled delivery to the tail, and timed releases from Tarrow to match the 3–5 day travel time, may recover part of the 18% shortfall at lower cost than a payment scheme.

**What I would need before the vote**

- Tarrow storage levels and the historical release schedules
- Gauge records above the Lower intake for the last three summers, to check the 40% return figure and its seasonal variation
- Metering coverage on both canals
- The water-right conditions on each canal
- Water needed per hectare for each crop, to estimate how much reduction each grower can realistically offer

**Assumptions I made:** I took the 40% return as a steady seasonal ratio and the 3–5 day travel times as fixed. I did not have data on metering, rights, or crop water needs, so the points above are conditional on those.

**Bottom line for the board:** Her instinct to use a demand-response style of programme is sound. The version she has proposed would pay for the wrong thing, at the wrong time, from the wrong place, and it could not be verified. A redesigned, limited pilot with lead-time-based calls, return-flow-adjusted payments and Lower Canal focus is a reasonable step for next summer.
