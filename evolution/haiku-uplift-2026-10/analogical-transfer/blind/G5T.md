## Short answer for the committee

Dr Messer is right about one thing: the Tribunal has no front-end sorting, and a queue run strictly by order of receipt is a poor way to handle a mixed caseload. But an urgency-ranked queue on its own will not fix your problem, and on your numbers it could make some things worse. I would tell the committee to pilot a version of the ED idea that borrows more of what emergency departments actually do than the five-category label, and to build in safeguards the ED does not need.

## Why urgency triage alone does not fit

**1. It reorders the queue and does not shrink it.** About 640 cases come in each month and about 590 leave, so you are 50 short every month. If category 1 gets a hearing in three weeks, someone else waits longer. The ED has the same arithmetic, which is why a good ED treats triage as a front-end step and then works on throughput behind it, in beds, diagnostics and discharge. Nothing in the proposal yet says what raises throughput.

**2. Category 5, "as capacity allows", would never be seen.** With the backlog growing, spare capacity never appears. Category 5 would be a permanent holding pen, and the real wait for those filers would be open-ended. An ED can leave low-acuity patients waiting because they leave, get better or are treated in a separate stream. A tenant who is owed a deposit has no such exit.

**3. In an ED the harm of delay is clinical, and a clinician judges it. Here the harm is financial, and the filer describes it.**
- A nurse assesses the patient's condition. Your triage officer would mostly read a free-text box the filer wrote.
- Seventy percent of tenant filers already complete it, so most will say it is urgent. Expect category inflation within a few months. If category 1 fills up, the three-week target either fails or eats the capacity meant for everyone else.
- Articulate, represented or experienced filers will write better boxes. Self-represented tenants and those with limited English will be ranked lower for reasons that have nothing to do with need. You would be building a bias into the queue.

**4. The delay has a beneficiary.** The landlord holds the deposit until a ruling or settlement, so waiting costs tenants and costs landlords nothing. The 85% of claims brought by tenants are the ones pushed down the ranking when urgency is not shown. Any scheme that lets low-ranked cases wait longer rewards the holder of the money. The 15% brought by landlords have no equivalent hardship to cite and could sit in the lowest categories by default. You need a view on whether that is acceptable, and it will be asked.

**5. The ED has a different problem with cases that resolve themselves.** An ED treats nearly every arrival. You have the opposite: about 60% of your cases settle or are withdrawn, and most of that happens in the four weeks before the hearing date. That is the largest lever you have, and urgency triage does not touch it.

## What the ED does that is worth copying

- **Sort by what the case will consume, as well as by how urgent it is.** Modern ED triage scales also estimate how many resources the patient will need, and this is what lets a department stream patients. For you the equivalents are: a simple documentary claim (tenancy ended, no damage claimed, landlord has not responded) that can be decided in minutes; a standard disputed claim; and a complex one with a long hearing. Short, clean cases should go to a fast-track stream, as ED "minors" areas do. That frees adjudicator time and clears numbers quickly, which is what moves the median wait.
- **Make the first contact do real work.** In an ED the first assessment starts treatment. Your triage officer should also check whether the landlord has been served and has responded, whether an itemised deduction was provided, and whether the evidence is in. A case that is not ready should not take a hearing slot.
- **Pull flow forward.** EDs manage crowding by working on what happens downstream (discharge, bed turnover), not by ranking harder at the door. Your downstream is the hearing slot that goes unused when a case settles late.

## The change I would put first

Most of your cases settle, and the settlements bunch just before the hearing because the hearing date is the first moment either side has a reason to compromise. So bring that moment forward.

- Within the first three to four weeks after filing, require the landlord's response and an itemised account of any deductions, then hold a short conciliation call or written exchange run by a registry officer or member.
- Give the case its hearing date only if it does not resolve, or keep the date but fix the settlement pressure point well ahead of it.
- Because late settlements free up prepared slots, schedule hearings using the settlement pattern. Airlines and clinics overbook for the same reason. With a 60% attrition rate you can book more cases per slot than one per slot, provided cases are screened for readiness and there is a fallback for the days when everyone turns up. Without the early checkpoint, overbooking mostly shifts the cancellations to later; with it, the data should let you set the ratio safely.

If even a third of the cases that now settle late settle early, the hearing load falls and the cases that remain are better prepared. I would expect that to do more for the 50-a-month gap than any urgency ranking. These are my estimates from your figures, and the pilot should measure them.

## What I would tell them about the pilot design

**Scope.** Start January, run at least six months, and include the early checkpoint and the readiness check as well as triage. If you only test urgency categories, you will learn how category 1 behaves and little about the backlog.

**Make categories hard to game.**
- Replace the free-text box with a short structured set of questions (for example: is there a notice to vacate, is the filer in receipt of a benefit, has the tenant already moved into new accommodation and paid a replacement deposit, is the tenant at risk of homelessness), each requiring a document or statement that can be checked.
- Cap category 1 at a fixed share of weekly hearing capacity, and decide in advance what happens when it is full.
- Have a second person check a random sample of the triage officer's decisions each week, and record how often two reviewers agree.

**Prevent starvation.**
- Every case gets a maximum wait. Put a long-stop date on category 5 (for example, no case waits more than 12 months) and let cases move up a category as they age.
- Reserve a fixed number of ordinary slots per month for older cases so that the oldest do not drop further behind.

**Handle the landlord-holds-the-deposit problem.** Decide whether landlord-initiated claims and tenant claims are triaged on the same criteria, and write the reasons down. Consider whether a case where the landlord has not responded at all should move up, not down.

**Test it properly.** You cannot randomise who is urgent, but you can run the pilot on alternating filing weeks or on one registry, and compare with the other. Agree the measures before you start:
- time to hearing by category and by tenant or landlord filer
- backlog change per month (the 50 figure)
- share of cases in categories 1 and 2 (an early warning of inflation)
- share of cases settling before the checkpoint, and the timing of the rest
- hearing slots left unused after notice
- the oldest case in each category
- any difference in category by whether the filer was represented

**Staffing.** At 640 filings a month, a 48-hour review means about 30 files a working day. Say who does it. If the officer comes out of adjudicator or registry time, the pilot is taking capacity from the thing it is meant to improve.

## Where this advice is uncertain

- I have assumed the 590 monthly outflow includes settled and withdrawn cases, so the real hearing capacity is lower. If the 60% attrition figure is solid, that is about 250 hearings a month for six adjudicators.
- I do not know what share of waiting tenants are in real hardship. If it is high, a stronger case for urgency streaming exists, and the cap on category 1 matters even more.
- Whether you can legally move the point at which a landlord must respond, or require deposit protection, depends on your statute. That is the cleanest long-term fix for the incentive problem and is worth raising with the president even if it is outside the pilot.

## What I would say to Dr Messer

Keep his instinct. Emergency departments teach that a queue needs active management at the front door, that the first assessment should start the work and that flow, not ranking, ends the crowding. Test those in January. Do not promise the committee that urgency categories on their own will bring the 14-month wait down, because on a monthly deficit of 50 they cannot.
