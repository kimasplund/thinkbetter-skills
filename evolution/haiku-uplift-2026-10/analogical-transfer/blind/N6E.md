Short answer: the demand-response playbook fits part of your problem, but not the part that caused the shortfall. The two specific design choices, an hourly water price and payments per cubic metre below each grower's three-year average, would likely cost money without getting more water to the tail of the Lower Canal. I would not put that version to the board as it stands.

## Where the electricity comparison breaks

- **Peaks are about one moment; your shortfall is about a season.** A grid cuts demand at 6 pm to help at 6 pm. Your problem is total volume across July and August, and the water has to be physically in the river, at the right place, on a schedule.
- **Your delays are days, not hours.** A release takes about three days to reach the Lower intake and up to five to reach the end of the canal. A price posted today cannot change what reaches the tail this week. The useful signal is a shortage call issued at least five days ahead.
- **Upstream savings leak back.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts returns to the river above the Lower intake. Each unit the Upper Canal stops taking adds roughly 0.6 units at the Lower intake, after a delay, not a full unit. A programme that pays for upstream reductions has to be measured on what actually reaches the Lower intake, or the board will pay for water it does not receive.
- **Three-year baselines reward the wrong thing.** A baseline built on recent years can be inflated by a wet run, or stale if growers change crops. Growers would be paid for water they would not have used anyway, and the payments would be hardest to justify in exactly the years the board most needs them.
- **The price signal points at the wrong users.** The shortfall lands on the tail-end barley, hay and grass seed farms. Orchards almost certainly earn more per cubic metre than hay or barley (an assumption you should check), so a payment or price for cutting use pulls reductions from the users with the most to lose per unit, while the water still has to travel the same route to the tail.

## What the analogy does get right

- A clear shortage trigger, voluntary enrolment and paid reductions are sound tools, and those parts can stay.
- Growers who can shift irrigation in time (irrigating in June rather than July, or drawing on stored water) are the real equivalent of shifting a peak. That is the part of the playbook worth keeping.

## What I would put in front of the board

1. **Build a water balance before voting.** For the last two summers, record the Tarrow release, Upper and Lower diversions, the gauge flow above the Lower intake, and deliveries at the tail. This gives the actual shortfall in cubic metres and shows how much upstream saving would arrive. Without it, sizing any programme is guesswork.
2. **Issue shortage calls on a forecast with at least five days' lead,** set by what the tail needs, not by what the intake shows on the day.
3. **Pay for verified reductions only.** Measure them by meter or at the Lower intake gauge, count them at the net figure (roughly 60% of the Upper cut), and avoid three-year averages as the baseline. A fixed per-hectare reduction offer is a simpler alternative that is harder to game.
4. **Decide the rule for trading water explicitly.** Allowing growers to sell temporary water to each other could move water toward the higher-value orchards unless the board sets a rule. A sensible option is a guaranteed minimum delivery to the tail before any trade or payment.
5. **Ask Tarrow operations to test a release schedule.** A guaranteed release timetable or rotation may protect the tail better than any price, and costs less.
6. **Pilot for one summer,** with a fixed budget, a cap on payments, and a report to the board after August.

## What I cannot tell from your message

- The size of the shortfall in cubic metres, which is what sizes the programme.
- What payment rate the board can afford.
- Whether Tarrow can hold carry-over storage from one season to the next.
- Whether the Lower Canal farms would enrol.

I have assumed the 40% return and the three- to five-day travel times hold steady across summers, and that orchard water is worth more per cubic metre than barley or hay. If any of those is wrong, the advice changes.

## Recommendation

Take a narrower version to the board: a voluntary shortage-call programme with payments based on measured net reductions, a release or delivery rule that protects the tail, and a one-season pilot. Do not adopt the hourly price app as the main tool for this shortfall.
