Short answer: parts of Odile's playbook will transfer, but the programme as proposed would probably not fix your problem, and in places it could make it worse. I'd put a modified version in front of the board, built around your river's geometry rather than the grid's.

(I'm assuming no metering detail beyond what you've given me, that growers hold roughly proportional entitlements, and that "18% short" is measured against Lower Canal demand.)

## Where the grid analogy holds

- Both are shared-capacity systems where a few hours or days of restraint by some users protect everyone else.
- Paying people for verified reductions can beat mandates, because it surfaces who can cut cheaply. Orchard and cereal growers differ a lot here. Fallowing a barley or grass seed block is cheap compared with losing a cherry crop.
- A clear event call, advance notice and settlement rules are all reusable.

## Where it breaks

1. **Lag and geography.** In a grid, a demand cut anywhere helps everyone instantly. Here, water not diverted at the Upper Canal reaches the Lower Canal intake about three days later, and the Lower Canal tail up to five days later. A shortage event called today helps nobody until the middle of next week. The grid's hourly price signal has no meaning on a five-day delay. An hourly water price is mostly noise. A price or event call needs to be set days ahead, on forecasts.

2. **Who can actually help the people in shortage.** Your shortfall lands on the Lower Canal tail. Only upstream reductions (Upper Canal, and Lower Canal head-end users) send water to them. Lower Canal users nearer the head who cut back also help the tail, but only if the saved water stays in the canal and is not re-diverted. A programme that pays any enrolled grower anywhere would pay for reductions that do little for the tail. Payment should depend on location and timing, not just volume.

3. **The baseline problem.** Paying for diversion below each grower's own three-year average is the grid's customer baseline logic. It has known weaknesses that are worse for you:
   - Growers can inflate the baseline by diverting more in non-event periods (gaming).
   - Anyone who was going to use less anyway (a fallowed block, a wet spring, a young orchard) gets paid for nothing.
   - Two of the last three summers were short, so the three-year average already includes shortage-reduced diversions, particularly at the tail. The tail's baseline is low and upstream baselines are high, which pays upstream growers for cutting from a generous base and gives the tail nothing for a loss it already absorbed.
   - Orchards need water at fixed phenological stages, and a cut at the wrong time costs a year or more of production in perennial trees. A "voluntary" cut by an orchard is not like a factory shifting a shift.

4. **Return flows.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river before the Lower intake. So each cubic metre the orchards save only adds about 0.4 of that to the river... more precisely, a saving upstream is a saving of consumptive use only to the extent that diversions turn into evapotranspiration. If an orchard reduces diversion by 1 unit, the Lower Canal sees roughly 1 unit more in the river minus the 0.4 that would have returned anyway, so it nets about 0.6 units extra at the Lower intake. Paying for diverted volume therefore overstates the real benefit by roughly 1.7 times. Pay on estimated consumptive-use savings or apply a discount factor.

5. **Voluntary and few participants.** There are two canals and probably a few hundred farms. Grid programmes work through large numbers and statistical baselines. With few participants, a handful of large growers can strategically choose when to enrol, and uptake is uncertain. You cannot plan a supply on a programme whose response you can't forecast.

6. **Equity and politics.** The tail farms bear the loss. A scheme in which the board pays upstream growers from board funds, or in which the tail effectively pays upstream growers to share, may be unpopular and may conflict with your entitlements and water law. Check that paying for non-use fits your enabling rules and any priority system.

## What I'd propose to the board

Keep the instrument (paid, voluntary, pre-committed reduction) but change the design:

1. **Forward option contracts, not an hourly price.** Before the season, offer growers a small annual retainer for the right to call a reduction of a stated volume during a stated window. Calls are issued at least 5 to 7 days ahead, which matches the travel time. Tiered by location: higher value for upstream and head-end commitments whose water actually reaches the tail.

2. **Pay on a fixed, agreed reference, not a rolling average.** Use each grower's contracted entitlement or a pre-season declared demand, set before the season, with metering. This removes most of the gaming and the problem of shortage years polluting the baseline.

3. **Net for return flow.** Settle at roughly 0.6 of diverted-volume reduction for Upper Canal orchards (calibrate with your gauge data), and let the factor be a transparent parameter.

4. **Target the right crops and times.** Expect most of the cheap volume to come from hay, grass seed and barley, and from late-season irrigation cuts, not from orchards at fruit-set or sizing. Offer orchards a deficit-irrigation option at stages where it is agronomically tolerable, with advice.

5. **Trigger from forecasts.** Use reservoir storage, snowpack and forecast inflow in spring to decide on a shortage level for July and August, so contracts are called early rather than reactively.

6. **Pair it with sharing rules for the tail.** Whatever the market does, agree a pro rata shortage-sharing rule so the tail does not carry nearly all the shortfall. Consider also operational fixes: tighter release scheduling, tail-end storage or regulation ponds, canal loss reduction, and recording orchard return flow timing.

7. **Pilot, don't scale.** For next summer, run it as a pilot with a capped budget and a fixed number of contracts, measure the response, and learn before the board commits to a full programme.

## What to ask the board for in five weeks

- Approval in principle for a capped pilot of forward reduction contracts, with a budget ceiling.
- A mandate to set the pro rata shortage-sharing rule, or to consult on it.
- Funding for metering at the main diversion points and the gauge analysis to estimate the return-flow factor.
- A legal check that paying for non-diversion is allowed under your entitlements.

## What I'd want to know first

- The actual size of the shortfall in cubic metres, by week, against what a realistic 18% reduction would cost.
- How much of the cheap volume is available, meaning what hay and grass seed growers would accept per cubic metre.
- Whether entitlements are metered at farm level.
- What the board can afford, and whether growers or the board pay.

Odile's experience is useful for the settlement, verification and communication side. I would tell her the central difference plainly: the grid is a system with instant, uniform response, and your river is a delayed, one-way system where location and timing matter more than price.
