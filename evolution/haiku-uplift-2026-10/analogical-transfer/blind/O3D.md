The electricity comparison is useful, but it breaks in the places that matter most for Lennick Valley, so I would not put the programme to the board as proposed. Here is what carries over, what does not, and what I would put in front of the board instead.

**Where the comparison holds**

A shortage is a mismatch between demand and a capacity limit, and paying users to move their demand away from the constrained period is a proven tool. Enrolled growers who cut diversion during a called event, and are paid for it, are doing the same thing a grid customer does when they shift load. That part is sound.

**Where it breaks**

1. **The lag runs the wrong way.** A grid peak is met by storage or generation that responds in minutes. Here a release takes about three days to reach the Lower intake and up to five to reach the end of the canal. An hourly water price, or a shortage event called on the day, arrives after the water it is meant to save has already been committed. The board would be pricing water that is already in the river. Any useful signal has to be issued days ahead, from a forecast of Tarrow storage and demand.

2. **Upper-canal savings do not stay in the river.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts returns to the river above the Lower intake. If Upper growers divert less, the Lower Canal gets back less of what it relies on, and the reduction is partly offset before it reaches the tail. A programme that pays for reduced diversion upstream, without accounting for return flow, can pay for water that never reaches the people who are short. Credits should be measured as volume that actually reaches or stays in the river at the Lower gauge, not as volume not diverted at the head.

3. **The baseline invites gaming and the wrong incentives.** A three-year average baseline pays growers for ordinary variation. A grower who had a wet or unusually heavy year is paid for normal behaviour, and a grower who expanded recently has an inflated baseline. Orchards are also perennial crops. Cutting irrigation during July and August can damage trees for years, which makes the payment a poor price for a real cost, so few orchards would enrol at a level that matters.

4. **The shortfall is a routing and timing problem, not only a volume problem.** The farms at the tail of the Lower Canal took nearly all of the 18% shortfall. That points to losses and delivery priority along the canal, and to release timing, as much as to total water. A price on water does not fix a tail that never gets its share.

**What I would put in front of the board**

- **A seasonal shortage forecast and an early-warning trigger** (declared at least 7 to 10 days ahead, based on Tarrow storage and projected demand), so that every decision has enough lead time to act on.
- **Re-timed releases and canal delivery rules**, sized to the tail. Measure the losses along the Lower Canal before spending on anything else, since this is probably the cheapest source of the shortfall.
- **A voluntary conservation credit for the Upper Canal, if you want one**, measured at the gauge, paid on the water that reaches the river, and based on a baseline set by agreement rather than a rolling average. Treat it as a supplement to the first two items, not a substitute.
- **A barley and hay tail-end arrangement**: short-term water trading or rotation between the orchards and the Lower Canal, where the low-value, flexible crops are the ones that accept reduced supply in exchange for a payment. Orchards are the least flexible demand on the system and should not be the main lever.

**Assumptions I have made**

- I have no figures for Tarrow's storage, the canal losses, or the cost of a credit per cubic metre, so the programme's size and cost are unknown. The board should ask for these before voting.
- The 40% return flow is treated as a steady ratio. If it varies much by month, the credit rules need to account for that.

**Recommendation**

Do not adopt the programme as a peak-pricing scheme in five weeks. Vote instead for a forecast-based shortage trigger, a loss audit and release-timing review on the Lower Canal, and a pilot conservation credit measured at the Lower gauge. That gives next summer a working early warning and a fix for the tail, and it keeps the payment scheme for a second stage once the return-flow and baseline questions have measured answers.
