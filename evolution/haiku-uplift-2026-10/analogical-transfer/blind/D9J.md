**Short answer:** Her programme would not work as proposed. The electricity analogy holds for some parts of the idea, but it breaks on the features of your system that matter most: travel time, return flows, and the fact that a shortage is a depleting seasonal stock rather than a short peak. You can still take the good parts of her playbook to the board, but I would not ask for a full programme vote next summer.

## Where the analogy breaks

**1. A water shortage is a stock, not a peak.** A grid peak is a short spike that can be shaved by shifting load within hours. Your shortfall is a season-long drawdown: July and August need a fixed volume from Tarrow, and that volume is the constraint. Orchards cannot shift their irrigation to another week the way a factory can shift a shift, and deficit stress in perennial trees has effects that last beyond the season.

**2. The timing is wrong for an hourly price.** A release from Tarrow takes about three days to reach the Lower intake and up to five days to reach the tail of the Lower Canal. An hourly price that a grower sees today can only change water that arrives in three to five days. Any signal has to be a day-ahead or multi-day forecast with a clear shortage call, not a live price.

**3. Upper Canal savings do not arrive in full.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts returns to the river above the Lower intake. If the Upper Canal diverts 1 m³ less, the gauge sees roughly 0.4 m³ less return flow, so the net gain at the Lower intake is about 0.6 m³ (assuming return flow stays proportional to diversion). Paying Upper growers per cubic metre cut therefore buys only about 60% of its face value for the Lower Canal. That needs to be in the payment rate or in the accounting, and it needs checking against the actual gauge data.

**4. The baseline is open to gaming.** Paying per cubic metre below a three-year average rewards growers who had high use in the past and rewards nothing for growers who already conserved. A grower can inflate the baseline in the lead-up to the scheme. Averages also do not adjust for a wet or dry year, so the payment will change with weather rather than with behaviour.

**5. The people who lose most are the ones the scheme is meant to protect.** The tail of the Lower Canal took nearly all the shortfall. A scheme that pays Upper orchards to cut will not necessarily change the water that reaches the tail. The scheme should be designed around where the shortage falls, not where the payment is easiest to administer.

## What I would put in front of the board

Ask for a staged decision, not a full programme:

1. **Measure first.** Fund metering or telemetry at the Upper and Lower intakes and at the gauge above the Lower intake, so the return-flow ratio and travel times are measured rather than assumed.
2. **Forecast the release.** Build a simple day-ahead model that links Tarrow releases to the Lower intake and tail-end flows, with the three- and five-day lags built in. Use it to issue shortage alerts. The hourly app can wait until the forecast is reliable.
3. **Pilot the paid-for-reduction scheme on a small scale.** Enrol a limited group, use metered history with weather adjustment for the baseline, pay on the net effect (credited at about 60% for Upper cuts unless the measurements say otherwise), and set a cap on total payments. Evaluate after one season.
4. **Test a water trading or lease option.** Barley, hay and grass seed growers often use less water per unit of income and may be able to fallow or lease allocation to orchards during shortage. A voluntary, temporary transfer may move water to where it is most valuable more cheaply than a fixed payment. This needs legal confirmation that transfers are allowed under your entitlements.
5. **Protect the tail.** Consider scheduling or rotation rules that guarantee a minimum delivery to the tail of the Lower Canal during shortage, rather than leaving the tail to absorb the whole shortfall.

## Assumptions and open questions

- I assumed the 40% return flow is roughly proportional to diversion. Please check this against several seasons of gauge data.
- I do not know your entitlement rules. If an unused allocation can be forfeited, a voluntary cut has legal consequences that must be cleared first.
- I do not know whether the board has the authority to pay growers for conserved water, or what the funding source would be.
- The 18% shortfall figure is for July and August in two of the last three summers. I have not tried to size the savings needed to close it, because that needs the Tarrow storage volume and the crop water demand figures.

## Bottom line for the vote

Her instinct, that the board should use prices or payments to move demand in a shortage, is reasonable. The mechanism she proposes is built for a different kind of problem. I would present the board with the measurement and forecasting work, a limited pilot with corrected payment rates, and an explicit check on the trading option. That gives you something in place for next summer without committing the board to a scheme whose main risks are already visible in your own numbers.
