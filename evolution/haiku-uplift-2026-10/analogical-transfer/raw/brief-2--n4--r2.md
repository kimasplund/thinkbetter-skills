The emergency-department idea is worth piloting, but only the part that carries over. ED triage works because it sorts patients by how fast they deteriorate and treats the sickest first, with clinicians making the assessment. Your queue differs in three ways that change the design.

**1. Triage reorders the queue. It does not add hearing capacity.**
Six adjudicators and roughly 590 disposals a month (640 filings in, backlog up about 50) set the ceiling. Putting category 1 first means category 5 waits longer, possibly indefinitely. Ask the committee to decide now what "as capacity allows" means in practice, and to report median and 90th-percentile waits for each category, not one overall figure. Expect that category 5 may become a permanent wait.

**2. The urgency is not clinical, and the money sits with the landlord.**
In an ED a clinician judges urgency. Here the category would rest on free text that about 70% of tenants complete, and landlords do not write reasons. That creates two problems. Tenants who write well or have help get placed higher, and the 30% who leave the box blank fall to lower categories by default. Since 85% of claims are tenants seeking their deposit, a delay penalty falls mostly on the people the tribunal serves. The landlord holds the deposit until a ruling or settlement, so delay favours the landlord. Any triage that slows some cases should be checked for that effect.

Prefer structured fields that can be checked against documents: the date the tenancy ended, whether the tenant has moved out or faces homelessness, the amount of deposit held, whether the landlord has disputed the claim, and any hardship or rent arrears. Keep the reasons box as supplementary information, and have the triage officer verify it. It should not set the category alone.

**3. The largest waste is in slot release, not queue order.**
About 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn, mostly in the four weeks before the hearing and after notices have gone out. Slots booked months ahead are released late and go unused. Triage does nothing about this. The levers that do:
- Contact both parties at filing and offer mediation or settlement talks, so settlements happen earlier.
- Move the settlement window earlier in the notice timetable, and require parties to confirm attendance a set period before the hearing. Unconfirmed slots go to a waiting list.
- Overbook modestly, based on the observed settlement rate for each case type, with a cap so adjudicators are never given more hearings than they can take. Track how often both parties attend.

Check the arithmetic first. If 60% settle, only about 250 cases a month need a hearing, so the wait may be driven more by slot release than by filing volume. Confirm this against the case data before committing to a design.

**Pilot design**
- **Retrospective test first.** Apply the proposed categories to the 9,400 open cases and recent filings, using only fields available at filing. Check how the categories would have ordered the queue and how waits would have changed. Test whether the free-text box predicts settlement or hearing outcomes. If it does not, do not use it for ordering.
- **A comparison that can be read.** Split intake by filing week, or by registry team, with the rest of the caseload on current rules. Two or three months is short for a 14-month queue, so measure monthly and report trends rather than a single result.
- **Measures.** Median and 90th-percentile time to hearing by category and overall; share of hearing slots unused; settlement timing in days before the hearing; hearings completed per adjudicator session; time to deposit return for tenants; the number and wait of category 5 cases; complaints and internal reviews; and how often the category changes on review.
- **Guardrails agreed in advance.** Set a limit for category 5 waits, and a threshold for worsening tenant-side outcomes or complaints, at which the pilot is paused or the rules changed.
- **Decide before January.** Settle the category 5 policy and who can request re-categorisation, so tenants have a clear route to challenge a category.

**Recommendation to the committee**
Pilot triage on structured fields rather than the free text. Pair it with earlier settlement engagement and slot release, which address the largest waste. Do not present triage as a fix for the backlog: it reorders the queue but does not create capacity. Waits will fall only if disposals rise or wasted slots are recovered.

**Assumptions.** I do not have figures on hearings per adjudicator session, the mix of case types, or whether the 14-month median includes adjournments. Each of these changes the sizing, so they should be checked before the pilot is designed in detail.
