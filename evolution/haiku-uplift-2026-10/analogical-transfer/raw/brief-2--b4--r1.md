Short answer: the emergency-department idea is worth piloting, but not in the form Dr Messer has described. Triage can change who waits longest. It cannot shorten the queue by itself, because your capacity is fixed. The pilot should be designed to test that distinction.

**Where the analogy fits**

- An ED sorts arrivals by how fast they could come to harm, and reassesses them while they wait. Your version would do the same: sort filings by how fast delay causes serious harm to the claimant, then re-sort when circumstances change.
- A 48-hour review of every new filing is a sensible, achievable standard. At about 640 filings a month, that is roughly 30 a working day, which one or two officers can handle.

**Where it does not fit**

- **Capacity is the binding constraint.** Your backlog of 9,400 cases grows by about 50 a month on 640 filings, so you are disposing of roughly 590 a month. At that rate the queue is about 16 months of work, which matches the 14-month median. Triage reorders that queue. It does not add hearing slots.
- **The categories compete for the same slots.** If categories 1 and 2 are promised three to six weeks, those cases take most of the hearing capacity. Category 4 and 5 cases then wait longer, and the category 5 "as capacity allows" promise may never be met. Before setting any target time, model how many slots each category needs per month and what that does to the rest of the queue.
- **Harm is different in this jurisdiction.** In an ED, delay mainly harms the patient. Here the landlord holds the deposit, and about 85% of claimants are tenants who want it back. Delay costs them money, and for many it also means being stuck in a tenancy or without a deposit for a new home. Define categories by the harm that waiting causes (tenancy ended, eviction notice served, cannot fund a new deposit or rent), not by the seriousness of the dispute.

**The self-reported urgency box is a risk**

- Category would be set from free text that about 70% of tenant filers complete. The 30% who leave it blank would default to a lower category. That is an equity problem, and it means the tool measures writing skill and awareness of the box, not need.
- The landlord's side may not complete the box at all, so the triage would be applied mostly to tenants. That may be fine given the claim mix, but it should be a deliberate choice that the committee understands.
- Replace the free-text box with a short structured set of questions with clear definitions, and have the triage officer check the answers against the rest of the file. Also tell filers how to ask for an urgent review.

**The settlement problem is a separate fix**

- About 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn, mostly in the four weeks before the hearing, after the notice has gone out. Triage does not address this.
- Options to test: offer mediation or a settlement conference at filing or shortly after; ask parties to confirm attendance and release slots earlier; and, if the data supports it, overbook slightly. Overbooking carries a real risk of hearings with no room for the contested cases, so pilot it only with a clear rule for overflow.

**Pilot design**

1. **Define the goal first.** Is it to reduce harm to urgent claimants, to reduce the overall median wait, or both? These can conflict, and the committee should choose which to prioritise.
2. **Use a comparison group.** Either phase the pilot by filing week or randomise filings within a month. Without a comparison, you cannot separate triage effects from the settlement and backlog trends already in play.
3. **Measure the right things.** Track time to hearing and time to deposit return by category, the settlement and withdrawal rate by category and by month of hearing, the share of hearing slots that go unused, and how many cases are re-triaged.
4. **Audit the categories.** Have a second officer review a random sample each month, and record how often categories change at review.
5. **Give a right of challenge.** Let either party ask for a re-categorisation with reasons. Track the outcome.
6. **Fix the rules before January.** Check with the tribunal's legal adviser that the triage categories and priority rules fit the governing procedure, and that prioritisation cannot be read as bearing on the merits.
7. **Set a review point.** Decide in advance what result would lead to continuing, changing, or stopping the pilot, and when that review happens.

**Recommendation**

Support a pilot of triage, with a structured intake form, a 48-hour review, a small number of categories defined by harm, re-triage on new information, a comparison group, and the settlement changes run in parallel. Tell the committee plainly that triage alone is unlikely to bring the 14-month median down much. Its main benefit is getting the most time-critical cases heard sooner, and the pilot should be judged on that.

**Assumptions I have made**

- The 640 filings a month and the 50-a-month backlog growth are steady, and the six adjudicators are fully available.
- The 60% settlement rate applies across all categories, though it may differ by category.
- No new legal rules are needed for triage. This should be confirmed.
