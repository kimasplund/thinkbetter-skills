The library model is a good fit for how people will use the cultures, but several of Ines's rules treat a living culture like a book. Before the cards go to print, I would change these five things.

**1. Lend copies, never originals.**
A book survives being lent out and returned as it was. A culture does not. Every loan changes it, through the borrower's kitchen, their feeding habits and their flour, and the volunteers already report that home portions of the rye drift after a few months. So the Commons should keep each line in a master reserve that is never lent, and lend only from a propagated working copy. Two jars per culture is a start, but the reserve should be separate from the lending stock, and ideally backed up somewhere cold (dried flakes or frozen portions for sourdough and kefir grains) so one bad week cannot end a line.

**2. Do not reshelve returned jars.**
"Returned jars go back on the shelf" is the rule most likely to cause a repeat of the kefir loss. A returned jar may carry foreign yeast, mould or a changed population, and it has been in a home fridge. Put returns into quarantine, inspect them, and re-propagate from the master reserve before anything goes back out. Returned jars can be used as donations or discarded, but they should not be mixed with the shelf stock. The rule should also say plainly that nobody tops up shelf jars with anything from home, and the membership terms should state that.

**3. Rethink the €3/week overdue fee and the replacement charge.**
In a library, the overdue fee is a nudge to return a book. Here, the risk is that a member keeps a culture alive by feeding it at home longer than they are allowed to, and that is good, not bad. A weekly fee also gives people a reason to return a jar early and in poor shape, or not to return it at all. A replacement charge for a jar that "comes back dead or spoiled" will discourage borrowing and can push people to hide problems. I would replace both with a simpler rule: the loan is four weeks with renewals, a member who cannot keep a culture alive can return it early with no penalty, and the Commons charges only for deliberate damage or for a jar kept past a long final deadline (for example, eight weeks). This keeps cultures moving without punishing the people who care for them.

**4. Plan for the labour, and for the rye.**
Teodor is already at his limit, and lending adds work: quarantine checks, re-propagation, and answering questions about feeding. Before March, the board should decide who owns propagation and how many hours that costs. For the rye, 80 holds on a single culture is more demand than the working copies can cover, so the holds queue needs a real capacity plan (how many copies can be made per month, and whether the queue is per family or per line). Consider a short class or starter kit so that members can feed a culture properly, which also reduces dead returns.

**5. Tell members what they are borrowing.**
Many families asked for their original line to be kept going, and the honest answer is that a lent culture is a living copy that will drift, not the original. That should be stated on the card and in the rules, along with the lineage: which master it came from and when it was propagated. This fits the donors' wishes better than a promise that the rye at home is the 1890s rye.

**Assumptions:** I have assumed the cultures are fed regularly at the Commons and that the lending rules apply to members who take jars home. I have not seen the full text of Ines's rules, so if any of these points already appear there, the place to fix it is the returns and quarantine wording.

In short: keep the library's borrowing and holds system, but treat each jar as a copy from a protected master, quarantine everything that comes back, and remove the penalties that work against keeping the cultures alive.
