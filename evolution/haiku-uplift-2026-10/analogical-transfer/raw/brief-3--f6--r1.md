I'd change the core of the rules, not the details. Ines's draft treats a jar as a book: a fixed object that goes out, comes back unchanged, and goes back on the shelf. Your cultures are living populations that grow, drift and can be contaminated. Most of the problems you describe come from that mismatch, and the draft would make them worse once 230 people are handling jars.

## Where the library model breaks

1. **Returned jars can't go back on the shelf.** A borrowed culture spends four weeks in an unknown kitchen, with other flours, other ferments and other hands. Last spring's lost kefir line is the same failure on a small scale: one volunteer's home grains went into the shelf jars. Put returns from 230 members into that loop and you will lose lines again.

2. **A holds queue solves a problem you don't have.** A library queues because a book can't be in two places at once. A starter can. Eighty people wanting the 1890s rye need 80 small portions, not an 80-person queue at four weeks each, which would take years. The scarce thing is Teodor's time and the purity of the original line, not the jar.

3. **"Return it" is the wrong ending for a borrower.** After four weeks of feeding, a starter is no longer the culture you lent. Your volunteers already see this: the home portions of the rye smell and rise differently after a few months. That is normal. Flour, water, temperature and kitchen microbes reshape a culture. It's also why a returned jar can't stand in for the donor's line.

4. **The fees punish the wrong things.** Lateness doesn't hurt you if nothing comes back to the shelf. A replacement charge for a "dead or spoiled" jar will make members hide failures, and a hidden contamination is what you most need to hear about. Dead starters are common with beginners.

## What I'd use instead

The closer model is a seed library or a culture collection (a yeast bank, for example). Both keep a protected master stock and give out working copies.

- **Keep a sealed master line for each culture.** Two jars per culture is fine, but treat them as the archive. Only Teodor or one or two trained volunteers touch them. Nothing from outside ever goes in, including volunteers' home grains. Keep a dried or frozen backup of every line. Starters dry well, and koji and kefir have their own preservation routes. A backup protects the donor families' wish that their line be kept going, which the fridge alone can't.

- **Lend a portion, not a jar.** Each member gets a small starter portion, or dried flakes, from the master. They keep it. It's a gift with a promise attached: feed it, share it on, and tell us what happens to it. For the rye, you can send out all 80 portions over a few weeks from a single working jar that is refreshed in between. Check with a microbiologist or an experienced baker how many portions the line can supply per cycle.

- **Add a working-stock tier between master and members.** A "shelf" jar is a copy drawn from the master and is replaceable. If it goes bad, you re-draw it from the master or the backup. Teodor's hours then go mostly to the masters and the working jars.

- **Make returns optional and one-directional.** If a member wants to give something back, accept it into quarantine, never the shelf. Treat it as information (does it smell and behave as expected?), not as stock. Only after it passes a check does it count as anything else. Most returns can simply be a fresh dried sample or a note.

- **Drop late fees and the replacement charge.** Replace them with a small refundable deposit or a modest membership fee for the portion, and no penalty for a failed culture. Ask for a quick report instead: "tell us if it died, smelled off, or got a mold". Replace failed portions free, once or twice. The reporting loop is your early warning for contamination.

- **Keep Ines's good parts.** Membership cards, a limit per member (say one or two cultures at a time), a clear sign-up record, and a hold list for cultures where the master is truly small (koji strains and rare kefir lines) all still apply. Her years of running a loan system will be useful for the records, the catalogue and the member communications.

## Teodor's workload

This model moves effort away from the person already at his limit. Members feed their own portions, so the six weekly hours go to the masters only. You may even be able to reduce the load by sharing the master care across three or four volunteers on a rota, with a written protocol. Also consider keeping rarely requested cultures in a slower, colder regime or dried, so each doesn't need a regular feeding.

## Before the cards go to the printer

You don't need the whole system by March. Do these in three weeks:

1. Reword the card and rules: "borrow one jar for four weeks" becomes "take a portion of a culture". The cards can carry a name and number and little else.
2. Decide the portion rule for the rye: size, dried or wet, and how many per week.
3. Write a one-page sterility rule for the back room: no outside material in master or shelf jars, ever.
4. Start drying or freezing backups of the most valued and most at-risk lines now, since this is the part that can't be rushed later.
5. Pilot with ten members before opening to everyone, and see how many portions Teodor can really make per week.

## Where I'm less sure

- Whether a portion should be wet or dried varies by culture. Kefir grains, vinegar mothers and koji don't behave like rye starter, so check each type.
- There may be food-safety or hygiene rules for handing out cultures where you are. A short check with your local authority is worth the time.
- I'm assuming members are happy to keep and feed what they get. If many are just curious, expect a high drop-out rate and plan more portions than members.
- The families who donated may care about what "keeping the line going" means. Ask them. A sealed master plus a backup plus a share of the living culture in many homes is arguably more faithful than a single jar in your fridge, but they should hear it from you.

The short version is to keep the library's good habits (records, limits, fairness) but give each culture a protected master, hand out copies instead of lending originals, and stop sending jars back into the stock.
