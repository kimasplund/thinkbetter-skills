The emergency-department model can help, but only in part, and the pilot should be built to test the parts that might fail.

**Where the ED analogy fits**

An ED does not treat patients in arrival order. It sorts them by how fast they need care, and it re-sorts as conditions change. Your backlog has the same shape: a single first-in, first-out queue where a deposit dispute that is urgent (a tenant who needs the money for a new home, or a landlord-side claim with a time limit) waits behind cases that can wait. A triage step at intake, with a target time to hearing per category, fits that problem.

**Where the analogy strains**

1. **Triage only changes who waits, not how many wait.** You have 9,400 open cases, 640 new filings a month, and six adjudicators. Reordering does not add hearing capacity. Unless the category targets are set so that the total demand in each category is within what you can hear, some category will still queue indefinitely. Before the pilot, estimate hearing throughput per adjudicator per month and check whether the category targets sum to something you can meet. If they do not, the category 1 promise (three weeks) will be broken, and that is worse than having no promise.

2. **The inputs are not clinical.** A triage nurse examines the patient. Your triage officer would read a free-text box that about 70% of tenant filers complete. Three problems follow:
   - Urgency is self-reported and will be inflated once filers learn it changes their place in the queue. Expect strategic use of the box.
   - Landlord claims (about 15% of cases) may be filed with different content, so the 70% completion figure says little about their urgency.
   - The 30% who leave the box blank may be the least able to write one, so triage may systematically place them later. That is an equity issue as well as an accuracy issue.
   Consider a short structured urgency question with defined criteria (for example, eviction notice served, housing needed by a date, sums held in dispute), and have the officer record the basis for each categorisation so it can be audited.

3. **Settlements are a separate lever, and may be bigger than triage.** About 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn before hearing, and most of that happens in the four weeks before the hearing date, after the notice has gone out. That is the cause of the wasted slots. Triage does not address it. Options to test in the pilot:
   - Send a settlement or mediation offer at filing, or at the point the landlord's response is due, rather than only near the hearing.
   - Issue hearing notices later, or issue a firm date only once the response is in, so slots are booked closer to the point of use.
   - Overbook slots with a known settlement rate, carefully and with a fallback, so a settlement does not leave an adjudicator idle.
   Measure the share of settlements by time-from-filing before and after any change. This is cheap to analyse from existing records and should be done now.

4. **Deposit holding changes the incentives.** The landlord keeps the money until a ruling or settlement. That gives landlords a reason to delay and gives tenants a reason to want speed, and it means the category 1 promise will be most valuable to tenants who need the money. Watch for landlords filing or responding late to gain time, and check whether the triage category changes how landlords behave.

**How I would design the pilot**

- **Compare, do not just launch.** Run triage on a randomly chosen share of new filings (for example, alternating weeks or a random 50% of intake), with the rest on the current first-in, first-out process. Without a comparison group you cannot separate the effect of triage from the effect of the settlement timing changes or from seasonal variation.
- **Pick outcomes in advance.** Primary: median and 90th-percentile time from filing to hearing or settlement, by category. Secondary: share of settlements before hearing, slots wasted (hearing slots unused on the day), share of category 1 cases heard within three weeks, and any change in the rate of late responses from landlords. Equity: time to hearing for filers who did and did not complete the urgency box, and by filer type.
- **Get a baseline first.** You need at least six months of current data on time to hearing, settlement timing and wasted slots. You likely have this already.
- **Audit the categorisation.** Have a sample of triage decisions reviewed by an adjudicator each month, and record disagreements.
- **Set stop conditions.** For example: category 1 breach rate above a set limit, or the 90th-percentile wait for any category getting worse than the baseline, triggers a review.
- **Size the pilot honestly.** With 640 filings a month, a 50% split gives about 320 per arm per month. That is enough to see large effects within two or three months, but not small ones. Decide what size of change would justify a rollout and check that the pilot can detect it.
- **Plan for the adjudicators.** Their hearing lists will change shape, so involve them in setting the category targets and in the review.

**What I would tell the committee**

- The ED approach suits the queue-ordering problem, but it will not clear the backlog by itself. The backlog is a capacity problem.
- The biggest waste is the late settlements, which the triage model does not touch. Fix or test that in parallel, since it may be cheaper and quicker.
- The pilot should be a controlled comparison, with a baseline, pre-set outcomes and a capacity check, and not a switch-over from January.
- Before January, the team should be able to answer: how many hearings per month can the six adjudicators deliver, what the category targets imply for monthly demand in each category, and what the current late-settlement rate is by time from filing.

**Assumptions I made**

- Hearing capacity is roughly stable over the pilot period.
- The 9,400 open cases and 640 monthly filings are accurate, and the backlog figure (about 50 a month) is net of withdrawals.
- Hearings are the main output; a case settled before hearing does not need an adjudicator.
- Category targets would be set by the tribunal, not by statute. If there are statutory time limits, the design needs changing.
