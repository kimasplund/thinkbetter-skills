Ines's rules treat a culture like a library book: scarce, one copy, and it has to come back. That's the part I'd change, because a culture can be copied and a returned jar is a contamination risk.

**What the library model gets wrong**

1. **Copies are cheap, so most of the queue disappears.** A starter can be divided. 80 people wanting the 1890s rye doesn't need a holds queue. It needs a few batches of portions. A queue and a four-week loan make sense when there is one copy, and here there isn't.
2. **Returned jars should never go back on the shelf.** Your own history shows why. Losing a kefir line came from outside grains being topped into a shelf jar. A rule where 230 members' jars come back into the fridges is that same risk repeated many times. Never merge returns into the collection.
3. **Late fees and dead-jar charges punish the wrong thing.** Cultures sometimes die in a beginner's kitchen. Charging for that discourages people from admitting it, and you'd rather hear about it. Late fees also assume the jar must come back, which is the thing to drop.

**What I'd change**

- **Lend a portion, not a jar.** Members receive a labelled portion from a dedicated "distribution" jar. They keep it and feed it at home. If theirs dies, they get a fresh portion free, with a short note on what went wrong.
- **Make returns optional, and only into quarantine.** Offer a pay-it-forward ask instead of fees: share a portion with a new member, or donate to the distribution stock. If anyone does return a jar, it is discarded or kept in a separate quarantine fridge. It never touches the master jars.
- **Split each culture into a protected master and a distribution line.** The two back-room jars stay as sealed masters, with a strict rule that only the designated feeder opens them. Add a third tier of distribution jars that portions come from. Also make dried or frozen backups of the masters where the culture allows. Drying works well for sourdough and kefir grains, and less well for some others, so check culture by culture.
- **Stop volunteers topping up with home cultures.** Write it as a firm rule with a reason, since the kefir loss happened this way.
- **Treat drift as expected.** Your volunteers' home rye smells and rises differently after a few months. That's normal, because temperature, flour and microbes change a culture. So don't promise a member the identical 1890s rye. Promise the line, and label every portion with the culture name, the date it was split from the master, and a generation number. Re-issue from the master or the backup periodically. That also honors the families who asked for their original line to be kept going.
- **Ration by portion, not by time.** Cap people at one new culture per month or similar, and fill the rye demand in batches. Prepare 80 portions across a few weeks instead of making Teodor feed 80 queue slots.
- **Cut Teodor's load.** Moving feeding to borrowers removes most of the ongoing work. Add a second trained volunteer for the masters, so one person at his limit isn't the single point of failure.
- **Add a basic safety and handling sheet.** Cover hygiene, what spoilage looks like, a disclaimer about eating ferments, and an allergy note. Also ask how a member will tell you about problems.

**Before the cards go to the printer**

- Keep loan terms, fees and renewal limits off the cards. Print only the member name, number and contact details, so you can change the rules without reprinting.
- If you want something on the card, make it a line like "Cultures are shared, not returned."

**Assumptions**

I'm assuming you have fridge and bench capacity for distribution jars, and that your families' donation agreements allow portions to be shared. Check that second point, because some families may have asked for the line to be kept going rather than passed on.

Overall, keep Ines's strengths: clear membership, fair access and a queue for anything genuinely scarce (for example, a rare koji strain with very little stock). Replace the lend-and-return mechanics with portioning and a protected master.
