**Short version:** Ines's rules fit objects that stay the same and can only be in one place at a time. Your cultures are living and they multiply, so the library model is the wrong one. A better fit is a seed library, where members take a portion and keep it going. Your main constraints then become protecting the original lines and Teodor's hours. Here is what I'd change before the cards go to print.

**1. Stop lending jars and give out portions**
- A culture is not scarce the way a book is. A healthy starter can be divided many times, so 80 people wanting the rye doesn't have to mean a queue.
- Give members a small portion, for example a spoonful of starter or a tablespoon of grains, in a clean sealed container. Don't hand over a shelf jar.
- Drop the four-week loan, the renewals, the holds queue and the €3 weekly overdue fee. Nothing needs to come back on a deadline.
- A queue can still make sense for the first weeks, because you can only produce portions as fast as the stock grows. Make it a "portions available" list with a pickup date, not a loan queue.

**2. Never put returned material back on the shelf**
- Your kefir loss came from outside material entering a shelf jar. Ines's rule "returned jars go back on the shelf" makes that the normal process, with 230 members' home environments as the source.
- Make this the one absolute rule: nothing that has left the building ever goes back into a stock jar.
- Keep two tiers:
  - **Master jars** stay in the fridge and are only ever fed from their own line. Ideally a few people can touch them.
  - **Distribution jars** are fed from the master and are what portions are taken from.
- Your current two jars per culture could become master and working copy. Decide whether that is enough protection for the cultures families asked you to preserve. For the irreplaceable ones, add a third copy, dried or frozen where the culture allows it. For the koji strains, check what storage suits each one.

**3. Replace "return" with "pay it forward" and "report back"**
- Ask members to pass a portion on to someone else, or share a short note on how it's doing.
- Anything brought back goes through a quarantine step. Keep it apart from the stock, label it, and only let it join the collection after a volunteer has checked smell, rise and appearance. Often it will be better to say thanks and not take it.
- This spreads the work of keeping cultures alive across the membership and is the main way to ease the load on Teodor.

**4. Drop the replacement charge for dead or spoiled cultures**
- A culture that dies at home is usually down to temperature, feeding or water, and it is a normal beginner experience. Charging for it will put people off and doesn't match the value of a spoonful.
- Offer a free replacement portion, perhaps once or twice per member, and use each case as feedback. Add a short care sheet to every portion.
- If you want a fee, make it a small membership or donation, not a penalty.

**5. Decide what "the original line" means**
- Several volunteers say their home rye starter smells and rises differently after a few months. That is expected. Cultures drift with flour, water, temperature and feeding rhythm.
- Say plainly in the rules that the Commons keeps the reference line, and that what members take home will change in their kitchen. This avoids disputes with donor families.
- Record donor, date, origin and feeding regime for each culture. If the 1890s rye is a treasured one, make it the first to get extra backup copies.
- Treat drifted home portions as a separate "variants" collection, not part of the master.

**6. Keep Teodor's workload from growing**
- Under the portion model, work grows with propagation, not with number of borrowers. Limit it:
  - Cap how many portions you produce per culture per month.
  - Schedule distribution days, say two a month, with portions prepared the day before.
  - Train two or three more volunteers to feed cultures, and rotate. Right now one person is a single point of failure.
  - Launch with a smaller catalog. Perhaps 20 to 30 of the most robust cultures go public first, and fragile ones such as koji and some vinegar mothers stay volunteer-only until you know the load.

**7. Add basic safety and hygiene rules**
- Printed handling guidance on every portion: clean utensils, label with date, feeding frequency, what spoilage looks like and when to discard.
- Check local food-safety rules for giving cultures to the public, including labelling and allergens (gluten in sourdough, dairy in kefir). I can't tell you the legal position for your country, so a short check with your local authority is worth it.
- Ask members not to bring their own cultures to the back room.

**8. What I'd keep from Ines's draft**
- Membership with a card and a simple sign-up.
- One culture per person at a time at the start, to protect supply while the first batches grow.
- A waiting list, in the changed form described above.
- Her experience with administration: records, member communication and fair handling of demand.
