Ines's rules are sound for books, but the Commons is lending living things, and that changes several of them. The most useful comparison is a seed library. Seed libraries face the same problem: a lent seed is grown out by the borrower, and the varieties stay pure only if the library controls what comes back. They lend the seed, ask for seed back, and keep a master stock that never leaves the building. Here is what I would change, in order of urgency.

**1. Stop returned jars going back on the shelf.** This is the change I would make first, and it is the lesson of the kefir loss. A jar that comes back has been fed by someone else, in someone else's kitchen, possibly alongside other cultures. It may carry wild yeast or bacteria, and it may have drifted. Your volunteers' home rye already smells and rises differently after a few months, which shows drift happens even without any contamination. Make the rule: returned jars are quarantined, checked, and either discarded or fully rebuilt from a master jar before reshelving. Returned material never goes back into the shelf stock.

**2. Lend daughter jars, not the master line.** Each culture should have two kinds of jar. Masters stay in the back-room fridges, fed on schedule, and are the only source for new loans and rebuilds. Lendable jars are propagated from a master and are the ones that circulate. When a lent jar fails, you rebuild from a master, not from another borrower's jar. This also addresses the 1890s rye: 80 people want it, so you will need several daughter lines fed from one master, and you should label each line with its provenance and the date it was split.

**3. Rewrite the overdue fee.** A €3 weekly fee assumes the harm is lateness. For a culture, the real harm is that a jar left unfed for a long time dies or spoils, and the replacement charge then lands on the borrower for a loss that is really yours to prevent. Four weeks is also a long unfed period for many starters and kefir grains, even in a fridge. I would make the loan period shorter for the first lending season, or make it depend on the culture. A borrower should also be expected to show they can feed it, so a reasonable condition is a short handover in which they feed the jar once with you watching. The fee can stay for lateness, but it should not be the main lever.

**4. Make the dead-jar charge a reason to report, not to hide.** Right now a borrower who notices a spoiled jar has a reason to say nothing or to top it up. Set the replacement charge so that reporting early costs less than reporting late, and make it clear that a jar which has been mixed with anything else is always treated as dead.

**5. Protect Teodor's time first.** Lending adds work: quarantine checks, rebuilds, handovers and queue management. Teodor is already at his limit. Before opening in March, decide who does each of these tasks, and consider whether a second trained volunteer can take over the masters, so that one person's absence does not put a line at risk. You may find that the holds queue gets shorter if you cap how many daughter jars each popular culture produces, rather than trying to meet all 80 requests at once.

**6. Keep the donor promise in writing.** Many families asked that their original line be kept going. Put that in the rules: masters are never lent out, and any line that is discontinued is announced to the donor family first.

**What I would keep:** the four-week loan, the two renewals, and the holds queue are reasonable once the loan is a daughter jar and the borrower has been shown how to feed it. The library model works for the front desk. It does not work for the back room.

**Assumptions:** I have not seen the fridge setup, your volunteer rota, or the exact text of Ines's rules, so the specific numbers (the loan length, the fee and the number of daughter jars) are starting points you should test with her. Seed libraries are the best reference I know of for this kind of lending, and they would be worth a conversation with an existing group before March.
