Short answer: not as written. The main numbers don't support signing on June 2, and the kiln timing and capacity have problems that could hit your existing members too. Here is the reasoning, with the assumptions I used.

**1. The break-even comparison mixes units.**
$13,600 is what four full cohorts pay for one 8-week course (40 students x $340). $9,400 is a monthly cost. An 8-week course is about 1.85 months, so if the cohorts run back to back, the space earns roughly $7,400 a month at 100% full, not $13,600. That is about $2,000 a month short before any materials, clay, glazes, or gas.

To break even on $9,400 a month with 40 seats, you would need about $434 per student per course, or about 51 students paying $340 continuously, which is more seats than the room has.

**2. The Christmas target is probably out of reach.**
If the first cohorts start August 4 and run back to back, two full courses end around late November, bringing in about $27,000 at full capacity. August through December costs about $47,000 at $9,400 a month. Even with every seat filled and nobody dropping out, the new space would need roughly $10,000 to $20,000 from the $22,000 cushion (or the original studio) before Christmas. Fit-out, permits, kiln installation, and the instructor's start-up time are not in the $9,400 and would make this worse.

**3. The kiln is not ready when the courses start.**
- Apply June 2 and a 10-14 week meter upgrade lands between about August 11 and September 8. The gas kiln cannot run before then, so the first cohorts' pieces must go to the original studio's electric kiln, which you already expect to fail.
- Kiln delivery, gas line hookup, venting, and inspection can add more time. Check these with the installer and the city before you rely on the dates.
- If the electric kiln dies before the gas kiln is working, your existing 30 members lose firing too. That is the most important risk to the current business, and the plan doesn't have a fallback for it.

**4. Kiln capacity looks tighter than the plan implies.**
- Members: about 60 pieces a week. Beginners: 40 students x 2-3 pieces = 80-120 a week. Total: 140-180 pieces a week.
- Each piece usually needs two firings (bisque and glaze). That is 280-360 piece-firings a week. At 70 pieces per load, that is 4-5 loads a week.
- Three firings a week gives 210 piece-firings at full loads. That is not enough if every piece is fired twice, and it leaves no slack for partial loads, failed loads, or glaze problems even if you assume pieces are counted once.
- If each piece is fired only once, you would be at 67-86% of capacity, which is still tight. Check which of these your numbers use.

**5. Demand and cash risk.**
- The 140 names came from a free booth sign-up, not paid enrollments. Plan on a conversion rate you can measure (for example, 20-30% paying), and count how many names are still interested and local.
- Courses have to refill every eight weeks. The waitlist fills one cohort cycle, not the whole year.
- You are personally guaranteeing a five-year lease, about $246,000 in rent plus the kiln loan. The $22,000 cushion covers only about 10 months of a $2,000-a-month shortfall, and less if ramp-up is slow.

**What I would do before June 2:**
1. Ask the utility about the meter application now. Find out whether they need the signed lease, the owner's consent, or only the address, and whether the date can move earlier.
2. Renegotiate the lease: remove or cap the personal guarantee, add an early-exit clause (for example after 12-18 months), and ask for more rent-free time or a fit-out allowance.
3. Get a firm fallback for firing. Ask a local studio or a kiln-share to take overflow, and set a date to replace the electric kiln, even if you don't buy it.
4. Pilot before committing. Run one or two cohorts in the existing studio's space, or with a partner, and measure actual paid enrollment and firing time per piece.
5. Rebuild the model with a monthly view: courses per month, paid seats, price, and all costs, including materials, glaze, gas, and the new kiln's operating cost. Then find the seat count and price that cover $9,400 a month, and decide whether that is realistic.

**Assumptions I made:** all four cohorts run continuously at 10 students each; the $340 is for the full 8-week course; the $9,400 includes rent after the free months; and the 60 member pieces are fired in the same kiln as the beginners' work. If any of these are wrong, the numbers change, but the price gap and the kiln timing would still need to be solved first.

Bottom line: the plan only works if the courses fill at a higher price or more seats, the kiln is running before the first cohorts need it, and you have a way to keep the original studio firing if the electric kiln fails. Right now none of those is in place, so I would not sign on June 2 without changes.
