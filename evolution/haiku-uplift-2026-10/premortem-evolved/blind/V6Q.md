# Premortem: Arbelle expansion from 4 to 14 schools
**Horizon:** Aug 31 (the summer release of the second-year money) · **Success was defined as:** "all 14 schools running in January, and the second-year money released next summer"
**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure, meaning "if this dies, this is what killed it". They are not the probability the plan fails overall, and they sum to ~100. **Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** ~90%. Both halves of your definition have to hold, and the second half fails on your own numbers before any risk is added.
**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- The board vote on Oct 8 is the start line. Nothing has been filed with the district office yet, because the plan names no filing date.
- Arbelle reimburses every dollar of the ~$50k a month, including coordinator pay before tutoring starts, and the first payment arrives on the stated schedule.
- The $150k reserve is also the cushion for the four existing schools, and there is no credit line.
- "Outscore a comparison group" is measured by Arbelle or the district on the mid-April benchmark, with the comparison group chosen by someone other than you.
- Harmon has not yet committed a number of practicum seats in writing.

## The Autopsy
**The strongest case for this plan:** A serious funder chose you, so the model has already survived outside scrutiny. A 12-point gain on a district benchmark after a full year is a large effect for any reading program, and you have the data. The tutor ratio is proven: 70 tutors serve about 160 kids, roughly 2.3 kids per tutor, and the expansion keeps that ratio (400 kids, 175 more tutors). Ten principals want you, which is rare, and you already hold one district MOU and know the people who sign the next one. The grant budget matches the work, since $600k a year is $50k a month, and your reserve covers the first three months of payroll outright. A smart advocate would say the downside is a slower January, not a failed program.

Six ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The January launch ran, the mid-April benchmark showed a gap of about 5 to 7 points against a required 15, and the $600k was never released
**Probability:** ~30% — the test needs 125% of your proven full-year effect from under half the instruction time, and it is the one failure that fires even if everything else goes right (the lower share reflects that modes #2 to #4 usually kill the plan before the test is taken)
**Cause of death:** The new schools got roughly 12 to 13 instructional weeks (Jan 12 to mid-April, less holidays and testing weeks), against roughly 28 weeks for the legacy schools' Oct to April year. Scaled by dose, 12 points becomes about 5 to 6. That is my arithmetic, not a measured dose-response curve, but nothing in the plan argues the effect arrives faster. The plan needed 15.
**How it unfolded:**
- Jan: ten schools opened with first-year coordinators. Tutors from Harmon were not enrolled for practicum credit until Jan 20, so the first week or two ran thin.
- Feb: sessions ramped slowly. Testing windows, a school holiday and tutor no-shows cut delivered sessions below the scheduled two a week.
- Mar: new coordinators were still fixing scheduling and attendance, which the legacy sites had solved years earlier. Kids at the new sites had about 15 sessions by the time the legacy sites would have had 40.
- Apr: the benchmark gave a single-digit gap. Arbelle's condition was explicit, so the decision was administrative.
- May to Aug: the year-two money was refused. Year-one money ran out around Oct, leaving ten coordinator salaries, about $50k a month, with no funding behind them.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That a program's effect belongs to the program rather than to the number of weeks it ran. Your 12 points was earned over a full year.
**Reference class:** your own full-year 12 points. Disanalogy: the new schools may pick the highest-need kids, who have more room to gain, which pushes the number up. Half the dose, new staff and first-year sites push it down harder. Programs generally show smaller effects when scaled quickly (weakly sourced, recalled from the tutoring-scale-up literature and not checked); I did not rely on that, only on your own numbers.
**Existing guard:** none. The plan never connects the January start to the April test.
**First warning sign:** the grant agreement text in October. Hard evidence would be your own legacy schools' mid-January interim results: if they show less than 15 points at about 13 weeks, the test is unreachable.

### 2. The district MOU was not signed for most of the ten schools by Jan 12, so the coordinators were hired and sat idle
**Probability:** ~20% — your own MOU took five months, and the plan needs ten signed in under three
**Cause of death:** The district review process, not school willingness, gated access to school space and student data. From an Oct 8 vote, Jan 12 is about 14 weeks, against 21 for the last MOU. Even at 1.5 times faster this lands at the edge, and the plan runs ten packets at once through the same office, with winter closures in the middle.
**How it unfolded:**
- Oct: the vote passed and the plan assumed warm relationships would speed things up. Nobody asked the contract office for a written turnaround.
- Nov: coordinators started Nov 1 into schools they could not enter. They did hiring, planning and tutor recruiting, but nothing for children.
- Dec: packets queued with each item reviewed separately, and the office slowed over Dec 20 to Jan 5.
- Jan: two or three MOUs were signed. Principals who had said they were excited kept saying so, but could not open a door without the paper.
- Feb to Apr: schools started on a rolling basis. By April some had one month of tutoring, so every benchmark result worsened (#1).
**The assumption that allowed it:** That "we know the people there now" converts to speed. The most-certain statement in your plan is that all ten principals are excited. They are at best Accept: they agree, and then the district decides. The district office itself holds no commitment from you yet. That certainty survives only as a statement about willingness, never about timing.
**Reference class:** your own five-month MOU. Disanalogy: an amendment to a known program should be faster than a first MOU, which pushes the number down. Ten simultaneous packets and a December holiday push it back up.
**Existing guard:** none. No filing date and no district commitment exist on paper.
**First warning sign:** no written turnaround date from the contract office by Oct 24.

### 3. Reserves hit zero on Feb 1, the first Arbelle payment landed Mar 17, and payroll stopped before the program could prove itself (the compound entry)
**Probability:** ~20% — it is arithmetic on your numbers, and it fires harder when #2 delays the program but not the payroll
**Cause of death:** Coordinators were paid from Nov 1. The Nov to Jan quarter closed Jan 31, and 45 days later is Mar 17. Costs through Jan 31 were $150k, the whole reserve. Feb 1 to Mar 16 added about $75k with nothing to pay it. After Mar 17 the same gap reopened: the Feb to Apr quarter closes Apr 30 and pays Jun 14, so roughly $70k was uncovered again before that payment, every dollar of it unreimbursed. This is the compound: the MOU slip (#2) made early expenses look idle and invited questions on the first expense report, so the first payment slid, while payroll kept going.
**How it unfolded:**
- Nov to Jan: $150k of payroll went out on schedule, and reserves reached zero on Jan 31.
- Feb: the board learned there was nothing to cover payroll. The executive director spent February raising a bridge instead of running January's launch.
- Mar: Arbelle's first payment came Mar 17, only after a query on pre-launch coordinator costs.
- Apr to Jun: the second gap opened. The board froze hiring and trimmed legacy site hours to protect cash.
- Jun to Aug: with the legacy program thinner, its results softened, and the second-year case weakened further.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That reserves are available. They are the only cushion for the four existing schools too, and that "usually 45 days" holds for a new grantee's first report.
**Existing guard:** the $150k reserve. It is inadequate: it covers exactly three months and leaves zero cushion for any slip.
**First warning sign:** Oct 31 passes with no credit line and no changed payment terms.

### 4. The 175 new tutors did not arrive cleared, enrolled and trained by Jan 12
**Probability:** ~15% — the plan grows a pool built over years by 2.5 times in two months, from a single university
**Cause of death:** Two omissions. First, nothing in the plan schedules background clearances for 175 people, and clearance of that volume queues. Second, Harmon's spring semester started Jan 20, so students trained during finals, then went on break, and were not enrolled for credit when the schools opened. Tutor capacity set the number of kids who could be served.
**How it unfolded:**
- Nov: recruiting drew from the same education cohort that supplied the first 70, so sign-ups plateaued near 110.
- Dec: finals and the break emptied the training calendar. Clearances queued and nothing was filed before Dec 1.
- Jan: about 100 new tutors were cleared on Jan 12, and none could earn practicum credit until the 20th. Schools ran at one session a week or fewer kids than planned.
- Feb: attrition set in on a pool still learning the work. Kids per tutor rose from 2.3 to 4 or more.
- Mar: sessions fell below the dosage the test needed, which fed #1.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That Harmon would supply 175 more students on the same terms as 70, with no written seat commitment from the department. Harmon's grade is Accept at best.
**Existing guard:** none. No practicum seats or clearance dates are on paper.
**First warning sign:** fewer than 110 confirmed sign-ups by Dec 1.

### 5. The expansion launched, and success starved the legacy program and the director
**Probability:** ~10% — a mundane key-person failure that surfaces only after launch
**Cause of death:** One executive director was the single point of contact for ten new principals, the district, Arbelle's reports, a Nov 1 mass hire, and four existing schools. The best legacy coordinators and veteran tutors were pulled to lead new sites. The proof point for the whole expansion, the legacy 12 points, eroded.
**How it unfolded:**
- Nov to Dec: 10 coordinators had to be hired and trained in 24 days after the vote, so the executive director did it herself.
- Jan: launch attention went to new sites. Legacy site visits dropped.
- Feb: the strongest legacy tutors moved to new sites as leads.
- Mar: legacy attendance sagged and a first coordinator quit.
- Apr: the legacy results, now the credibility behind Arbelle's trust, slipped below 12.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That growth is additive. Four schools do not stay at full strength while ten are launched on the same management.
**Existing guard:** none. The plan names no deputy.
**First warning sign:** legacy attendance below 85% of scheduled sessions for two weeks in Feb.

### 6. Something in the measurement chain moved, and the test was not the one you planned for (a blind spot in this frame)
**Probability:** ~5% — I cannot yet name the mechanism, which is the point
**Cause of death:** The plan's most consequential number is produced by other people, on data you have not seen: who the comparison group is, whether the district benchmark or its timing changes, how Arbelle reads "outscore". I cannot chain this one with confidence and treat it as a judgment call. The adversary section tries to resolve it.
**How it unfolded:**
- Fall: nobody asked who selects the comparison group or what contaminates it.
- Winter: a district tutoring initiative reached some comparison kids.
- Apr: the gap narrowed for a reason unrelated to your work.
**The assumption that allowed it:** That the measure is neutral and fixed.
**Existing guard:** none seen in the plan.
**First warning sign:** the comparison roster not delivered by Feb 15.

**Tie-break (#2 over #3, both at ~20%):** the MOU slip ranks first because it fires earlier, in the Nov 1 to Jan 12 window, and because it is the input that turns the cash gap in #3 from a squeeze that a credit line can cover into a hole; #3 without #2 is fixable with money, #2 is not.
**Considered and cut:** coordinator hiring slipping past Nov 1 (folded into #5), school-space conflicts with other after-school users (folded into #2), tutor attrition after March (folded into #4 and #5).
**Downgraded — guarded for now:**
| Mode | Guard that holds it down | Signal that re-upgrades it |
|---|---|---|
| A principal reverses after the vote | The MOU gate is district-level, so a single principal cannot unilaterally cancel, and all ten have so far said yes | Any principal who has not confirmed room and time slots in writing by Nov 15 |
| A child-safety incident involving an unscreened tutor | District requirements for outside programs, assumed to include screening before any child contact | Any tutor placed on site before their clearance is logged |

**Most likely:** #1, because it ties #3 for the top I×F score and has the highest feasibility. It is arithmetic and does not need anything to go wrong.
**Most dangerous:** #3, because its impact is the highest and it reaches the four existing schools, not only the new ones. By the time the problem is undeniable (Feb 1 with zero reserves), the irreversible step has been taken: ten coordinators hired on Nov 1. Silence is low, since the gap is visible today, but irreversibility is high and the first payment arrives six weeks after the money runs out. It differs from #1, whose loss is the second-year $600k but not the legacy program.
**The hidden assumption:** Modes 1 to 4 all die unless the same belief holds: that January 12 can be met by all four calendars at once, which are Arbelle's payment clock, the district's review clock, Harmon's semester clock and the April benchmark clock. The date was set by the money's enthusiasm, not by the slowest dependency. Four modes sharing that root is stronger evidence than any single share.
**Fatal flaw:** Yes. As structured, the second-year test cannot be met: 15 points in about 13 weeks, from a program that earns 12 points in about 28. Unless the test or the start date changes, the expansion is funded for twelve months and then orphaned. The cash gap and the MOU timing are the binding constraints that decide whether you reach April at all.

## The Rebuild
**The revised plan:** Accept the grant, but vote to accept and gate the irreversible steps separately. File one amendment to the existing MOU covering all ten schools in the week of the vote. Hire coordinators and open schools in waves: wave 1 is every school with a signed MOU by Dec 15, capped by cleared tutor capacity (tutors times 2.3); wave 2 opens in February or September. Close a bridge before the first coordinator starts. Negotiate the second-year test so each cohort is measured after a full Oct to April year, or at a dose-adjusted threshold. Redefine success as "wave 1 running in January, all 14 by September, second-year money released on a cohort test that a January start can pass."

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **The test.** Check: the grant agreement's definition of the 15-point test, the window, and whether a January cohort qualifies. How: written request to the program officer by Oct 17. Walk away from the all-ten January launch if the test is non-negotiable as written. Falsifier for the assumption that 15 points is reachable: your legacy schools' January interim results, if they show less than 15 points at about 13 weeks.
2. **The MOU.** Check: the contract office's written turnaround for ten schools. How: one email with a named contact, by Oct 24. Walk away from Jan 12 for any school with a stated timeline over 90 days. Falsifier for "we know the people": no written date, or fewer than five packets in legal review, by Oct 31.
3. **The cash.** Check: a closed credit line of at least $100k and Arbelle's payment schedule in writing. How: bank commitment letter and a signed side letter. Walk away from starting any coordinator on Nov 1 if neither exists on Oct 31.
4. **The tutors.** Check: Harmon's department chair on seats, enrolment and whether Nov to Dec training counts for credit. How: a signed one-page commitment. Walk away from the number of schools beyond (confirmed tutors divided by 2.3 per school). Falsifier: fewer than 110 confirmed seats by Nov 15.
5. **The reconciliation.** Check: the coordinator pay schedule against the payment schedule, month by month through Jun 14. How: a 13-month cash sheet with the board treasurer's sign-off. Walk away if the lowest balance is below minus $100k with no line in place.

**Decision rule:** IF by Oct 31 all of the following are true: Arbelle has agreed in writing to a test a January cohort can pass, the district has committed in writing to signing dates, and either monthly reimbursement or a closed $100k line exists, THEN hire wave-1 coordinators from Nov 1 and open schools with signed MOUs on Jan 12, up to tutor capacity. ELSE accept the grant but start no coordinator before Dec 15, open only schools with a signed MOU and cleared tutors, and budget as if the second-year money will not come. ELSE IF your legacy interim data show 15 or more points at about 13 weeks, the test stands as written and the other two conditions alone gate the launch. The risky capability test (MOU turnaround) comes before the irreversible step (hiring ten people).

**If the decision rule lands on delay, reduce, or don't — the reverse autopsy:** The horizon passed and we held back, and it turned out to be a mistake. (1) Arbelle read the gating as hesitation and moved the second-year money to another grantee. (2) The district signed all ten within eight weeks, and a semester of instruction was lost. (3) Harmon's 175 tutors appeared in full, and the unspent year-one funds were clawed back.
**What tips the balance:** A signed district letter with dates for all ten schools by Oct 31, plus the cash condition met, reverses the caution and puts all ten schools in wave 1. That is written into the rule above as the first branch.
**Consciously accepted:** The measurement risk in #6. No cheap fix exists, because the comparison design is Arbelle's. Priced at about 5% of $600k, or roughly $30k, against the cost of tracking it, which is negligible.
**How the rebuilt plan dies:** The wave plan trades one risk for another. Arbelle may read a phased roll-out as failure and fund less, and wave-2 principals' enthusiasm may fade by September, with turnover in the new school year. Its weakest joint is the test renegotiation: if Arbelle refuses, the plan drops back to the original exposure on fewer schools.
**Residual risk:** Even rebuilt, a first-year program at new sites can miss a 15-point test. Only the renegotiation removes that, not the execution.

## The Adversary
**Playing:** A competing literacy tutoring provider in or near Fennick. I do not know whether one exists; this is the best-positioned rival. The motivation is that Arbelle's $1.2M shows the district is a funded market. Its resources are a ready model and perhaps district relationships. It cannot bypass the same MOU queue, and it cannot match your Harmon pipeline. Its best alternative is other districts, so the fight costs it little.
**The kill chain:**
1. **Oct 15 to Nov 15, probe.** It approaches your ten principals offering to start sooner than you.
2. **Dec, position.** It pitches the district contract office on a model that needs no after-school space, to an office with ten of your packets on its desk.
3. **Jan to Feb, strike.** A principal offered two providers defers to the district, which queues you behind the other.
4. **Mar to Apr, entrench.** Its tutoring reaches kids in your comparison pool, or it offers pay to Harmon students you rely on.
**Detection opportunities:** (1) a principal mentioning another provider's visit, by Nov 15; (2) the contract office asking for an "alignment" meeting or a pause, by Dec 15; (3) any sign of competing tutoring in the comparison roster, by Feb 15; (4) Harmon students mentioning paid offers, by Feb 1.
**The move you'd never see coming:** The district itself starts a free school-day tutoring program, perhaps from state money, for the same kids. Your comparison group gets better, your gap narrows, and nobody told you.
**What this means you should change:** Ask the district in October what tutoring it already plans for these schools this year, and get the comparison-group roster in writing before you start.
