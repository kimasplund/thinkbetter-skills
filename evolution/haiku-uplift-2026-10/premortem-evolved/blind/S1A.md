# Calloway Markets deal: how it could die

**Horizon:** September 30, 2027 · **Success was defined as:** Calloway is your biggest account and the business is profitable by September.

**Note on the percentages:** shares of failure, meaning "if this dies, this is what killed it." They sum to 100. They are not the odds the deal fails overall, and the odds that it misses the September goal are higher than any single share below.

**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- Calendar: signing December 15, 2026; Halvard order January 5, 2027; store date March 1, 2027; the September goal means September 30, 2027.
- Calloway's six jars per store per flavor per week holds across all 48 stores and all three flavors from March. The brief calls it what the buyer "expects." It is not a contract term, and I treat it as the driver of reorders.
- The 25% launch promotion is billed back on the $8.99 shelf price, which is $2.25 a jar. If it is 25% of the $5.40 wholesale price, the cost falls to $1.35 a jar, about $9,300 instead of $15,600. The contract needs to say which.
- Slotting is netted from invoices as they come in. Freight is $0.30 a jar. Halvard's minimum is 5,000 jars per flavor, so a run is 15,000 jars.
- Your current business is left out of the cash math because I don't know its margin or costs. Its contribution would shrink the gap below but not close it.
- You gave no reference class, so the ones I use are my judgment and are labeled as such.

## The strongest case for this

Calloway wants all three flavors in every store, which is a placement most small brands never get. At the buyer's own rate, the account is about $242,000 of wholesale revenue a year, roughly twice what you sell now. Each jar clears $2.00 (37% of wholesale) after the co-packer and freight, and the switch ends the rented-kitchen production load. The trial batch gives you a sign-off gate before the first run. A informed advocate would say this is the step that makes the business bigger than markets and online can.

## What would kill this

Six ways it died, ranked by probability. The compound scenario is entry 1.

### 1. The second production run was paid for before any Calloway money came in, and cash ran out in June (~40%)
**Probability:** ~40%. The cash arithmetic is fixed by the terms; the only thing that varies is whether velocity holds, and the reorder happens only if it does. Reference class: small food producers taking a first chain placement on slotting and net-60 terms, where working capital usually binds before demand does (judgment, weakly sourced). Disanalogy: you already have buyer demand and some existing cash flow, which pushes the number down; the line of credit is capped at $25,000 and unconfirmed, which pushes it up.

**Cause of death:** cash. In dollars, from the brief's own terms:
- January 5: Halvard deposit, 50% of 15,000 jars at $3.10, is $23,250. Cash left: $36,750.
- Late February: balance $23,250 plus freight 15,000 × $0.30 = $4,500. Cash left: $9,000.
- The first run sells through around mid-June (about 13,300 sellable jars at 864 a week). The next run needs a deposit in early May: $23,250. Cash: about −$14,250.
- Late June: balance and freight, $27,750. Cash: −$42,000.
- Calloway pays net 60, and slotting absorbs the first invoices, so the first net dollar from Calloway lands around late July.

**How it unfolded:**
- Month 1 (January): you pay the deposit on a trial batch that hasn't run yet. The December 15 signing sits three weeks ahead of that order.
- Months 2–3 (February–March): the balance and freight take $27,750, leaving about $9,000. Stock arrives late (see entry 3).
- Month 4 (April): the promotion sells at a loss of $0.25 a jar. Invoices are netted against slotting and billback, so no cash comes in.
- Month 5 (May): the reorder deposit takes cash below zero.
- Month 6 (June): the balance takes cash to about −$42,000, with $36,000 of slotting still being netted from invoices.
- Month 7 (July): the first net Calloway cash arrives, too late for June.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the six-a-week rate holds from March, so the reorder is needed, and that net-60 is cash you can wait for.
**First warning sign:** a 13-week cash forecast showing a negative balance before the May deposit. It is visible in December or January if someone builds the forecast.
**Guard in the plan:** none. The line of credit is counted in the cash total but is not confirmed for deposits.

**Compound scenario:** entry 2 shows up in April scan data, but the May reorder is placed on the buyer's six a week. The deposit goes out before the shortfall is trusted, and unsold first-run jars then expire into credits in September. The stall eats the recovery months and net-60 turns the June gap into an August one.

### 2. Calloway's six a week doesn't arrive, leading to expiry credits and a delisting at the first review (~20%)
**Probability:** ~20%. The rate is an expectation, not a term. Reference class: a buyer's first-year sell-through expectation for a new item, which is often more intention than commitment (judgment, weakly sourced). Disanalogy: this buyer has already given 144 shelf slots, which pushes the chance down; the expectation sits outside the contract, which pushes it up.

**Cause of death:** the product doesn't turn, and the six-month shelf life starts consuming credits. At half velocity (three a week), about 3,800 of the first run's 15,000 jars are unsold by about September 1 and credited back at $5.40 each, roughly $20,000. A chain that sees weak turns at its first review can cut flavors or stores, and nothing in the brief says slotting is refundable.

**How it unfolded:**
- March–April: the promotion carries the volume for eight weeks.
- Late April: post-promotion sales settle at two to three a week per store-flavor.
- May–August: Calloway holds stock but stops reordering, and unsold jars approach expiry.
- September: expired product is credited, slotting is not refunded, and a few stores are dropped.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the buyer's expectation is a fair forecast of sell-through and that the brand will match it at launch.
**First warning sign:** scan data from weeks 3–6 (late March to mid-April) below four a week per store-flavor.
**Guard in the plan:** none.

### 3. The first delivery lands after March 1 because the lead times add up to more than the plan allows (~15%)
**Probability:** ~15%. The date miss is arithmetic. Whether it kills the deal depends on whether Calloway holds it against you (judgment).

**Cause of death:** the plan's own timeline. An order on January 5 plus six weeks gives finished goods on about February 16. The trial batch adds two to three weeks, so finished goods arrive around March 2–9. The brief says the warehouse receives in late February, which would need about seven weeks where the stated lead times total eight to nine. Stores get stock one to two weeks after March 1, and the promotion starts late.

**How it unfolded:**
- December 15: signed with a March 1 date and no slip clause.
- January: order placed; trial sign-off needs your time and a week's turnaround.
- February: finished goods on February 16 at best; trial sign-off pushes them to late February or early March.
- March: warehouse receives in early March, stores in mid-March. The buyer re-plans the reset.
- April: the eight-week promotion starts late, and the first deposit sits longer.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that trial and production run in sequence on the stated lead times, and that March 1 is firm.
**First warning sign:** no written trial date and finished-goods date from Halvard by mid-January.
**Guard in the plan:** none. The December 15 signing, the January order and the March 1 store date are all fixed in the plan, and none has a slip clause.

### 4. Calloway's deductions outrun the invoices (~10%)
**Probability:** ~10%. Deductions are a standing feature of these terms. The risk is the amount beyond plan. Each 1% of year-one gross ($242,000) in unplanned deductions costs about $2,400.

**Cause of death:** expired, damaged and promotional credits have no cap or itemization in the terms, and they are netted against invoices that are already 60 days out. You see the net, not the reasons.

**How it unfolded:** April's first deduction statement shows the promotion billed on shelf price. May's statements net slotting against the invoices. June through August bring damage and expiry claims. By September the net receivable is well below your model.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that invoices reconcile to the terms as written and that you will catch the differences.
**First warning sign:** the first April statement not matching the promotion basis in the contract.
**Guard in the plan:** none. There is no deduction review.

### 5. Costs nobody priced: retailer onboarding, insurance, compliance chargebacks and owner time (~10%)
**Probability:** ~10%. These costs will arrive. Whether they kill the deal depends on how much margin is left.

**Cause of death:** the brief prices product, co-packing, freight and slotting. It does not price a certificate of insurance and vendor setup, barcodes, fines for short or late orders, the owners' delivery and invoicing hours across 144 store-flavors, or reconciling deductions. Against a $2.00 margin, every $1 a jar of unpriced cost wipes out half of it.

**How it unfolded:** January brings insurance and vendor forms. February brings barcode and setup work. March and April bring short-order fines and evenings spent on invoices. By May the real margin is thinner than the model, and the reorder squeezes harder.

**The tie-break with entry 4:** both sit at ~10%. Entry 4 is listed first because its trigger is a statement that arrives on a known schedule in April. Entry 5 depends on which charges Calloway actually applies.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the $2.00 margin is the margin.
**First warning sign:** an onboarding invoice that isn't in the budget before February 1.
**Guard in the plan:** none.

### 6. Success runs out of stock: sell-through beats the forecast and the lead time can't keep up (~5%)
**Probability:** ~5%. It needs sell-through above the buyer's own forecast. Feasibility is low.

**Cause of death:** at nine a week per store-flavor, the first run's sellable jars are gone by about May 13. A reorder placed then lands around June 24, a gap of about six weeks across 48 stores. Out-of-stock charges and lost facings at the reset follow.

**How it unfolded:** strong March and April. Mid-May: the first run is gone. June: shelves empty. July: the reset gives the empty facings to a competitor.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that Halvard's six-week lead time holds for repeat runs and that reorders can be placed on demand.
**First warning sign:** scan data above eight a week in any two consecutive weeks in April.
**Guard in the plan:** none. Halvard's lead time is the only buffer.

**Considered and cut:** co-packer recipe drift at the trial (folded into #3), promotion billback basis (folded into #1), competitor reset (folded into #2).

**Downgraded, guarded for now:**

| Mode | Guard that holds it down | Signal that re-upgrades it |
|---|---|---|
| Co-packed recipe drifts from the market version | Trial batch sign-off before the first run is scheduled | Any taste complaint from farmers-market or online customers after March |

Each store-flavor takes its entry costs back (slotting, free case, promotion loss) around mid-October 2027.

## A rebuilt plan

**The revised plan:** sign only with a written promotion basis, a delisting review after week 12 that refunds or reduces slotting, a dated Halvard trial, and a store date that holds two weeks of slip. Fund the peak with the line of credit plus a factoring quote on Calloway invoices. Or phase the launch: two flavors in March, the third at the first reset. At the same velocity, phasing cuts the trough from about −$42,000 to about −$8,000. That is still short, so the funding line is needed either way.

**What changed and why:**

| Change | Failure mode it closes | What it costs | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Promotion basis (shelf or wholesale), expiry and damage cap, in writing | #4, #1 | A negotiation that may fail | Launch-Blocking, by Dec 10, 2026 |
| Delisting review after week 12, slotting refunded or reduced if delisted | #2 | Calloway may refuse, and the deal may die | Launch-Blocking, by Dec 15, 2026 |
| Dated Halvard trial and finished-goods date before signing, or the store date moved in writing | #3 | Trial fee (quote needed), or a later store date | Launch-Blocking, by Dec 15, 2026 |
| Phase flavors: two in March, one at first reset | #1 (trough about −$42k to about −$8k) | Calloway may refuse "all three from March" | Launch-Blocking, by Dec 15, 2026 |
| Funding confirmed in writing: line of credit, plus factoring quote | #1 | Factoring fees (quote needed) | Launch-Blocking, by Dec 15, 2026 |
| Price onboarding and compliance costs: insurance, vendor setup, short-order reserve, owner hours | #5 | Some cents a jar against a $2.00 margin | Fast-Follow, by Jan 15, 2027 |
| Weekly store-level scan data written into the contract from week one | #2, #6 | Calloway may decline | Track, with tripwires below |

**Response labels:** entry 1 (the most dangerous) is **limit**. Velocity can't be moved by you, but phasing and the funding line contain the damage. Entry 4 is **prevent** (contract caps). Entry 5 is **prevent** (price it).

**Decision rule:** **Sign on December 15 IF** the promotion basis, the week-12 review clause and a dated Halvard trial are in the contract AND the lender confirms, in writing, funding for the peak (unphased about $42,000, phased about $8,000 plus buffer). **ELSE** ask Calloway for 30 days to fix the dates and terms. If Calloway won't move the March 1 date or the review clause, decline.

**If the decision rule lands on delay or decline: the reverse autopsy.** The horizon passed. We declined in December, and it was a mistake. Markets and online stayed near $9,000 a month because no new shoppers came. A competing brand took the three shelf slots at the first reset. The savings were still there, and we had no account bigger than the markets.

**What tips the balance:** written agreement to the review clause and a dated trial, even without phasing. Without that, the caution stands.

**Consciously accepted (at most one):** the promotion billback of about $15,600. It is the buyer's launch condition. During the eight promotion weeks the jars earn about $13,800 at base margin, so the promotion costs about $1,700 more than they bring in.

**How the rebuilt plan dies:** the new weakest joint is the factoring line. A regional chain's invoices that are already being deducted may be expensive to factor or unavailable. If it fails, phasing alone leaves a June gap of about $8,000. The second joint is Halvard's trial gate, which is theirs to schedule.

**Residual risk:** velocity. Even rebuilt, Calloway is about twice your current revenue, so one buyer's slow review would hurt badly.

## The adversary

**Playing:** Calloway's buyer and category team. They gain the most: $36,000 of slotting, promotion funding, expiry and damage credits, net-60 on your money, and a supplier carrying all the risk. They can't guarantee sell-through and they are rewarded for shelf space, not for your brand's velocity. Their best alternative is a national chili-crisp brand with stronger turns, and it costs them nothing to replace you at the first review.

**The kill chain:**
1. **Probe (December 2026):** sign with a March 1 date, no slip clause, and slotting netted from invoices.
2. **Position (March 1 to April 25, 2027):** an eight-week promotion funded by you, with the billback running whether or not stores display it.
3. **Strike (late May 2027):** the twelve-week review. Velocity below plan, so one or two flavors are cut and slotting is not refunded.
4. **Entrench (June to September 2027):** expiry and damage credits netted through the summer, and the reset gives facings to a competitor.

**Detection opportunities:**
- December 2026: the contract draft shows no slip clause and no review clause. Signal: the draft itself, before December 15.
- April 2027: the first deduction statement shows the billback on shelf price. Signal: the statement.
- April 19, 2027: scan data for weeks 3–6.
- Late May 2027: any written review notice.
- July 2027: expiry and damage claims.

**The move you'd never see coming:** the billback is paid whether or not the stores run the display. Calloway can take your funding for the promotion without proving the promotion happened.

**What this means you should change:** get proof-of-performance for promotion billbacks (display records by store), the review clause, and scan data from week one, before you sign.

**Kill condition:** stop if the Halvard trial and finished-goods dates are still unconfirmed on January 29, 2027, and Calloway won't move the March 1 date or the promotion basis in writing. Also stop if velocity is under four in weeks 3–6 and the projected cash at the May reorder is below −$10,000 with no funding line.

**What this premortem probably missed:** the existing farmers-market and online business. The analysis treats Calloway as additive. It doesn't price whether a co-packed jar changes repeat buying at markets, or whether two owners can run a 48-store account alongside those channels. The frame under-weights that because the brief doesn't mention it.

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **Contract terms in writing.** Check the promotion basis, slotting refund on delisting, the expiry and damage cap, and the slip clause against the draft. Walk away if the promotion is billed on shelf price with no cap and no review clause.
2. **Halvard dates.** Ask in writing for a trial slot and a finished-goods date. Walk away if neither is committed by December 15, or if the trial can't run before February 16. The second case falsifies the March 1 date.
3. **Velocity evidence.** Ask Calloway for store-level scan data on comparable items. Walk away if comparable items sell under four a week per store. That falsifies the six-a-week assumption.
4. **Funding.** Get the line of credit confirmed in writing and a factoring quote on Calloway invoices. Walk away if committed funding is under $42,000 unphased, or under about $8,000 phased, plus a buffer.
5. **Onboarding costs.** Get quotes for the insurance certificate and vendor setup, and set a reserve for short-order fines. Walk away if unpriced costs exceed $0.50 a jar, a quarter of the margin.

Commitment grades: Calloway's buyer endorses the placement but has not committed to the six a week (accept). Halvard endorses the price, but the trial and dates are uncommitted (comply). Both need to be on paper before you sign.
