# Ledgerwood expansion to 14 schools: where it breaks

**Horizon:** April 15, 2027 (the mid-April benchmark and the summer release decision) · **Success was defined as:** all 14 schools running in January, and the second-year $600k released next summer.

**Note on the percentages:** these are shares of failure: "if this dies, this is what killed it." They sum to about 100. **Overall chance this misses its success definition by the horizon:** likely (above half), because the second-year condition is out of reach on the plan's own numbers (see the second entry).

**Assumptions I'm working from:**
- Today is October 13, 2026, after the October 8 vote date you gave. I treat the vote as pending or already decided, and count the timeline from today.
- Coordinators cost $50k a month from November 1, flat through the grant. That is how I read "once coordinators start."
- The $1.2M is $600k per year. The first-year $600k equals twelve months at $50k, so there is no float.
- The current 70 tutors stay with the four existing schools.
- I know of no district clearance rules beyond what the plan states. Where I say "if," I mean it.

## The strongest case for this plan

Four schools already run a working model with a measured 12-point result after a full year. Arbelle has put $1.2M on the table, so the funder has seen the model and wants it to grow. The district has accepted the program at four schools, and all ten new principals are enthusiastic. Harmon has supplied about 70 tutors, so a pipeline exists and has worked. The $50k monthly cost averages exactly to $1.2M over 24 months, so the budget looks sized to the program. An informed advocate would say expansion is the obvious next step and the risks are scheduling problems that a competent director can manage.

## Ways this fails

Six ways this died, ranked by probability.

### 1. The district-office MOU is not signed by January 12, and ten coordinators are on payroll for schools that cannot open.

**Probability:** ~40%. The plan gives no evidence the MOU has been filed, and the one precedent took about five months.

**Cause of death:** Fennick's MOU, required for any outside program using school space or student data, has not cleared for the ten new schools when the January 12 start arrives. The most certain statement in the plan is that the principals are excited. That is true and does not bind anyone: each principal can say yes, but the signature sits with the district office. Commitment grade for the principals is **Accept** at best, since they have agreed in conversation and signed nothing that counts.

**How it unfolded:**
- October–November: the MOU request goes in covering fourteen schools rather than four, and it routes through a review that has not been scheduled. Coordinators are hired November 1 as planned.
- December: district review is still open. Tutor recruiting runs against a start date that no one outside the organization has confirmed.
- January 12: the start passes without a signed MOU. Ten coordinators are paid for schools they cannot enter. The cash drain begins (see the fourth entry).
- February–March: the MOU signs, or it goes back for revisions. The new schools open in March at the earliest, a quarter late, and the year-2 window shrinks (see the second entry).

**The assumption that allowed it:** that "we know the people there now" shortens the approval. It may shorten one signature. It does not shorten a review of ten new buildings' space and student-data use. The plan's own timing rule applies: approvals with a lead time over two weeks run two to three times the stated timeline until evidence says otherwise, and here there is no evidence at all.

**First warning sign:** no MOU draft routed for review by November 30. The coordinator hires on November 1 are the point of no return for the payroll.

**Reference class:** their own four-school MOU, about five months. Disanalogy: the relationships are warmer now, which pushes toward faster. The scope is ten new buildings against four, which pushes slower and dominates. The reference class sets the number.

### 2. The new schools get about two to three months of effective tutoring before the mid-April test, and they miss the 15-point bar. The $600k never releases.

**Probability:** ~20%. This needs the January launch to partly survive, so it is less likely to be the first thing that kills the plan. The plan's own arithmetic says it fails anyway, which is why it ranks second.

**Cause of death:** the plan misses its own target on its own numbers. The existing four schools reach +12 after a full October–April year, roughly seven months of tutoring. The new schools, even on the plan's dates, reach full staffing in February or March and are tested in mid-April, so they get two to three months of effective exposure. The bar is 15 points, which is above the best result the program has measured. Asking schools with a third to a half of the exposure to beat the full-exposure result by three points is the plan missing its own bar.

**How it unfolded:**
- January–February: new schools staff partially, tutor counts lag (see the third entry), and attendance runs below plan.
- March: new schools have about two months of tutoring. Coordinators report catch-up plans. Nobody has asked whether the catch-up can close a gap of this size.
- Mid-April: the benchmark shows the new schools in single digits at best (my estimate, not a sourced figure). The bar is not met.
- Summer: the second-year $600k is not released. The board has to cut year-2 spending while the new schools are already running.

**Reference class:** the existing four schools' own year, 12 points after a full year. Disanalogy: the new schools have less exposure, comparison groups that have not been matched, and untested staff. Each pushes the number down. The 15-point bar itself is not justified by any figure in the plan.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that new schools will perform like the existing four despite starting late, and that the 15-point bar was calibrated to something real.

**First warning sign:** by March 15, the share of new-school students with at least eight tutoring sessions. A shortfall shows in early March, about six weeks before the test, which is the last point where the metric can be renegotiated.

**Compound:** see the fourth entry. The two are linked: a cash squeeze in February cuts coordinator hours, and fewer hours lower the attendance that the bar depends on.

### 3. Tutor supply on January 12 is a fraction of the 175 needed, and the new schools open with a thin, unsupervised base.

**Probability:** ~15%. It is close to certain that the launch is short of tutors. It kills the plan only if the shortfall is not patched by a later start, so its share of the death is smaller than its likelihood.

**Cause of death:** the plan's own ratio is about 2.3 kids per tutor (160 kids on about 70 tutors). Four hundred new kids need about 175 tutors on day one. Harmon's spring semester starts January 20, eight days after the planned January 12 start, so new practicum students cannot earn credit before then. The plan also does not say whether the current 70 tutors, whose practicum credit is tied to the fall term, will tutor through the December break or the January gap.

**How it unfolded:**
- December: open-call recruits are fewer than planned, and the practicum seats for spring are not confirmed in writing.
- January 12: ten new schools open with a handful of tutors each. Coordinators split the 70 existing tutors across fourteen schools.
- January 20 onward: spring practicum students arrive but need training first. The first complete tutor cohort at a new school arrives in late February.
- February: sessions run below plan, kids miss sessions, and coordinators burn out in their second month.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that a university practicum pipeline can deliver 175 trained tutors on a semester calendar that starts a week after the launch date.

**First warning sign:** confirmed spring tutor count below 100 on December 15.

**Reference class:** none offered at intake for tutor recruiting, so this is a judgment call with no statistic behind it. The current 70 tutors show Harmon can supply a pipeline, which pushes the risk down. That pipeline serves four schools on a fixed term, which pushes it up for a sudden 175.

### 4. The reserves run out in February, and the first reimbursement arrives in mid-March.

**Probability:** ~10%. The arithmetic makes a gap close to certain if the plan runs as written, but the gap kills the plan only if no bridge is found. A bridge is survivable, so this mode dies on a second failure.

**Cause of death:** $150k in reserves against $50k a month from November 1 covers exactly November through January. Arbelle reimburses quarterly in arrears, about 45 days after the quarter closes. The November–January quarter closes January 31, so the claim pays around March 17. February and early March spend, about $100k, has no money behind it. The deficit peaks near $100k from about March 1 to March 16. The plan treats the grant as cash on hand when it is a receivable.

**The compound scenario:** the MOU slips (first entry). The coordinators keep drawing pay against schools that cannot open, and the January expense report claims costs for a program that did not run. Arbelle may question costs that are not tied to delivered activity, which would push the reimbursement later still. Lose the MOU, and the cash cliff arrives on schedule and is worse.

**How it unfolded:**
- November–January: spend runs at $50k a month. Reserves reach zero on January 31.
- February 1: no cash for February payroll. Either pay is late or the program cuts coordinator hours.
- March 1: a deficit of about $100k. The emergency choice is to cut hours or stop tutor recruiting.
- March 17: the reimbursement arrives and the cash crisis ends, but two months of hours have already been cut, which feeds the second entry.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the grant's payment terms are a financing plan rather than a timing problem.

**First warning sign:** the January 31 balance near zero, with no bridge line approved by then.

### 5. The plan works, and what kills it is the tutors pulled from the four schools that proved the model.

**Probability:** ~10%. Tied with the fourth entry; see the tie-break below.

**Cause of death:** to staff ten new schools in January, coordinators pull part of the 70-tutor base from the four schools that carry the 12-point result. Those schools lose consistency: fewer tutors per student, more missed sessions. Their spring result drops below 12. The year-2 case rests on that number, so the proof erodes at the same time the expansion is claimed as a success.

**How it unfolded:**
- January: 70 tutors are spread across fourteen schools.
- February: session rates fall at the four existing schools. Coordinators read the stable headcount as a good sign and do not compare it with the baseline.
- April: the four existing schools land in single digits (my estimate). The program now shows the model does not hold at scale, which the funder will read in the year-2 decision.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the 70 tutors are interchangeable across schools, and that nobody tracks the four existing schools separately during the expansion.

**First warning sign:** existing-school tutor count below 60 by February 1.

**Tie-break (fourth and fifth entries, both on the 10% rung):** the fourth ranks first because its cash arithmetic is already on the page and needs no other failure to happen first. The fifth needs the January launch to succeed before it can kill anything, which is the less likely path.

### 6. Clearance and onboarding time for 175 tutors is never scheduled.

**Probability:** ~5%. The plan is silent on district clearance. If clearance is required, which is common for school-based work, the gap is real and measured in weeks. If it is not required, this mode drops out.

**Cause of death:** a tutor cannot enter a building until clearance is complete. Clearance for a batch of 175 is a queue, and the plan does not put it into the November–December training window.

**How it unfolded:**
- December: applicants pile up in the clearance queue.
- January 12: tutors exist on paper but not in buildings.
- February: tutors enter in waves, and the effective start moves further out.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the plan's silence means nothing is required.

**First warning sign:** median time from application to first session above 21 days by January 31.

**Considered and cut:** coordinator hiring delays (folded into the first entry), principal support fading (folded into the first entry's commitment grade), comparison-group data access dispute (folded into the first entry, since the MOU is the instrument that covers student data).

**Downgraded, guarded for now:**

| Mode | Guard that holds it down | Signal that re-upgrades it |
|---|---|---|
| Tutor attrition after week six | Harmon practicum credit ties tutors to the term | Any practicum tutor withdraws before May |
| Comparison-group data refused | The MOU already covers student data for the current four schools | The district asks for a separate data agreement |

**Most likely:** #1. It has the highest share and the highest I×F among the high scorers. The MOU sits outside the plan's control, and the plan has no evidence it has started.

**Most dangerous:** #2. Impact is 5, and the warning sign comes in early March while the outcome is not measured until mid-April. **The hidden assumption:** the January 12 start is real. Entries 1, 2, 3, and 4 all depend on it. The plan treats the start as a fixed date and everything after it as a scheduling question. **Fatal flaw:** the January 12 date. At the plan's own lead times, the district approval, the tutor count, and the cash position cannot all be in place for that date. The binding constraint is the calendar, not the program.

**Commitment grades, held rather than narrated:**

- District office: no commitment yet, below Endorse. Convert to paper before any hiring beyond the existing four.
- Harmon practicum office: no documented commitment on spring seats, below Endorse. Convert to paper.

## Rebuild

**The revised plan:** do not launch ten schools on January 12. Launch four on February 9 if the district has signed a four-school MOU and Harmon has confirmed enough spring tutors. Hold the other six until a second MOU is signed and tutor supply is confirmed. Ask Arbelle for a year-2 bar set against new-school exposure, or a two-year measure, before the grant agreement is signed. Hire only three coordinators on November 1, and the rest after the MOU signs. Ring-fence the 70 tutors for the four existing schools, and recruit new tutors separately.

**What changed and why:**

| Change | Failure mode it closes | What it costs | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hire 3 coordinators November 1; the rest only after an MOU signs (decision date December 15) | #1, #4 | Slower start for six of ten schools; a month of coordinator time lost if the MOU signs early | Launch-blocking |
| Phase the launch: four schools February 9, six later on signed MOUs | #1, #3 | Fewer schools in the year-2 window; accepted, see below | Launch-blocking |
| Get a written payment schedule from Arbelle, or a board-approved bridge of at least $100k, by January 15 | #4 | Bridge interest, or a negotiation with the funder | Launch-blocking |
| Renegotiate the year-2 bar before the grant agreement is signed | #2 | Political capital with the funder | Launch-blocking |
| Confirm spring practicum seats with Harmon in writing by November 15 | #3 | Staff time | Launch-blocking |
| Ring-fence the 70 existing tutors for the four current schools | #5 | Slower expansion on the same budget | Fast-follow |
| Schedule clearance and onboarding into the training window, if clearance applies | #6 | Two to three weeks of staff time to map the process | Track |

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **District MOU status.** Check in writing with the district office that a four-school MOU is in review. Walk away if there is no routing record by November 30 and no signature date by December 15.
2. **Harmon spring seats.** Get the practicum seat count in writing. Walk away from any launch beyond four schools if fewer than 100 spring tutors are confirmed by December 15.
3. **Arbelle payment terms.** Get written confirmation of invoice timing. Walk away from hiring beyond three coordinators if the funder will not agree to monthly billing or a bridge.
4. **Year-2 metric.** Get the bar in writing. Walk away from the second-year commitment if it stays at 15 points against a single April test.
5. **Principal letters.** Get a signed letter from each new principal. Walk away from any school whose principal has not signed by December 1.

Items 1 and 2 gate the top two assumptions. Their falsifiers are the first two checks above: no MOU routing by November 30, and fewer than 100 confirmed tutors by December 15.

**Decision rule:** IF a four-school MOU is signed by December 15 AND at least 100 spring tutors are confirmed AND Arbelle agrees in writing to monthly billing or a bridge of at least $100k is approved, THEN hire the remaining coordinators for the first cohort and launch four schools on February 9. ELSE hire three coordinators, keep the expansion to the four current schools, and move the new-school launch to August.

**If the decision rule lands on delay, the reverse autopsy:** the horizon passed, and we did not expand. It was a mistake. First, the February window closed, and Arbelle used the second-year slot on a program that had a working spring calendar. Second, the four current schools were the only evidence the funder had, and the delay read as weak execution rather than caution. Third, the bridge we did not take was the cheapest money the organization would ever have been offered.

**What tips the balance:** if Arbelle agrees in writing to a bar set against new-school exposure, the caution stands for January, but the expansion is worth signing. ELSE branch: if the district signs a four-school MOU by December 15 and Harmon confirms at least 100 spring tutors, expand on the February 9 date.

**Consciously accepted (one):** a later start for six schools. Cost: about seven weeks less tutoring at those schools, which makes the year-2 bar harder to reach for them. That cost is the price of not carrying a $100k deficit through March.

**How the rebuilt plan dies:** the new weakest joint is the funder's payment terms. A bridge line still depends on board approval, and a monthly billing schedule still carries a 30-day lag. The fix for the cash gap is strictly better than the original plan, but it leans on one board vote in January. The year-2 renegotiation is the same kind of joint: it works only if the funder agrees.

**Residual risk:** the year-2 bar may stay at 15 points even after renegotiation, and four new schools with two months of exposure may still miss it. The district may reject the four-school MOU, and then the whole expansion moves to August.

## The calendar as adversary

No competitor is in this plan, so the pressure comes from the calendar and the review queues it runs through. I have reframed this section around the calendar.

**Playing:** the calendar gains most from the plan proceeding as written. It has no resources to commit and no stake in the outcome, but every date in the plan is fixed, so a late MOU or a late practicum seat is not absorbed. Its best alternative cost is nothing; the only cost is the plan's.

**Kill chain (dated):**
1. **October–November:** the MOU enters a review queue that is not scheduled. Coordinators hire on November 1 regardless.
2. **December:** Harmon's spring seats are unconfirmed, and tutor recruiting runs against a date no one has confirmed.
3. **January 12:** the start date passes with no MOU and no tutors. The January 20 semester start pushes tutors out a week further.
4. **February–March:** the cash gap opens, and coordinator hours are cut.
5. **Mid-April:** the benchmark arrives and the bar is not met.

**Detection opportunities:** the MOU routing record by November 30; the confirmed spring-seat count by December 15; the January 31 balance; the February 1 existing-school tutor count; the March 15 new-school session share; the April 15 benchmark.

**The move you would never see coming:** the district changes the person who approved the four current schools. The relationship that makes "we know the people there now" true is one retirement or reassignment away from being a new, cold review. This sits outside the plan's frame, which models the district as a fixed process.

**What this means you should change:** do not treat the January date as fixed. Get the MOU routing and the Harmon seats in writing before any hire beyond three coordinators.

**Kill condition:** the MOU is unsigned by December 15 AND fewer than 100 spring tutors are confirmed by December 15. Together they mean stop the expansion this year and keep the four current schools whole. Either one alone means adjust.

**What this premortem probably missed:** the district's own priorities. The frame treats the MOU as a process with a timeline. It does not model what the district office wants from a ten-school expansion, what its staff are juggling, or whether the district's budget or leadership will change before spring. The plan's biggest external dependency is the one this analysis treats most mechanically.
