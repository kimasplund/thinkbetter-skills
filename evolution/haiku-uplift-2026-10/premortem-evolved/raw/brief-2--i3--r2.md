# Ledgerwood expansion: where it breaks before January

**Horizon:** July 1, 2027 · **Success, as you defined it:** all 14 schools running in January, and the second-year $600k released next summer

**Note on the percentages:** these are rough shares of the failure, meaning "if this plan dies, this is what most likely killed it." They are not the overall chance the plan fails. They sum to about 100.

**Assumptions I'm working from**
- Today is October 13, 2026, and you said the board votes October 8. I'm treating the decision as still open for changes. If the vote has already happened, read the decision rules below as applying to your next irreversible step.
- Arbelle's payment timing follows the 45-day rule, so the November–January payment lands around March 17, 2027.
- The 70 current tutors are fall-semester practicum students. I assume they are not free to tutor in January on the plan's schedule.
- The $50k a month covers coordinators only. Tutor recruitment, background checks, and training are not priced anywhere in the plan.
- The second-year money is released after the summer, and year two starts after the April benchmark.

## The strongest case for this plan

You run a working model in four schools, with a measured result: students outscore comparison peers by about 12 points after a full year. A funder has put $1.2M in writing. Principals are enthusiastic, and the district has already tolerated your program. A careful advocate would say the hard part is done: the model works, the money is offered, and the expansion is a matter of hiring and scheduling. The risks below are about timing and cash, not about whether one-on-one tutoring works.

Six ways this most likely breaks, ranked by share. I stopped at six rather than padding to seven. Two smaller risks, a principal backing out and burnout after month four, are folded into the ones below.

### 1. The reserve runs out before Arbelle's first payment (~40%)
**Why this number:** it is arithmetic on your own figures, not a judgment call. It fires unless you have a bridge you haven't mentioned.

**Cause of death:** your $150k in reserves equals the November–January spend exactly, at $50k a month from November 1. Arbelle pays that quarter around March 17. Reserves reach zero on January 31. By February 28 you are about $50k short, and by mid-March about $75k short, before the reimbursement lands.

**How it unfolded:**
- November: ten coordinators start and $50k goes out with no schools open. Reserves fall to about $100k.
- December: coordinators recruit and train. Tutor recruitment, background checks, and training costs, which are not in the $50k, push spending higher.
- January: the program launches January 12. Reserves hit zero on January 31. The quarter's expense report goes in at close.
- February: spending continues and nothing comes in. The gap is about $50k by February 28.
- Mid-March: Arbelle pays roughly $150k. The gap peaked near $75k. Any late report, documentation request, or unpriced tutor cost pushes the payment later, and the gap grows with it.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the reserve is a buffer, when it is really exactly the first quarter's spend, and that Arbelle's payment date is a fixed fact rather than a process you don't control.

**First warning sign:** the November statement. If the balance is below $100k on December 1 with no bridge committed, this mode has started.

**Reference class:** expansions funded by reimbursement in arrears, where the grantee carries the float. **Disanalogy:** your funder pays on a fixed 45-day schedule, which pushes the risk down. You hold no float beyond the first quarter, which pushes it up. Net, your arithmetic decides it, not the class.

**Interaction with #2:** if the district signature slips, coordinators are still paid for two or more extra months with no schools open. The November–January report then shows $150k of salary with no new-school tutoring behind it. If Arbelle reviews outcomes before paying, the gap widens. This link is not verified. Whether Arbelle reviews outputs before paying is the first thing to ask.

### 2. The district MOU is not signed by January 12 (~20%)
**Why this number:** your own four-school MOU took five months. The window from October 8 to January 12 is about three. The plan has no line for the MOU at all, so nothing is scheduled to start it.

**Cause of death:** no school space or student-data access at the ten new schools until the district office signs. Principals cannot sign it, and the people you know there may not be the signers.

**How it unfolded:**
- November: the MOU enters the district queue with no date. Coordinators start with nothing to do but recruit.
- December: the district asks data-protection questions because the scope is now 400 more students at 10 schools, not 4.
- January 12: the launch date passes unsigned. Coordinators are on payroll for schools they cannot enter.
- February–March: the MOU is signed, possibly with conditions. The launch moves, and the tutor pipeline now starts after Harmon's spring term has begun.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the MOU clears on your calendar. The plan never schedules it.

**Merged here:** the district contact you know leaves or is reassigned before signature. That restarts the queue. It shares this mode's trigger (the district office) and timing (before launch), so it is one mode.

**First warning sign:** no written signature date from the district by December 1.

**Your own reference class:** the four-school MOU, five months. **Disanalogy:** your existing relationships pull this down. Ten schools and a new data-sharing scope push it up. This is the one number where your own record is the best anchor, and it points late.

### 3. The January 12 launch runs on the 70 existing tutors (~15%)
**Why this number:** the arithmetic is firm, but "running in January" can still be claimed at reduced reach, so this mode kills success less often than it hurts.

**Cause of death:** 400 new students twice a week needs about 175 tutors at your current ratio of roughly 2.3 students per tutor. You have about 70, and they are tied to fall practicum. Harmon's spring term starts January 20, eight days after launch. Recruits signed up in November and December cannot tutor before then.

**How it unfolded:**
- January 12: ten schools open with about 70 tutors spread thin. Reach is about 160 students, roughly 40% of the target.
- Late January: the 175 new recruits arrive in one cohort. Training runs behind, and coordinators spend their time triaging waitlists instead of supervising.
- February: the cohort tutors have little supervised practice. Dosage stays low, which feeds #4.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that Harmon students can be placed, trained, and cleared for school work within about three months, and that Harmon's credit approval runs on the plan's schedule.

**Concentration:** the entire tutor pipeline depends on one university's practicum office.

**First warning sign:** fewer than 120 spring practicum registrations for the new sites by December 15.

**Reference class (pattern-assumption, not sourced):** student-staffed tutoring programs usually ramp more slowly than their plans show. **Disanalogy:** Harmon already supplies 70 working tutors, and the spring start date is fixed. Both push the number up.

### 4. The second-year money is lost on the April benchmark (~10%, most dangerous)
**Why this number:** the bar is three points above what the current four schools achieved with a full year. It only looks reachable if the new schools get a full year, and they don't.

**Cause of death:** January 12 to the mid-April benchmark is about 13 weeks. Your current schools had a full October–April year and produced 12 points. The new schools get roughly a quarter of the instructional time, at partial reach, with a first cohort of tutors. The 15-point bar is measured against a comparison group that is not yet defined.

**How it unfolded:**
- January–February: thin reach, waitlists, and dosage well below plan.
- March: tutors stabilize, but too late to change the dosage picture.
- Mid-April: the benchmark shows a gap of about 8 to 10 points. The $600k is not released.
- Summer: ten sites are running on about $50k a month with no year-two funding. The board has to choose between cutting sites and finding a bridge.

**The assumption that allowed it:** that the 15-point bar is a reasonable target for a half-year at new schools, when it is a stretch even at full strength.

**Why this is the most dangerous mode:** it is silent. Nothing shows until mid-April, after about four months of payroll, and after the point of no return: coordinators hired, schools committed, and families enrolled.

**Incentive note:** the funder defines the success metric, and nothing in the plan says what happens if the comparison group is weaker than expected. No dissent about this is recorded in the plan.

**First warning sign:** average sessions per student per week at the new schools, measured February 15. Below about 1.5 of the planned 2 is the early chain for this mode.

**Response:** limit. The likelihood of the benchmark cannot be moved much by February, so the response is containment: a year-two plan at six schools that does not depend on the gate, and a written definition of the comparison group agreed with Arbelle before launch.

### 5. Referrals outrun placements once the program is visible (~10%)
**Cause of death:** once schools announce the program, principals and teachers refer more than 40 students per site. Forty is a plan ceiling, not a referral ceiling. Waitlists become complaints, and coordinators start admitting students without matching tutors to protect the reach number. One-on-one slots get crowded, and the quality of the tutoring drops.

**The tie with #4:** both sit at about 10%. #4 is ranked first because its share comes from the dosage arithmetic. #5 rests on an unsourced guess about referral rates.

**First warning sign:** a waitlist above 40 students at any site by February 15.

### 6. Coordinators sit idle and two leave before launch (~5%)
**Cause of death:** ten hires on November 1 with no school access until at least January 12, and possibly later. That is about ten weeks with nothing to coordinate except recruiting tutors who are not yet available. Two of the ten take other jobs by February.

**Guard against it:** none in the plan.

**First warning sign:** two or more coordinators gone by February 28.

## What I'd change

**The revised plan:**
1. Before any hiring, get Arbelle's payment terms in writing. Ask for monthly payment or an advance. If they refuse, arrange a written bridge of roughly $75k.
2. Price tutor recruitment, background checks, and training, and add them to the budget.
3. Stagger coordinator starts: four on November 1, the other six once the district gives a written signature date.
4. Open in two waves. Six sites on January 12 if Harmon confirms at least 120 placements. The other four on February 1.
5. Plan year two around six schools, and get the comparison group defined in writing with Arbelle before launch.

**What changed and why:**

| Change | Mode it closes | What it costs | Tier | Decision date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Written payment terms or a ~$75k bridge from Arbelle | #1 | Fees or interest on the bridge, unpriced | Launch-Blocking | Oct 31, before any hire |
| Staggered coordinator starts | #1, #2, #6 | The last six sites open about six weeks later | Launch-Blocking | Dec 1 |
| Written MOU signature date, with the data scope named | #2 | Staff time, and possibly fewer schools | Launch-Blocking | Dec 15 |
| Two-wave opening, six sites on Jan 12 | #3 | About 240 students, not 400, in January | Launch-Blocking | Dec 15 |
| Tutor recruitment, checks, and training priced in | #1 | Adds to the bridge need; amount unknown | Launch-Blocking | Nov 15 |
| Year-two plan at six schools; comparison group defined in writing | #4 | A narrower year two | Track | Jan 15 |

No change costs more than the mode it closes. The two-wave opening trades January reach for cash, which is the right trade while #1 is live.

**Response to the most dangerous mode:** limit (the six-school year-two plan and the written comparison group).

**Consciously accepted (at most one):** partial reach in January and February. About 240 students are not served for roughly six weeks, which is the priced cost of not opening all ten sites until the tutor pipeline exists.

**Pre-launch checklist:**
1. **Payment terms.** Check: Arbelle's written payment schedule and whether the first payment can be monthly. How: ask Arbelle for the terms in writing. Walk away: no written terms or bridge by October 31, so do not hire the ten coordinators. Falsifier: reserves below $100k on December 1 with no bridge in place.
2. **District MOU.** Check: a written signature date and the name of the signer. How: ask the district office directly. Walk away: no date by December 15. Falsifier: fewer than six of the ten school agreements signed by January 8.
3. **Harmon placements.** Check: confirmed practicum registrations for the new sites. How: written confirmation from Harmon's practicum office. Walk away: under 120 by December 15, which means opening six sites, not ten. Falsifier: fewer than 120 registrations on December 15.
4. **Comparison group.** Check: a written definition of the comparison group and who draws it. How: agree it with Arbelle. Walk away: if Arbelle will not define it before launch, do not treat the April number as a fixed trigger. Falsifier: the district sets the comparison group after the spring test.

**Decision rule (for the irreversible hires):** hire the first four coordinators on November 1 IF Arbelle's payment terms are in writing and the bridge covers November through February. ELSE hold all ten until they are. Open the six first-wave sites on January 12 IF the MOU has a signature date and Harmon confirms at least 120 placements. ELSE open them on February 1 once both are met. If neither condition is met by December 31, reduce to four new schools and keep the four current ones.

**If the decision rule lands on delay, the reverse autopsy:** the horizon passed. We delayed to February 1 and it turned out to be a mistake. First, Arbelle re-scoped the offer to the four current schools because the 14-school timeline had slipped, and the $1.2M shrank. Second, four coordinators took district jobs while we waited. Third, the expansion started with a shorter dose, and the April gate was missed by the same margin.

**What tips the balance:** if Arbelle agrees in writing to monthly payments, the cash gap in #1 largely closes. The caution on the April gate stands either way.

**How the rebuilt plan dies:** the staggered hiring ties everything to the December 15 signature date. That is the new weakest joint. If the district names a date after December 15, the six-site wave slips and the bridge must cover longer. The two-wave opening is not strictly dominant, because it lowers January reach.

**Residual risk:** the April benchmark. Even with the rebuild, a half-year at new schools may not reach 15 points. The rebuild limits the damage, but does not change the odds.

## Who has the most say here

There is no competitor. The party whose calendar matters most is the district office. Its incentive is to keep the answer slow rather than say no, which means nobody has to be blamed for a delay.

**Dated sequence:**
- November 1–15: the MOU sits in the queue with no date. (The district probes.)
- December: data-protection questions arrive for 400 additional students. (The district repositions the scope.)
- January 12: the launch passes unsigned. (The delay becomes the fact on the ground.)
- February–March: the MOU is signed with conditions that narrow it to six schools. (The district locks in its terms.)

**Detection:** no date by November 15. Review questions in December. No signature by January 8.

**The move you would not see coming:** the district does not block the MOU. It narrows the scope to six schools and signs. All 14 schools then look "running" on paper, but only six have a signed agreement.

**What to change:** get a signature date in writing and define the scope in writing before launch.

**What this probably missed:** school-day logistics. The plan treats the district as a checkpoint and principals as the owners of school time. It hasn't priced pull-out schedules, room availability, whether 30-minute sessions fit ten new bell schedules, or the district's own testing calendar in February through April, which overlaps the benchmark. Principals' enthusiasm was taken as clearance, but the people who control school time are not the same people.
