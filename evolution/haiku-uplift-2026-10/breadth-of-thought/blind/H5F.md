Short answer: you don't need to buy the whole field to get through the next 25 years, and buying it is the most expensive way to get there. There are five realistic routes. The strongest plan is probably a combination of two or three of them.

**The starting numbers**

At 95 burials a year, 25 years is about 2,400 burials. You have 360 spaces, which is about four years. The 380 reserved plots in the east section would add roughly 740 in total, or about eight years. That leaves a gap of roughly 1,600 spaces that has to come from somewhere else. If cremation holds at about 70%, that gap is what the plan needs to close.

**1. Ferney Bank (3 hectares, if it is council-owned as I have assumed)**

This is probably your best asset. There is no purchase cost, only the cost of conversion, a ground survey, and the layout. Three hectares laid out as a lawn cemetery could plausibly hold well over 1,600 graves, but get a surveyor's capacity figure before January. Check for planning issues (change of use from leisure), drainage and ground conditions left by the old course, and access. The main risk is lead time: plan on two to four years before the first burials, so this needs to start now.

**2. The reserved plots in the east section**

About 380 empty graves were sold in advance, mostly in the 1960s and 70s, and the rights run for 75 years. Before the council counts them, it needs legal advice on whether unused reserved rights can be reclaimed or the plots reused. That means checking the original deeds, whether the holders are alive or traceable, what notice or refunds are required, and whether any rights have already lapsed. If it works, it gives about 380 spaces with no land cost. If it is contested or slow, it could take years, so treat it as a bonus rather than the core plan.

**3. The north section (1888, full, mostly long grass)**

Reuse of very old graves is sometimes possible, for example where there are no living rights holders, but it is legally complicated and sensitive. A survey would show whether any re-layout is worth pursuing. The Friends of Sorrel Lane already mow the area twice a year, so they could be useful partners. Expect modest gains and a long timetable. This is a supplement, not a foundation.

**4. Buying part or all of the field**

At £1.15 million for 14 hectares, the field is far beyond the £280,000 reserve. You would need to borrow about £870,000 more, plus fees, and you would be buying much more land than 25 years requires. The farmer's all-or-nothing condition makes it a poor fit unless he can be persuaded to sell a smaller parcel, or the council wants to hold land for later expansion. Present it as the fallback if the first two routes fail, or as a longer-term decision.

**5. A shared arrangement with Ashby Combe**

Their extension has had almost no burials since 2017, so it has spare capacity three miles away. A reciprocal or purchase agreement could cover part of the gap quickly and cheaply. The drawbacks are that some residents will not want a burial that far from home, the arrangement needs a formal agreement between the two councils and may have its own charging rules, and it does not meet residents' preference for local burial. It works well as an interim measure while routes 1 and 2 are being arranged.

**Factors that affect every route**

- **Demand:** Cremation has held at about 70% for six years. Plan on that figure and do not rely on it rising, since that is what keeps the 25-year estimate honest.
- **Grave density:** A surveyor can say how many graves per hectare Ferney Bank and the north section could take. The figures above are rough, and this is the first thing to verify.
- **Lead time:** With about four years left, any route that takes three years to deliver has to start soon.

**A suggested package for January**

- Ferney Bank as the main plan: survey, planning advice, and a costed layout.
- The east-section reserved plots: a legal review, with a clear answer on what is possible.
- Ashby Combe as an interim agreement, so there is no gap while the main plan is built.
- The field held as a long-term fallback, not an immediate purchase.

That gives councillors a set of concrete paths to choose between, rather than a straight choice between buying the field and doing nothing.
