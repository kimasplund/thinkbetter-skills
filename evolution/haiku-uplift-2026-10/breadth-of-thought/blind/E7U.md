There are at least five serious options besides the two on the table, and several of them combine well. Your own numbers point to where the problem actually sits, so I'll start there.

## What your figures say

- **Demand is the odd number.** 6 m³ a day for 48 beds is about 125 litres per guest per day. Huts with dry toilets and no showers typically run at a small fraction of that. The 2016 flush toilets and showers are very likely most of your water bill. If you have pre-2016 consumption figures, put them in front of the AGM.
- **The shortage falls when you are half empty.** From mid-August occupancy is about 55%, so demand is roughly 3.3 m³ a day, not 6. The 12 m³ tank then lasts about 3.5 days.
- **Helicopter cost at that level.** At 3.3 m³ a day you need about 4 rotations a day, roughly €4,500 a day. Last year's 19-day gap would have cost about €86,000, more than your €75,000 reserves. At full occupancy it is about €8,250 a day. Flying water in is an emergency backstop, not a plan.
- **The borehole.** €140,000 at a 60% chance of success is about €233,000 per working borehole in expectation. It exceeds your reserves by almost double, and a failed hole leaves you with nothing. If the hydrologist's view is right that the snowfield is shrinking, a borehole near the hut may draw on the same dwindling source.
- **The water exists, at the wrong time.** In late June the spring overflows, and in late August it fails. That is a storage problem as much as a supply problem.

## The options

**1. Cut demand (the cheapest lever)**
- Replace the flush toilets with dry or composting toilets, or low-flush or vacuum units. Add metered or timed showers, push-taps and aerators. Run showers on a token or paid basis.
- Cost: low to moderate, and it needs a quote. Savings could be well over half of daily use.
- Risk: some guests will complain, as they did before 2016. A phased version works: keep the flush toilets in normal weeks, and switch to a "water-saving mode" with showers closed when the spring drops.
- This also shrinks the size and cost of every other option below.

**2. Store the June overflow**
- Add a large buried or covered cistern, or several linked tanks, to catch the surplus that currently floods the path. Fill it in June and July and draw on it in late summer.
- Rough sizing: 19 days at 3.3 m³ is about 63 m³. If option 1 halves use, about 30 m³ covers the gap. Either is far larger than the current 12 m³.
- Drawbacks: the water needs treatment (UV or chlorination) if it sits for weeks. It needs a frost-safe design, though you only operate in season. Earthworks and materials have to be flown in, so get a real quote. The risk is a dry early summer, so size it with margin.

**3. Pipe or pump water from the Grauberg alp**
- Their spring has never failed and the family is friendly. The hut sits 190 m above them over 1.1 km.
- Lifting 6 m³ through 190 m takes only a few kWh a day, even allowing for losses, and your solar array has spare capacity. Energy is not the obstacle.
- The hard parts are the buried or surface pipe (freezing, ground, landslide risk), a legal agreement on water rights, and whether their spring can supply you as well as the dairy. Their yield and their consent are unknown.
- A scaled-down version is a one-off agreement for emergency supply only, using a small pump and a temporary hose line in the weeks you need it.

**4. Improve the existing source and add other sources**
- Have the hydrologist check the spring capture and the pipe for leaks or loss. Look for a better intake point or a second spring or seep higher up. Consider meltwater or snow collection and rainwater from the roof.
- Cost: likely small, and it should come before any €140,000 decision. It won't solve a failure by itself.

**5. Recycle water on site**
- Treat and reuse greywater (shower and basin water) for toilet flushing. That cuts fresh demand by perhaps a third where flush toilets stay.
- A treatment plant at altitude means maintenance and a skilled operator. It makes sense mainly if you keep the flush toilets.

**6. Change how the season runs**
- Set a lower bed cap in late season, or run a reduced service when the spring drops (no showers, limited kitchen use). Add a clause to bookings allowing a water-limited mode, so a shortage does not force a closure and refunds.
- Last year you closed for 11 days and refunded bookings. A reduced service that stays open might cost much less.

**7. Staged borehole**
- Commission a cheaper geological and geophysical survey first, and only drill if the odds improve well beyond 60%. Ask about a staged contract, a partial payment, or a no-water-no-fee arrangement.
- Look for grants from the club's national body, regional funds, or mountain-hut improvement programmes. I can't say what is available to you.

**8. Helicopter supply, but smarter**
- Keep it as the backstop only. Ask for quotes on larger water bags or a bucket-type load and on combined flights with supply runs. The 0.8 m³ per rotation figure may not be fixed.
- With demand cut to roughly half, the same 19-day gap would cost closer to €40,000, which your reserves could cover.

## How they compare

| Option | Upfront cost | Fixes the August gap? | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut demand | Low to moderate | Partly, and it helps everything else | Guest reaction |
| Store overflow | Moderate (needs quotes) | Mostly, if sized well | Treatment, sizing, a dry early summer |
| Grauberg link | Moderate to high | Yes, if their spring can spare it | Agreement, pipe route, their capacity |
| Better capture / new source | Low | Unlikely alone | May find nothing |
| Greywater reuse | Moderate | Partly | Maintenance at altitude |
| Reduced-service plan | Near zero | Avoids closure, not the shortage | Guest expectations |
| Staged borehole | High (survey cheap) | Only if drilling succeeds | Money lost on a dry hole |
| Helicopter backstop | None until used | Yes, at a price | Cost per day of shortage |

## What I'd put to the AGM

Rather than two options, offer three or four packages:

- **A. Minimum:** the helicopter backstop plus a water-limited mode in the booking terms. It costs nothing up front, but a repeat of last August could exhaust reserves.
- **B. Use less and store more (my suggestion):** water-saving fixtures and a reduced-service plan, plus a cistern for the June overflow, with the helicopter as a backstop. This tackles the cause and probably fits within €75,000, subject to quotes.
- **C. Neighbour:** B's demand cuts plus an agreement with Grauberg, either as emergency supply or a permanent line.
- **D. Borehole:** survey first, drill only if the odds improve, and only with grant money behind it.

## What to find out before November

1. Pre-2016 daily consumption, and current use measured per guest.
2. Quotes for a 30 to 60 m³ cistern and for toilet and shower changes.
3. Whether Grauberg's spring can supply you, and whether they would agree in principle.
4. The hydrologist's view on whether a borehole would draw on the same shrinking source.
5. Grant options through the club and the region.
6. Helicopter quotes for larger loads.

My costs here are rough and some are guesses, so the quotes matter. The main point holds: the shortage comes from using 125 litres per guest in the weeks when supply is weakest, and the June overflow is water you already have.
