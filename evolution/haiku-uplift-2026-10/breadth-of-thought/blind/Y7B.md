Buy-or-don't is a false choice, and your own background notes contain several other routes. I've assumed England, a town council acting as burial authority under the Local Authorities' Cemeteries Order 1977, and that your figures are accurate. Legal points below need checking with your solicitor or NALC/SLCC before they go to council.

**Three framing points**

1. **Timing matters more than the choice.** Four years of stock is shorter than the usual lead time for a new burial ground. Surveys, planning, groundwater checks and construction typically take 3 to 5 years from decision. You need a bridging measure whichever way you go.
2. **You need far less land than 14 ha.** 25 years at 95 burials is about 2,400 interments. At a rough 1,000 to 1,500 graves per hectare, that is about 2 to 3 ha. At the farmer's price of about £82,000/ha, 3 ha would cost about £250,000, which your £280,000 reserve covers. The real problem is the all-or-nothing lot. Ask a valuer whether £82,000/ha is far above agricultural value, which would mean the price includes hope value.
3. **Don't count on cremation.** At 75% cremation, burials fall to about 79 a year, so 360 spaces last 4.6 years. At 80%, they last 5.7 years. Even 80% leaves about 1,600 burials over 25 years. An ageing population pushes the other way.

**A. Make existing space last longer**

- **Audit the 360 figure.** If some of the 95 burials are second interments in existing graves, the new-grave rate is lower and 360 lasts longer. Also check whether the 360 spaces are dug to double or triple depth, which changes capacity.
- **Reserved graves in the east section.** About 380 plots have been sold but are unused. That is roughly as many as your remaining stock. Rights granted in the 1960s and 70s run out from about 2035 to 2054, so many return inside your 25 years if unused and not renewed. You could also write to the holders or their heirs and offer buy-backs or refunds, which can release plots sooner. Check the deeds, because earlier sales may carry different terms.
- **Stop selling in advance, or limit it.** Cap pre-purchase at a few plots per family, or charge more for it.
- **Re-use of the north section.** It is called full, but its 75-year rights expired long ago unless renewed. Where graves were dug deep enough to leave the required cover above older coffins, new interments may be possible. Disturbing remains is restricted, and any consecrated ground needs a faculty. The Friends group and heritage and biodiversity interests need consulting. Commission a grave-depth survey before assuming any capacity.
- **Smaller plots for cremated remains.** Interments of cremated remains take much less space. Use odd corners and the north section's grass.
- **Pricing.** Check non-resident fees and multi-depth pricing, which encourages family graves. The aim is to manage demand, not to cut it.

**B. Use land you already own**

- **Ferney Bank (3 ha).** On the rule of thumb above, it could cover the whole 25 years, or about half of it if you use only part and keep the rest as open space. Check groundwater (Environment Agency vulnerability maps and distances to watercourses and boreholes), soil and drainage, access, planning use class, and any recreation-land appropriation procedure, covenants or Fields in Trust protection. A feasibility survey would cost a modest five-figure sum. It is a satellite site, so check distance and local feeling.
- **Natural or woodland burial** on part of it, which costs less to lay out but uses more land per burial.

**C. Partner**

- **Ashby Combe.** It has had hardly any burials since 2017, three miles away. Options include a service agreement to buy burial capacity, a joint committee, or buying land or plots from them. Find out why it is empty: price, location, or something wrong with the ground. This is also cheap insurance during the gap in A.
- **Other burial providers.** Ask the church, neighbouring parishes and the principal authority about spare capacity or suitable land.

**D. Acquire land**

- **Buy the whole 14 ha and borrow.** About £870,000 beyond the reserve. At 5% over 25 years, that is roughly £60,000 a year, or about £4 to £5 per resident. Borrowing needs approval. Offset it by selling or leasing the surplus, with the planning, covenant and public-perception risks that brings.
- **Whole purchase with a partner.** A community land trust, wildlife trust or the principal authority could take the 11 ha you don't need. Aim for a back-to-back deal.
- **Negotiate again.** Offer a phased or option purchase, a right of first refusal, or a lease of 3 ha with an option for more. The farmer may accept if the money is secure.
- **Land swap** involving Ferney Bank. Ferney Bank and the field are partly substitutes, so decide them together. Using one frees or removes the need for the other.
- **Compulsory purchase** is a last resort and slow, but it gives leverage. It would cover only what you need.
- **Look for other sites.** Ask the planning authority about other land, and check the local plan.

**E. Do nothing**

After about four years you stop burials. Residents travel, and income of about £138,000 a year from grants of right (95 x £1,450) disappears. Maintenance continues indefinitely. This is the baseline to show council.

**What I'd bring in January**

A staged package: do A now (audit, depth survey, reserved-grave review, stop advance sales), start the Ferney Bank feasibility study, open talks with Ashby Combe, and ask the farmer about a part-purchase or option. Council could then pick a preferred route in the spring with real data. Keep in mind that 25 years is short for a cemetery, which has a perpetual obligation. A smaller site solves your term but passes the problem on, so consider an option on further land.

**Data to gather before the meeting:** new graves versus reopenings in the last five years; how many of the 380 reserved plots are still held and on what terms; depth capacity of the north section; Ferney Bank's legal status and ground conditions; Ashby Combe's capacity, fees and willingness; a valuation of the field.
