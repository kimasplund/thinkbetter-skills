There are more than two choices, and some of them look better than either proposal. First, some numbers that shape everything else.

**What the numbers say**

- At full occupancy you use about 125 litres per guest per day (6 m³ / 48). Huts with dry toilets and no showers typically run at 20-40 litres. The 2016 flush toilets and showers are almost certainly most of your demand.
- Flying in full-demand water costs about 7.5 rotations a day, roughly €8,250 a day. The 19-day outage in August would have cost about €157,000 at full occupancy, or about €86,000 at the 55% mid-August level. That is more than your reserves, so helicopter-only is not a plan for a recurring problem.
- Your tank holds 12 m³, which is about two days at full use.
- The borehole costs €140,000 against €75,000 in reserves, with a 60% chance of success. The expected cost per working borehole is around €230,000. A well drilled into the same mountain may also weaken in the same dry spells as the spring.
- If the spring is dwindling rather than stopping, the real gap in a bad August may be smaller than the total demand. This is worth checking against last year's flow records.

**The options**

1. **Cut demand (cheapest, and it multiplies every other option).** Options range from showers on tokens or timers, to waterless or low-flush toilets, to returning to dry toilets in the second half of the season. At 40 litres per guest per day, full-house demand falls from 6 m³ to about 1.9 m³, and at 55% occupancy to about 1 m³. A 19-day shortfall then needs about 20 m³ rather than 60-110 m³. The cost is guest comfort, and you installed these facilities because guests complained, so it needs a clear explanation. Members may accept it more readily if it is framed as a drought measure that switches on only when the spring drops.

2. **A "dry mode" operating plan instead of closing.** When the spring fails, stay open on drinking and cooking water only (about 5 litres per person per day). At 55% occupancy that is roughly 130 litres a day, so one helicopter rotation lasts about six days. A 19-day outage then costs about 3-4 rotations, or €3,300-4,400, instead of closing for 11 days and refunding bookings. Guests would need to be told in the booking conditions. It also lets you stay open for the guided groups that were cancelled.

3. **More storage, filled from the June overflow.** In late June the spring produces far more than you can use. Extra cisterns, say 20-30 m³, filled then and held for August would bridge most or all of a 19-day outage once demand is cut as in options 1 and 2. Costs depend on tank type and on getting materials up by helicopter, so you would need quotes. The limits are frost protection, water quality over weeks of storage (some treatment will be needed), and the fact that the tanks must be full when the spring fails.

4. **Pipe or pump water from the Grauberg alp.** The alp is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower, so water would have to be lifted. The lift itself is cheap in energy, only a few kWh a day for 6 m³, and your solar array has spare capacity. The costs are the pipeline, the pump station, and keeping it frost-safe and unharmed by snow, stock and rockfall. Their spring has never failed and the family is friendly, but you would need a formal agreement covering water rights, the dairy's own needs, and what happens in a very dry year. Treat this as an option to cost out properly, not yet a recommendation.

5. **Capture more water higher up.** The spring is 300 m above the hut and fed by a shrinking snowfield. A hydrologist could look at a second catchment, a deeper or better-protected spring capture, meltwater from other snow patches, or rainwater collection from the hut roof. Roof collection will not be enough alone but can contribute to storage.

6. **Haul water by helicopter, but on a budget.** This is the committee's second proposal, in a smaller form. If demand is cut and storage is added, helicopter flights become a top-up for the worst weeks rather than the main supply. You could also put aside a ring-fenced "water reserve" of roughly €10,000-15,000 a year, enough for about 10-14 rotations.

7. **Drill the borehole, but only in stages.** Start with a cheaper test, such as a geophysical survey or a smaller pilot hole. Ask the geologist whether the 60% figure refers to any water or to enough water, and what yield is expected. Grant funding from the alpine association or a regional body may also exist for water security in mountain huts. Without funding, €140,000 more than uses up your reserves.

8. **Change the season.** From mid-August, occupancy is already about 55% because the glacier route is bad. Closing about 10 September, or opening a week later in late June when the overflow floods the path, would put the fewest guests in the driest weeks. This costs income, so it is a fallback, not a first step.

**Which combinations make sense**

- **Cheapest and fastest:** options 1, 2 and 6. Cut demand, adopt a dry-mode plan, and keep a helicopter budget. Together these could cover a 19-day outage for under about €5,000, and none needs a big capital decision.
- **Most resilient without drilling:** add 3 and 4, with the Grauberg line costed. Storage plus a second source gives you two independent safeguards.
- **Highest cost and risk:** option 7 on its own. At 40% odds of an expensive dry hole and a price above your reserves, it should be the last thing you commit to.

**What I would take to the AGM**

Put forward a first stage of options 1, 2 and 3, plus a hydrologist's costed report on options 4 and 5, and a staged test of the borehole under option 7. Suggested decision points:

1. Approve the demand-cutting and dry-mode plan now, for the 2027 season.
2. Commission quotes for storage and for the Grauberg connection, with a decision at a later meeting.
3. Hold the borehole until the quotes and grant options are known.

**What I would still need to know**

- Last August's actual flow readings, and how low the spring got.
- The cost of extra tanks and of a pipeline to the alp.
- Whether the Grauberg family would share water, and on what terms.
- The geologist's expected yield, not just the success probability.
- Whether the club's rules or guest contracts allow reduced service.
