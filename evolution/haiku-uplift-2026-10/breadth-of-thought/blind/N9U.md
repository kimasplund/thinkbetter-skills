Here are the options I see, with rough numbers. I've made some assumptions where the brief is silent, so please check them.

**The numbers that drive the choice**

- Your tank (12 m³) covers about 2 days at full occupancy (6 m³/day). A 19-day outage like last August needs about 114 m³ at full occupancy.
- From mid-August you run at about 55%, so roughly 3.3 m³/day. A 19-day gap then needs about 63 m³.
- Helicopter water costs about €1,375 per m³ (€1,100 for 0.8 m³). Covering 6 m³/day by air would cost about €8,250 a day. Covering 3.3 m³/day would cost about €4,550 a day. Your €75,000 reserve buys roughly 55 m³ by air, which is less than one bad August.
- The borehole costs €140,000, which is almost double your reserves. It has a 60% chance of success, so there is a 40% chance you pay for it and get nothing.

**Options**

1. **Pipe water from the Grauberg spring.** It is 1.1 km away and 190 m lower, and its spring has never failed. Your solar surplus could run a pump, and the lift is modest. This is probably the strongest option, but it depends on a deal with the farm. You would need an agreement on water rights, a fair price or shared-cost arrangement, and a water test, since it is a dairy catchment. Ask for a pipe and pump quote, and check whether the farm's own summer demand leaves enough spare flow.

2. **Expand storage and capture the June surplus.** The spring floods the path in late June, so that water is currently lost. A larger tank, or several linked tanks, could catch it. Even a tank holding 40 to 60 m³ would cover most of a dry spell at the 55% occupancy you already run in late summer. Get quotes for storage size and cost, and check how much of the June overflow can actually be routed in.

3. **Cut demand in late summer.** The two showers and the flush toilets are probably your largest water uses. Options include:
   - Limiting showers or running them on a timer when the tank is low.
   - Switching back to dry or composting toilets for the late-season months.
   - Installing low-flow fittings and greywater reuse for washing.
   Install a meter first, so you know the real split of usage.

4. **Water-based booking rules.** Set a tank-level trigger, for example "if the tank drops below X m³, cap or close bookings." This ties occupancy to supply, rather than cancelling refunded bookings after the fact. Your mid-August drop in occupancy already helps with this.

5. **Borehole, but later and only as a fallback.** Commission a hydrogeological survey first, which is much cheaper than drilling. Be aware that if the snowfield shrinks, groundwater recharge may also decline, so a borehole's reliability over the long term is uncertain. Drilling should only follow if options 1 to 3 fall short.

6. **Helicopter top-up as a short-term backstop.** This is expensive, at about €1,375 per m³, but it is useful as an emergency measure. Keep it as a contingency, not a plan. Ask whether the helicopter operator offers a standby contract that is cheaper than ad hoc flights.

7. **Insurance or a closure fund.** Consider business-interruption cover, or set aside a fixed part of the reserve for refunds and cancelled groups. This does not fix the water problem, but it limits the financial hit while you fix it.

8. **Monitoring.** Fit a simple flow gauge on the spring and log it. That gives the committee early warning and better data for the decision.

**A combination I'd suggest for the AGM**

- Start the Grauberg pipeline talks now. Get a pipe and pump quote, and a water test.
- Fund a storage expansion to catch the June overflow.
- Adopt late-season demand cuts and a tank-level booking trigger straight away. These cost little.
- Order the hydrogeological survey, and hold the borehole decision until the other options have been tested.
- Keep helicopter water as the last-resort backstop.

**Information to gather before November**

- The cost and feasibility of the Grauberg pipe and pump.
- Whether the farm can share water, and at what price.
- Storage quotes, and how much June overflow can be captured.
- Metered water use by area, including the showers and toilets.
- The hydrologist's view on how the snowfield decline will affect the borehole.

These are assumptions, so the quotes will change the picture. The biggest unknown is whether the Grauberg spring can be shared, because that could make the other options unnecessary.
