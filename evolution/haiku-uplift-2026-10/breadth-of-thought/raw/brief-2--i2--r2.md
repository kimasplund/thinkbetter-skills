There are far more than two options, and several of them use land or graves you already have. Here is the full range, grouped by where the space comes from, followed by what I would put in front of council.

**The size of the problem**

At 95 burials a year, 25 years means roughly 2,400 grave spaces. At about 1,000 graves per hectare once paths, parking and landscaping are allowed for, that is 2.5 to 3 hectares. The field is 14 hectares, so you would be buying about five times what the cemetery needs. At £1.15m that works out to about £82,000 a hectare. That is much higher than typical farmland prices, so an independent valuation matters.

Also, 95 new rights at £1,450 would bring in up to about £138,000 a year if every burial needed a new right. That is probably an overstatement, since some burials go into existing graves, but it is still a large income stream, and it changes how any capital cost should be judged. Also, a new site takes time: acquisition, planning, ground-water assessment and groundworks will probably take 2 to 3 years of your 4. The decision cannot wait long after January.

**A. Get more out of what the council already holds**

1. **Recover the unused reserved graves in the east section.** About 380 plots sold in advance in the 1960s and 70s are still empty. That is roughly four more years of supply, if they are not already in your 360 (I have assumed they are not, so check with the bereavement officer). A 75-year right granted in 1960 ends in 2035, and one granted in 1979 ends in 2054, so most will expire within your 25-year window.
   - Before expiry you can only get them back by agreement. Trace the holders or their heirs and offer a refund or a surrender.
   - Check your register and your solicitor's view on what notice procedure your cemetery rules allow. Many holders will have died, and some heirs will want the plots.
   - Cost is low and it is quick. It is not a long-term answer by itself.

2. **Survey the north section and the east section for hidden capacity.** The north section is full of graves but mostly long grass. Old graves were often dug deep enough for a second or third burial, and many have unused interments. There may also be gaps, margins and path areas. A proper survey could add years of space.
   - Reusing old graves is legally restricted outside London, so take advice before counting on it.
   - The Friends of Sorrel Lane would need to be involved early, since they care for that ground.

3. **Make the remaining space go further.** Offer double-depth graves, tighter layout in new rows, and dedicated plots for cremated remains. Ashes plots take very little room, and since cremation is at 70% they are worth providing. This does not change the number of burials, but it stops ashes from competing for full-size plots.

**B. Use land the council already owns**

4. **Ferney Bank, 3 hectares.** This is the option I would look at first. It is already yours, so there is no purchase price, and it is about the right size for 25 years.
   - It is the right size only if the usable area is close to 3 ha. Buildings, boundaries and wet ground reduce it.
   - Check the title for restrictions, any grant clawback, and whether it has to be kept as public open space.
   - You will need a ground-water and drainage assessment (Environment Agency rules on burial sites near water apply), planning permission for change of use, and likely a public notice about appropriating open space.
   - Ask whether it is close enough to Sorrel Lane for families, and whether access and parking are workable.

**C. Buy land**

5. **Buy the whole field and sell or lease the surplus.** Keep roughly 3 ha, then sell the remaining 11 or so, or lease it back to the farmer, or use it for something that pays or serves the town (allotments, a natural burial area, community woodland).
   - This turns a £1.15m purchase into a smaller net cost, but only if the surplus sells for a decent price, and £82,000 a hectare is unlikely to be recovered on farmland.
   - Your £280,000 reserve leaves about £870,000 to borrow. At an assumed 5% over 25 years that is about £62,000 a year. That is well under the possible burial income, but it needs borrowing approval and precept impact to be worked out, and the interest rate is my assumption.

6. **Negotiate a part sale.** The farmer says all 14 ha or nothing, but that is an opening position. Try an option to buy 3 ha now with the rest later, a phased purchase, or a price that reflects a smaller sale. Around 3 ha at the asking rate would be about £250,000, which your reserve covers. A farmer who wants to sell may accept this if the alternative is a compulsory purchase or no sale at all.

7. **Buy jointly with a partner who takes the rest.** A wildlife trust, community land trust, the district council or a neighbouring council could buy the surplus at the same time, with back-to-back contracts so the farmer still gets one price for one lot.

8. **Ask the district council about compulsory purchase.** As a last resort, burial ground provision is a recognised ground for acquiring land compulsorily. I am not certain of the exact route for a town council, so ask your solicitor. Its main value is as leverage in the negotiation in option 6.

**D. Use someone else's capacity**

9. **Make an agreement with Ashby Combe.** Their 2017 extension has had hardly any burials, and it is three miles away. A deal could give Wickham Lees residents access at resident rates, perhaps with a payment from you, or a joint arrangement. First find out why it is so empty: low demand, price, poor location or something wrong with the ground. If the reason is a problem, it will not solve yours. Also check what rates they would charge your residents.

10. **Private, church or natural burial providers.** A privately run natural burial ground nearby, perhaps on the farmer's own land, would take some demand. This costs the council nothing, but it also loses control of price and income.

**E. The baseline**

11. **Do nothing, and let burials end at Sorrel Lane in about four years.** Residents would be buried elsewhere, probably at higher cost and further away, and the council would lose up to roughly £138,000 a year in fee income plus a local service. Council should see this as a real option with a real price, not only as the thing to avoid.

**How these fit together**

Rather than choosing one, I would suggest a combination.

| Step | What it buys | Cost and risk |
|---|---|---|
| Recover reserved graves, survey north and east sections (1-3) | Possibly 4-8 years | Low cost, uncertain yield |
| Ashby Combe agreement (9) | Overflow capacity as a bridge | Low, depends on why it is empty |
| Ferney Bank (4) | About 25 years if usable area is right | Planning and ground-water risk |
| Part purchase or joint purchase of the field (6, 7) | Fallback if Ferney Bank fails | Needs negotiation |
| Whole field with resale (5) | Fallback of last resort | Largest financial risk |

The 70% cremation figure has been flat for six years, so council should not plan on it falling.

**What to find out before January**

- How many of the 380 reserved plots are still held, and what your cemetery rules allow.
- The usable area and ground conditions at Ferney Bank, and whether the title has restrictions.
- Why Ashby Combe is so quiet, and what they would charge.
- An independent valuation of the field and its agricultural value.
- What the district council says about borrowing and about compulsory purchase.

If Ferney Bank passes those checks, council can go in with a plan that uses what it already owns and keeps the field purchase as a backup. If it fails, the part-purchase route gives a figure within your reserve to negotiate toward.
