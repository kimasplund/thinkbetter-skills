There are more options than "buy the field or don't". Your own background has at least three that probably get less attention than they deserve: the council's own 3 hectares at Ferney Bank, the 380 unused reserved graves, and partnering with Ashby Combe. Below is a menu you could take to council, followed by a suggested way to frame it.

I'm assuming you're an English parish or town council. I haven't checked legal points, so have your solicitor, NALC or the county association confirm them before January. Where I give figures, they are rough.

**First, size the need**

- 95 burials a year over 25 years is about 2,400 graves. Allow 10 to 20% for population growth and ageing, so plan for roughly 2,700.
- You have 360 spaces, which is about 3.8 years. The practical deadline is therefore around 2029 to 2030. Land acquisition, planning, drainage and layout usually take 2 to 3 years, so a decision is needed in 2026/27.
- Planning figure: roughly 1,000 to 1,500 graves per hectare, net of paths. The 25-year need is therefore about 2 to 3 hectares. The 14-hectare field is four to five times more than you need, which is why the all-or-nothing lot is such a poor fit. It works out at about £82,000 per hectare, so ask the district valuer what agricultural value is and how much of the price is "hope value".
- Check whether the 360 figure already counts additional interments in existing family graves.

**The options**

1. **Buy the whole field (£1.15m).**
   - The gap over your £280,000 reserve is about £870,000.
   - At roughly 5% over 40 years, a loan costs around £50,000 a year. If you have about 5,500 to 6,000 Band D equivalents, that is on the order of £8 to £9 a year per household.
   - Borrowing needs approval, and you would need to confirm the PWLB terms.
   - It is the only option that settles the question for a century, but it is far more land than you need. Whether to buy at all should depend on whether the cheaper options below can't deliver.

2. **Buy the field, but make it work harder.** These are variants of option 1.
   - Buy it all, then lease most of it back to the farmer, or sell off the surplus with covenants. Only 2 to 3 hectares would be laid out for burial, and the rest would produce rent or sale income.
   - Make a counter-offer for a split. The farmer's "one lot" position is a negotiating stance, not a fact. Try a smaller parcel, a purchase option with phased drawdown, or a right of first refusal on the remainder.
   - Partner with someone who wants the rest, such as the district council or a housing or nature body.
   - Buy a smaller parcel by agreement, with compulsory purchase as a last resort, which would be a conversation for the principal authority.

3. **Use Ferney Bank (3 hectares, already yours).**
   - This is probably the strongest option on paper. Three hectares covers the whole 25-year need on the numbers above, and you avoid the purchase cost entirely.
   - Things to check: whether it is held as open space (appropriation and public notice requirements), planning permission for change of use, an Environment Agency groundwater and hydrogeology assessment, soil and drainage (a former golf course may be fertilised and drained), access and parking, the distance from Sorrel Lane, and any restrictive covenants or grant conditions.
   - Costs: layout, roads, fencing and a chapel or shelter. Budget a fraction of the field price, but get a proper quote.
   - It would be a second site rather than an extension, so staff, maintenance and the distance between sites all matter.

4. **Recover and reuse land inside Sorrel Lane.**
   - **The 380 reserved east-section plots.**
     - Rights granted in the 1960s and 70s for 75 years run out between roughly 2035 and 2050, so many will return to you inside your 25-year window. You can't take them back early.
     - You can trace the owners or heirs and offer to buy back the rights, or ask them to surrender them for a refund. Even a partial response helps. If a third of the plots came back, that is over 120 graves, more than a year of supply.
     - Before any buyback, check what the records show and what the 1977 Order and your own regulations say.
   - **The north section.** It has been "full" for a century and is mostly long grass. Commission a survey, with ground-penetrating radar if needed, to find unused space such as gaps, path margins and unrecorded plots. Check whether rights have expired and whether any lawful reuse is possible. Reuse of old graves outside London is legally limited, so get advice before promising anything.
   - **Existing graves.** Check for unused depth or width in graves that could take more interments with the owner's consent.

5. **Get more burials out of the land you have, or will have.**
   - Lay out new areas at higher density: double or triple depth, narrower plots, lawn sections.
   - Offer ashes plots, which take about one-tenth of the space, and cremated-remains burial within existing graves.
   - Stop selling further advance reservations, or limit them to a fixed term.
   - This is a multiplier on any option above, not a standalone answer.

6. **Partner with Ashby Combe.**
   - Their 2017 extension has barely been used.
   - Find out why. It could be low local demand, a poor site, or pricing, and the answer tells you whether it would work for you.
   - Possible forms: a formal agreement for your residents to use it at resident rates, buying a block of plots, or a joint burial committee.
   - It is cheap and fast. The downsides are that it is three miles away, a Wickham Lees funeral would be outside Wickham Lees, and you depend on their council and regulations. It is best as a bridge or fallback rather than the whole answer.

7. **Natural or green burial.** Woodland or meadow burial can be run on part of Ferney Bank or a small parcel of the field. It tends to be lower-density and lower-cost to maintain, and it may appeal to people choosing cremation. It adds a different offer, not much capacity.

8. **Manage demand and price.**
   - Review fees. £1,450 for 75 years may be well below the full cost of providing the land and its upkeep.
   - Charge a higher non-resident fee.
   - Treat the cremation rate as a risk, not a plan. It has been flat at about 70% for six years, so build the plan on 95 burials a year.
   - Shorter terms for new grants, such as 25 to 50 years, would eventually allow reuse but won't help within your window.

9. **Hand the problem over or wind down.**
   - Ask the principal authority what happens if you stop providing burials.
   - This is the do-nothing floor. Councillors should see it spelled out, including what residents would pay elsewhere.

**Suggested framing for January**

No single option is likely to carry the whole 25 years, so a combination is more realistic.

- **Now:**
  - Survey Sorrel Lane for hidden capacity.
  - Start tracing owners of the reserved plots.
  - Ask Ashby Combe what their demand and terms look like.
  - Do the feasibility work on Ferney Bank.
- **In parallel:**
  - Open talks with the farmer about a smaller parcel or an option, without making an offer.
  - Get a valuation.
  - Price the borrowing for the whole field so councillors can see the per-household cost.
- **Fallback:** Ashby Combe as a stopgap if nothing is ready by about 2029.
- **Decision for council:** choose the primary route in 2026/27, and ask for budget for the feasibility and survey work.
