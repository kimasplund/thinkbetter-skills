Here are more options than the two the committee has, with rough numbers so members can compare them. Figures are my estimates from your brief, not quotes, and I've flagged where you need a real quote.

**Key numbers**
- Tank: 12 m³. At full occupancy (about 6 m³/day) that is only 2 days of water.
- The August outage lasted 19 days. At full occupancy that is about 114 m³, and at 55% occupancy about 63 m³.
- Helicopter water costs about €1,375 per m³ (€1,100 for 0.8 m³). Covering a full day at full occupancy by air would be about 7.5 rotations, roughly €8,250 a day. Covering 19 days this way would cost about €157,000, more than the borehole.
- The borehole (€140,000) is more than your €75,000 in reserves. Any option that uses it needs an extra €65,000 or more from a loan, a levy, or fundraising. Also, with a 60% chance of success, you have a 40% chance of paying €140,000 and getting no water.

**Options**

1. **Use the late-June overflow.** The spring runs hardest in late June, before you open on 1 July, and that water is now lost to the path. A large storage tank (or a second tank) filled during June could carry you through a dry August. Only about 63 to 114 m³ of storage is needed, and tanks cost far less than a borehole. Needs: a quote, a site check, and a measurement of how much June overflow is actually available.

2. **Pipe water from the Grauberg spring.** It is 1.1 km away, 190 m lower, and has never failed. You would need a pump and a pipeline up the valley. The lift is small: about 6 m³/day raised 190 m needs only a few kWh of pumping energy a day, which your spare solar capacity could probably supply. The main cost is the pipeline, and the main risk is the agreement: you would need the farm family's consent, a water-supply contract, and a plan for who pays for maintenance. Needs: a survey, a pipeline quote, and a conversation with the family.

3. **Cut demand in dry years.** Showers and flush toilets use the most water. Options include time-limited showers, a water-saving rule for guests, or in extreme cases switching the flush toilets off during a shortage. This is cheap and reversible, and it works alongside options 1 and 2.

4. **Shorten the season or close in dry weather.** Occupancy already falls to about 55% from mid-August, so closing earlier (for example from late August) costs fewer bookings than it used to. You could also set a rule: when the tank drops below a set level, bookings stop automatically, with refunds and rebooking handled in advance. This matches the pattern the hydrologist describes.

5. **Helicopter water as a backup only.** Keep it for emergencies, with a clear trigger (for example, the tank below 4 m³) and a budget (for example €15,000 a year). Flying water is expensive per litre, so it works as insurance, not a long-term supply.

6. **Borehole.** Keep it as a last resort. Before you commit, ask the geologist for the cost and success chance of a smaller test well, and whether the €140,000 includes pumping, power and a connection to the existing system.

**How the options combine**

A phased plan could work well:
- **Now (low cost):** demand cuts (option 3), a booking-closure rule (option 4), and a backup helicopter budget (option 5).
- **Before the next season:** storage for June overflow (option 1), plus a survey and talks with the Grauberg family (option 2).
- **Only if both fail:** the borehole (option 6), with money raised first.

**Assumptions to check**
- The June overflow volume is large enough to fill a new tank. The hydrologist should confirm this.
- The Grauberg spring can spare about 6 m³/day for the hut. This needs a measurement and an agreement.
- The solar array has enough spare capacity to run a pump. The installer should confirm this.

For the AGM, the simplest framing is: a low-cost plan now (options 3 to 5), a storage and pipeline study this year (options 1 and 2), and the borehole held in reserve with its risks stated clearly.
