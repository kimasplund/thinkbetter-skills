You have more than two real options. Here are five that are worth putting to members, followed by a few that I would drop before the AGM.

**Facts the options turn on**

- Full occupancy uses about 6 m³ a day. The tank holds 12 m³, which is two days at full use.
- A 19-day dry spell at full use needs about 114 m³. At the mid-August occupancy of about 55%, it is closer to 63 m³.
- A helicopter rotation carries 0.8 m³ for €1,100, which is roughly €1,375 per cubic metre.
- Late June surplus is large enough to flood the path. Nobody has measured how much of it goes to waste.

**1. Bigger storage, filled during the late-June surplus**

Build one or more additional cisterns and fill them from the spring while it runs hard in June. This targets the failure you actually had: a dry spell in late summer. It needs no new source and no drilling.

- Trade-off: it only helps if the storage is large enough. Covering the August dry spell means roughly 60 to 115 m³, several times the current tank.
- Unknown: the cost of the tanks and their site. Get quotes, and have someone measure the June overflow rate before committing.
- Works alongside any other option.

**2. Reduce water use, and keep the season short**

The biggest single user is almost certainly the flush toilets, which you installed in 2016. Returning to dry or composting toilets, rationing showers, and closing the hut from mid-August when the spring is low would each cut demand. The late-season closure is already close to what happens naturally.

- Trade-off: reverting the toilets undoes a guest-comfort upgrade, and guests may be unhappy about it.
- Unknown: how much of the 6 m³ is toilets versus showers and kitchen. Measure it before deciding. The savings could be a large share of the problem.

**3. A standby water supply from the Grauberg alp**

The alp is 1.1 km down the valley and 190 m lower. Its spring has never failed, and it is a dairy with friendly owners. Agree a supply arrangement with the family, and build a pipe, or a hose route, that can carry water up when the hut's own spring fails. Solar power could run the pump.

- Trade-off: pumping 190 m uphill needs a pump that can run on solar, and the pipeline could be expensive over rough ground. The alp also needs its own water for the dairy, so the arrangement must be a fair, limited one.
- Unknown: the alp's spare capacity, the cost of the line, and who owns the water rights. Ask before the AGM.

**4. Drill a borehole (the committee's first proposal)**

- Trade-off: €140,000 is more than the €75,000 in reserves, so it would need €65,000 of new money. The geologist puts the chance of usable water at about 60%, so there is roughly a 40% chance of paying for a hole that gives nothing. Pumping also needs power.
- If you choose it, drill only after the cheaper options are in place, and ask the geologist for a test-hole plan that limits the cost of a dry result.

**5. Keep the helicopter, but as an emergency tool only**

- Trade-off: at about €1,375 per cubic metre, covering a whole 19-day dry spell by air would cost roughly €87,000 to €157,000, more than the reserves. It also depends on flying weather.
- Good as a backstop for a short, sudden shortage, not as the plan.

**Options I would drop**

- Moving the hut or building a new source higher up. The snowfield feeding the spring is shrinking, so a higher source is unlikely to be more reliable.
- Rainwater collection from the roof. It could help a little, but late-summer rain in the mountains is unreliable and the volume would be small against 6 m³ a day.

**How the options compare**

| Option | Upfront cost | Covers dry spells | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Bigger storage | Unknown until quoted | Yes, if large enough | Surplus may be too small to fill it |
| 2. Less water use | Low | Partly | Guest reaction to toilet change |
| 3. Grauberg standby | Unknown until quoted | Yes | Agreement and pumping |
| 4. Borehole | €140,000 | Only if it strikes water | About 40% chance of nothing |
| 5. Helicopter | About €1,100 per rotation | Expensive, weather-bound | Cost and flying conditions |

**What I would put to members**

1. Primary: options 2 and 3 together. Both are cheap or shared, and the Grauberg standby gives you a supply that has never failed.
2. Alongside that: option 1, sized after measuring the June surplus.
3. Hold the borehole back until those are costed, and then decide whether it is worth the remaining money.
4. Keep the helicopter as the emergency fallback.

Before November, the three things to find out are the June overflow rate, the true split of water use between toilets, showers and kitchen, and the Grauberg family's spare capacity and terms. Those three answers would settle most of the decision.
