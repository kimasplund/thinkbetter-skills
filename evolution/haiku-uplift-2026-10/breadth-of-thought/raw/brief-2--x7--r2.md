Here is a set of realistic options, grouped by how much they change the position. I have assumed the figures you gave are right, and I have flagged where a legal or ground check is needed before council relies on an option.

**The numbers first**

- 360 spaces at 95 burials a year is about 3.8 years, so you have roughly four years of stock at the current rate, not 25.
- The field costs £1.15m against a £280k reserve, leaving a gap of about £870k before fees, survey, legal and planning costs.
- Cremation at about 70% for six years is stable. Do not plan on it falling, and do not plan on a rise either.

So the real question is not whether to buy the field, but how to stretch the next four years and then bridge into a long-term answer over the 25-year horizon.

**Options that use land you already own or control**

1. **Reclaim the empty reserved graves at Sorrel Lane East (about 380 plots).** This is probably your largest single resource. These were sold as exclusive rights of burial for 75 years, so they are not the council's to reassign just because they are empty. Check the deeds and register for each plot: which were sold in the 1960s and 70s, whether any have lapsed or are close to lapsing, and whether the holders or heirs are traceable. Options include a surrender scheme (offering a refund or a credit, or a discount on a new purchase), a reduced fee for early surrender, and reclaiming lapsed rights where the law allows. Get legal advice before promising anything to families.

2. **Reuse the north section (full since 1888, now mostly long grass).** Re-use of old graves is possible in some cases, but the rules depend on the age of the grave, the type of right and local byelaws, so this needs legal confirmation. Physical options include re-opening and deepening old grave spaces where ground conditions allow, and lifting and re-using in a managed way. Work with the Friends of Sorrel Lane, who already know the ground. Be sensitive about memorials and heritage.

3. **Increase density in existing sections.** Use shallower or deeper grave layouts, double or triple interments where the rights allow, and tighter row spacing in newer areas. Each of these adds spaces without new land. Measure how many extra spaces each change gives before you present it.

4. **Cremated remains space.** If cremation stays near 70%, a columbarium, a memorial wall, or ash plots take far less land per funeral and can be sold at a lower fee. This will not solve the whole gap, but it reduces the pressure on full burial plots.

5. **Natural or woodland burial in a designated area of Sorrel Lane or Ferney Bank.** These sell well, need less maintenance, and suit meadow areas such as the north section if the Friends are involved.

**Options that use other land**

6. **Ferney Bank pitch-and-putt (3 ha, closed last year).** This is the most likely new-ground option if the council owns it. Burial use needs a planning decision, a groundwater and ground-suitability check (the Environment Agency will expect this for a cemetery), drainage and access work, and a feasibility report. It is a multi-year project, so it should start soon even though it will not help in four years. Also consider whether the pitch-and-putt has a sporting or community value that the council would need to replace.

7. **Share capacity with Ashby Combe.** Their extension has had very few burials since 2017, so they may have years of spare capacity. Options include an agreement for Wickham Lees residents to use Ashby Combe at a set fee, a joint arrangement under a cemetery agreement, or a transfer of rights. Check residence rules and whether the two councils' fees and procedures align. This is the cheapest option to test and could buy time at low cost, but it puts residents a few miles further away, so the council must weigh that.

8. **Buy part of the field.** Ask the farmer whether he would sell a smaller parcel, for example 3 to 4 hectares next to the existing boundary, rather than all 14. This is an open question and needs a valuation. Even a small parcel at a lower price could give 25 years of stock at your burial rate, and it would be a much smaller call on the reserve.

9. **Buy the full field, with a funding plan.** This is still possible but would need external funding, borrowing (check the council's borrowing limits and the cost of repayment), a phased purchase, or a partial sale of other council land. Present it as an option with a clear cost, not as the default.

**Options that change the fee model**

10. **Review the £1,450 exclusive right fee.** Options include a higher fee for new grave spaces, a lower fee for reuse or shared capacity, and a fee structure that reflects the true cost of new land. Compare with neighbouring councils before changing it. Note that the rights run for 75 years, so the fee is effectively a long-term commitment.

**What to bring to council in January**

- A year-by-year demand and stock table for 25 years, showing stock running out under the current pattern and under each option.
- A short list of two or three options to take forward, with cost, time, legal risk and resident impact for each.
- A clear recommendation: for example, test the Ashby Combe and reserved-grave surrender options now, start the Ferney Bank feasibility work, and treat the field as a last resort or a partial purchase.
- The legal and ground checks you still need, with a rough date for each.

**Assumptions I have made**

- The rights you describe are exclusive rights of burial for 75 years, and the council holds the records for them.
- The Ferney Bank land is in council ownership and could in principle take burials.
- Ashby Combe's spare capacity is real and they are willing to talk.
- The legal position on reusing old graves and surrendering reserved rights needs confirming with your solicitor or the relevant regulator before you rely on it.
