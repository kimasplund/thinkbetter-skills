Short answer: each of them is right about one thing and wrong about another, and the vote as framed (close or keep) is the wrong choice. Your own facts point to a third option.

**Where Dr Halloran is right**

Overnight work is costing you vets. Three exits in 18 months, all citing nights, is not noise. But look at what they were objecting to. Every one of your 11 small-animal vets does one or two nights a month on top of a full day rota, and you turned down an experienced emergency vet because she would only work nights. The policy was meant to be fair to everyone. In practice it has made nights a tax on every day-practice vet, while the one person who wanted the work was turned away. The thing driving people out is how the rota is built, and closing the service is only one way to fix that.

**Where Dr Halloran is wrong**

She is treating Brackley as a safe place to send clients, and you have just been told it isn't. A service that is already stretched, 70 km away, will not absorb about 1,950 patients a year. Some of those animals will wait too long, or won't be taken at all. You would be passing your clients a welfare and reputation problem and calling it a referral. Thornby's clients, roughly a fifth of overnight cases (about 390 a year), would be even worse placed, and Thornby would lose its only back-up.

**Where Dr Osei is right**

Closing does abandon clients. You run the only overnight service within 70 km, and that carries a community and professional obligation as well as a commercial one. It is also a source of goodwill and referrals that a closure would damage, and that damage is hard to put a euro figure on.

**Where Dr Osei is wrong, and this matters most for the vote**

Her main argument is that closing overnight would lose the four dairy contracts, worth about €260,000 a year. On your own facts, that doesn't follow. Calvings are attended on the farm by your farm-animal vets, who take calls from home on their own rota, and they have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. The round-the-clock cover the contracts require is provided by the farm team, and the clinic isn't part of it. So closing Kilbride overnight should not, by itself, put those contracts at risk.

Before 28 November, check two things. First, read the actual contract wording. If any of the four mentions hospital or surgical facilities for out-of-hours cases such as caesareans, hospitalisation or after-hours referral, then the clinic might matter. Second, ask the four farms what they actually rely on. If the answer is the farm team, her argument largely falls away. If it turns out some contracts do depend on the clinic, that changes the picture, so settle it before the vote and don't leave it as an assumption.

**What the numbers say about the clinic itself**

- 71% of overnight patients arrive before 1 am. After 1 am you average under two a night, so the 1 am to 8 am stretch, about seven hours of the thirteen, handles under a third of cases.
- The €190,000 loss is mostly the cost of staffing hours with very little demand. I don't have your cost breakdown, so check how much of the loss sits in the late hours.
- The late-night hours are where the rota hurts most, because those are the shifts that wreck a next-day working shift.

**A proposal that gives each partner what they actually need**

1. **Change who works nights, not only whether you do.** Hire one or two dedicated emergency vets on night-heavy contracts with proper pay and time off in lieu, the kind of person you turned away last spring. Then remove the compulsory night shifts from the 11 day-practice vets, or make them voluntary and paid at a premium. This answers the reason people left. If you can't recruit, keep a much lighter rota, for example one shift a quarter, with guaranteed time off the next day.
2. **Cut the hours to where the demand is.** Run the full service from 7 pm to about 1 am, covering roughly 71% of cases. For 1 am to 8 am, test one of: a single vet on call with a nurse and a triage phone line, with calls screened by the on-call vet; or, only if Brackley agrees in writing and can actually take the cases, a referral arrangement for those hours. Don't announce a referral route Brackley hasn't agreed to. Under two patients a night is a load that one on-call vet can plausibly carry, which is very different from an overnight clinic fully staffed throughout.
3. **Make others pay their share.** Thornby sends about a fifth of your overnight patients and has no out-of-hours service of its own. Offer them a formal service agreement with a retainer or a per-case fee, and raise out-of-hours consultation fees for your own clients, which should reflect what the service really costs. Even a partial improvement matters when the loss is €190,000. Don't assume every client will accept higher fees, so model a lower volume.
4. **Protect the dairy contracts independently.** Confirm in writing that the farm team's on-call arrangement meets the contracts' cover requirement, and look at whether their rota is sustainable too. Those four contracts bring in €260,000 a year, and farm vets taking calls from home have their own fatigue and retention risk.

**What this does not settle**

- Whether you can recruit emergency vets in a rural valley. If you can't, option 2 carries more weight and the 1 am cut-off becomes more important.
- Whether the loss falls enough. I can't calculate that from what you've given me. Ask your practice manager for the cost of the 1 am to 8 am stretch against the fees it earns, and for what a dedicated hire would cost. If the numbers don't close the gap, you then have a real choice to make, and it should be made on the figures with the dairy question answered first.
- If the only remaining choice is full closure, then do it as a managed withdrawal with notice, and reach agreed terms with Brackley and Thornby first, so clients are not simply left without cover in January.

**On the vote**

Don't ask the partners to choose between the two positions. Put a motion to them that does three things: tests the dairy-contract assumption, restructures the rota and hours from January, and sets a review date of, say, six months, with a loss target agreed in advance. Dr Halloran gets a real end to the forced-night model that is driving people out. Dr Osei gets the service kept in place for the clients and the community who rely on it, and her contracts are protected by checking, not assuming. If the contract check shows the clinic really is needed for the dairy work, then Dr Osei's case is stronger than it looks today, and I'd revisit the whole thing.

My confidence is fairly high that the either-or framing is wrong, and moderate that this particular package closes the financial gap, since I haven't seen your cost figures.
