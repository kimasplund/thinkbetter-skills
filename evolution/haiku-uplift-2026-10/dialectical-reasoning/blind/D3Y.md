The best answer is neither season as proposed. Gerrit and Ilse are each right about something that matters, and the grant condition is less restrictive than the current debate assumes.

**Start with the grant wording**

The condition says at least 20% of works programmed across the season must be by living composers. It is a share of works, not of concerts. Last season had 16 main-stage concerts with three or four works each, so roughly 48 to 64 works. Twenty percent of that is about 10 to 13 living-composer works for the whole season. That is far below "a fifth of our concerts," which would mean about three concerts. Before March, confirm with the arts council how they count works (for example, whether a short overture counts the same as a full concerto, and whether a commissioned work counts in the season it is premiered). If the reading holds, the grant does not force a contemporary-heavy season.

**What each side gets right**

- Gerrit: Friday subscription concerts averaged 81% full, and that audience is built on familiar repertoire. Single-ticket Saturday concerts, at 48%, are where the 22% fall in sales is concentrated. The board needs revenue, and the classics fill seats.
- Ilse: The grant is 38% of income and is tied to the orchestra's identity. A season of greatest hits competes directly with recordings, and the orchestra's distinctiveness is part of why the grant and the donors exist. Her warning about principal players is a real retention risk, not just an artistic preference.

**Why a split season fails**

If each concert carries half classics and half contemporary work in the same hall, the result tends to disappoint both audiences. Classics subscribers sit through unfamiliar pieces they did not choose, and contemporary listeners get a diluted version of the same programme. Splitting every concert does not create demand for either.

**What your own data says**

Demand is responding to format and setting more than to repertoire alone. Unbound sold out all six nights in a 180-seat foyer with a median audience age of 31, and it is mostly new music. The film and video-game concert was your only main-stage sell-out, and it was entirely living composers. The clear signal is that contemporary music sells when it is presented as an event, in a room that suits it. Ilse's view that the film concert is a one-off is worth testing rather than assuming.

**A direction the board can vote on**

1. Keep the Friday subscription anchored in core repertoire that Gerrit wants, and place one living-composer work in most of the main-stage concerts. Choose short, well-placed pieces such as an opening overture or a companion work to a concerto. Aim for at least 12 living-composer works across the season, which clears the 20% figure with some margin.
2. Let the principals shape which living composers are chosen, and give them a clear role in the Unbound series. This addresses Ilse's retention concern directly, and it gives players ownership of the new repertoire.
3. Expand Unbound, since it has the strongest demand per seat and the youngest audience. Consider one or two additional concerts in the same format.
4. Turn the film and video-game concert into a repeated format, one or two times a season, rather than treating it as a one-off. Ilse's objection is to the repertoire being presented as the orchestra's identity; a clearly labelled event series answers that.
5. Use the €35,000 commission for one new 20-minute work, ideally premiered at Unbound or on a main-stage concert where the audience is already warm, and pick a composer whose work suits either setting.

**Residual risks**

- Some Saturday single-ticket buyers may not want the unfamiliar works. Pilot the living-composer placements in a few concerts first and track Saturday attendance closely.
- Unbound and the film concert sold out at their current size. Growing them may dilute the effect, so add capacity gradually.
- If the arts council counts by concert or by performing time rather than by works, the numbers change sharply. The board should settle the grant reading before it votes on the season.
- A classics-heavy season alone may not reverse the 22% decline, because the same pitch has already been made. The new formats are where the growth is most likely to come from.

**Recommendation**

Ask the board to decide in two steps: first, confirm the grant reading with the arts council; second, approve a season built on core repertoire with a living-composer work in most main-stage concerts, an expanded Unbound series, a repeated film-and-game event, and one commissioned work. This keeps the orchestra's identity and the grant intact, while giving Gerrit a plan aimed at the audiences that are actually buying tickets.
