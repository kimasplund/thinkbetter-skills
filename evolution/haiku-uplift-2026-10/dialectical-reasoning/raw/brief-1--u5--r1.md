Short answer: each of them is right about a different half, and each is also leaning on an assumption your own facts undercut. I would not vote for either proposal as it stands. I'd put a third option to the vote on 28 November.

## What each partner has right

**Dr Halloran is right that the current model is driving vets out.** Three exits in 18 months, all naming nights, is a pattern. But look at what the nights are. Every one of your 11 small-animal vets covers one or two overnight shifts a month on top of a full day rota. The fairness rule is sound, but it means every vet is doing a second, different job (an emergency shift, on a few hours' sleep, then a normal day). The problem is how nights are staffed. You also turned down an experienced emergency vet last spring because she would only work nights. That is the person this service needed.

**Dr Osei is right that closing has real costs for clients and for the group.** You are the only overnight service within 70 km. Closing sends clients on a 70 km drive with a sick animal, to a hospital whose director says it is already stretched. About 390 of your overnight patients (a fifth) belong to Thornby, who have no cover of their own. Closing hurts them and leaves them with no fallback. It also hurts your reputation in the valley.

## Where each argument is weaker than it sounds

**The dairy contracts may not depend on Kilbride at all.** Calvings are attended on the farm, by your farm-animal vets, taking calls from home on their own rota. They have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. If that is also what the four contracts actually require (round-the-clock calving cover), closing the small-animal overnight clinic leaves the €260,000 intact. Before 28 November, someone should read the four contracts and ask the four farms what they believe they are buying. If a contract names Kilbride, or requires access to a hospital for complications such as caesareans needing theatre, that changes things, and you should know it before voting. But as described, this is the strongest fact in the whole discussion and it currently sits unexamined behind a €260,000 number.

**Closing overnight does not fix retention by itself.** It removes the cause the exit interviews named, but you would be giving up a service, and some of the vets who left may have wanted to do emergency work. A group that closes its emergency service and pushes clients 70 km away also becomes a different kind of employer. Closure should be the fallback if a better design fails, not the first move.

## The facts that point to a third option

- 71% of overnight patients arrive before 1 am. That is roughly 1,385 of 1,950 a year, or about 3.8 a night. After 1 am it is under two a night, so perhaps 565 patients a year, spread over seven hours.
- The least busy part of the night is the most expensive part to staff and probably the hardest on people. It is also the part where a vet is most likely to sit awake for hours seeing nothing.
- Your cost is €190,000 a year, and your loss is probably driven by fixed staffing across all 13 hours rather than by the patients.

## What I would put to the vote

**Restructure rather than close or continue.**

1. **Change who works nights.** Hire one or two dedicated emergency vets on night-heavy contracts, ideally the kind of person you turned away last spring, with proper premium pay and time off in lieu. Take most of the 11 day vets off the night rota, perhaps keeping a voluntary, paid, optional shift for those who want it. This addresses Halloran's retention concern directly. Fairness is kept by paying the people who do nights well rather than by rotating the burden.
2. **Shorten staffed hours.** Staff the clinic from 7 pm to about 1 am, which covers 71% of the demand. After 1 am, run a phone triage line with a single on-call vet from home, who comes in for genuine emergencies (likely one or two a night, often fewer). This cuts the heaviest cost and the worst hours while keeping a real service for the valley. Check what your regulator and your insurers say about the cover you must provide.
3. **Make Thornby pay or partner.** About 390 of your overnight patients are theirs. Agree a per-case fee or an annual retainer with Thornby, and perhaps shared out-of-hours arrangements. At the moment you subsidise their lack of cover.
4. **Price the out-of-hours work properly.** Look at your emergency fees against the real cost of a night. A €190,000 loss in the only service within 70 km suggests some room to raise prices, or to add a clear out-of-hours surcharge.
5. **Verify the dairy position now.** Read the contracts and talk to the farms. Their answer sets how much risk the whole decision carries.

## What I can't tell you from what you've sent

- What the redesign would cost against the €190,000 loss. Dedicated night vets cost real money, and the shorter hours save some of it. I'd want a rough budget (salaries, premiums, on-call pay, reduced fixed costs, Thornby income, any fee changes) before the vote. If the restructured service still loses most of that sum, the vote becomes a question of how much the group will pay to keep the service, and that is a legitimate decision for the partners to make openly.
- Whether you can recruit night-focused vets in the valley. You turned one down, but that doesn't prove others are available. Run the search before January, not after.
- Whether the three leavers would have stayed under this model. If you can ask them, or the vets still on the rota, do. Their answers tell you whether the issue is nights in general or nights on top of days.

## A sensible way to sequence it

- Before 28 November: check the dairy contracts and costings; ask Thornby and Brackley what they would agree to.
- At the vote: agree the restructure in principle, with a clear trigger. For example, if you cannot hire at least one dedicated night vet by a set date (say the end of March), or the first six months show the loss is not falling to an agreed level, you move to a 7 pm to 1 am service only, or to full closure with a referral arrangement.
- From January: new rota, reduced night obligations for the day vets, shortened hours if the numbers support it.

## Where that leaves the two partners

Halloran is right about the symptom and about the urgency: the current rota has to change from January. Osei is right that walking away from the valley's only overnight service should be a last resort, and the dairy argument for it deserves checking before it carries any weight. Neither of them needs to lose the vote: the proposal takes her retention concern and his concern for clients seriously, and each can see their main point written into it. If the numbers do not work after you have tried it, closure remains available, and you will have a better case for it than you have today.
