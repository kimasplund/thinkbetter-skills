Short answer: neither is right as stated, and the facts you gave suggest a third option that probably beats both. Each partner has the right diagnosis of one problem and the wrong prescription.

**Where Dr Osei is wrong on the facts**

Her strongest argument is the €260,000 of dairy contracts. But your own facts undercut it. Calvings are attended on the farm by the farm-animal vets, on their own home-call rota, and they have never used the Kilbride clinic at night. Closing the small-animal overnight clinic does not obviously remove round-the-clock calving cover, because that cover doesn't run through the clinic. I would check the four contract texts before the vote. If they require 24-hour farm-animal call-out, they are satisfied by the farm team's rota. If they require a hospital facility for caesareans or downed cows, that is a different matter and needs checking. This is the most important single thing to verify, because it decides whether the €260,000 is really at risk.

Her point about abandoning clients still stands, though. Brackley is 70 km away and says its overnight service is already stretched. Sending 1,950 patients a year there, in emergencies, is a real welfare and reputational cost. Thornby's clients, about a fifth of the patients, lose their only option. Thornby may also move its clients to another group, which would hurt your day business.

**Where Dr Halloran is right and wrong**

She is right that the current model is driving vets out. Three vets left in 18 months and all three named nights. Losing a vet costs recruitment, locum cover and the strain on the remaining team, and it may be a bigger cost than the headline loss.

But the policy that causes this is the rota design, not the existence of the service. All 11 vets do nights on top of full day work, because of an equal-share rule. You also turned down an experienced emergency vet who only wanted nights. That is the telling fact: the market offers a way to staff this that you refused for the sake of fairness. Closing the service solves the retention problem by removing the thing you do for the community. It also leaves Brackley overloaded.

**The third option: shrink and professionalise, rather than close**

1. Hire dedicated night emergency vets, or one or two on a nights-only contract, and drop the policy that everyone shares nights. Day vets then stop doing overnight shifts, or do far fewer. This addresses the exit-interview complaint directly.
2. Consider shortening the hours. 71% of patients arrive before 1 am, and after 1 am you average under two a night. A service from 7 pm to about 1 am captures most of the demand. For the remaining hours you could run on-call, with a vet at home and a nurse triaging by phone, or refer only the rare late-night cases. The longer you staff the quiet hours, the more of the loss comes from them.
3. Talk to Thornby. A fifth of your overnight patients come from them. They could contribute to the cost, by a retainer or a per-case fee, since you're giving them a service they can't provide. That also turns a risk into revenue.
4. Review pricing. Emergency fees at many practices carry a premium, and a €190,000 loss may partly reflect fees that don't cover the cost of night work.

I can't say the numbers work without your cost breakdown. If the loss is mostly staffing for the quiet hours, a shorter service with dedicated staff could cut it a lot. If it stays large, you have a clearer decision: the group is paying for a community service, and you can decide what it is worth.

**What I'd do before 28 November**

- Read the dairy contracts and confirm what the cover requirement actually says. Ask the farm-animal team whether any client has ever expected the Kilbride clinic.
- Get the overnight cost split by hour, to see what a 7 pm to 1 am service would cost.
- Ask the emergency vet you turned down, or similar candidates, whether a nights-only post is still possible, and what it would cost.
- Open talks with Thornby and sound out Brackley on whether they could take after-1 am cases.
- Put the third option to the vote as a time-limited trial, say six months from January with agreed measures: retention, cost, patients turned away. If it fails, closure is still available, and you will have lost little.

**My view**

If the contracts only need farm-call cover, Dr Osei's main financial argument largely falls away, but her instinct that you shouldn't just abandon the valley is sound. Dr Halloran is right that the status quo is unsustainable, but closure is the most drastic fix for a problem that comes from how you staff the rota. Voting on close versus keep is a false choice. I would ask both partners to back a restructured service.

Assumptions: I haven't seen the contracts or the cost breakdown, and I've assumed the €190,000 loss is after all night costs and that nights-only hires are available in your area.
