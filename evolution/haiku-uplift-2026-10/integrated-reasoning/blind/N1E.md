My recommendation: don't buy any of the three kilns by Friday. Let the quote lapse, or ask the supplier for a two to three week extension. The crazing is very unlikely to be a kiln problem, and none of the three kilns is the right fix for it. Your capacity problem is real, but it is a separate question that you can decide properly afterwards.

**Why the kiln is probably not the cause**

- Your test kiln is two years old and has run at about 8% crazing since May. A healthy kiln producing the same fault as your 14-year-old one points away from the kiln.
- The change that lines up with the timing is the April clay body switch. Rejects were about 2% for years with the same satin white glaze, and they jumped to 9% soon after you changed clay. The glaze recipe didn't change, but whether a glaze fits depends on the clay under it, so an unchanged glaze on a new body can stop fitting.
- Crazing that shows up two or three weeks later, after dishwashers, is typical of a glaze that is under tension on the clay. The pieces slowly absorb moisture and expand, and the glaze cracks. A kiln fault would more likely show up as visible defects at unloading, and you say most of this isn't caught at the kiln.
- The January element replacement doesn't fit the timing as well. Five months passed between it and the jump, and the test kiln shows the same problem.

I'd put about 80% on "new clay body and old glaze no longer fit" and about 10% on firing or cooling effects (which would be the one thing in Kiln B's favour). The rest is something I can't see from here, such as a changed glaze batch or a change in how loads are packed. I can't confirm any of this from a message, so I'd treat it as the leading hypothesis to test, not a verdict.

**What buying now would do**

- A kiln would not lower the 9%. You would spend €15-22k, wait six weeks or more, and still be remaking about 1 in 11 pieces.
- Controlled cooling on B might help a little, but a glaze that doesn't fit the clay is unlikely to be rescued by slower cooling alone.
- The cost of the problem is also eating your capacity. At 2,400 pieces a week, going from 2% to 9% means roughly 170 extra pieces a week that you make and then remake for free. That is about 7% of your output, which is a large part of the room you've been looking for. Fixing the crazing may free up more capacity than a small kiln purchase would, at close to zero cost.

**Cheap checks to run over the next 1-2 weeks, before any purchase**

1. Fire a test batch of the same glaze on the old clay body, in the same kiln, and compare it with the new body. If one crazes and the other doesn't, you have your answer.
2. Ask the new clay supplier for the body's thermal expansion data, and compare it with the old body's. A higher-expanding clay under the same glaze would explain the crazing.
3. Run an accelerated test on fired pieces: boiling or autoclave-type steaming, or repeated dishwasher cycles. You'll know within days instead of weeks.
4. If the clay is the cause, adjust the glaze (typically more silica or less flux, in small steps) and re-test. The alternative is to go back to the old clay body despite the price. Compare the clay savings with the cost of remaking about 7% of your output.
