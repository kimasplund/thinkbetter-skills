**Short answer:** Don't buy any of the three kilns on the strength of the crazing problem. The timing and your test kiln point at the clay body change, not the kiln. If you need more capacity, Kiln A is the only one of the three that adds it. Before Friday, ask the kiln supplier for a short extension, or make the decision on capacity grounds alone.

**Why the kiln is probably not the cause**

- The crazing rate jumped in May. The clay body changed in April. Your main kiln was not changed at all in that period (the elements were replaced in January, which is four months before the jump).
- Your two-year-old test kiln is a different machine and it is also crazing at about 8%. When two separate kilns show the same fault, the shared factor is more likely the thing that changed: the clay body, or possibly a new batch of glaze raw materials.
- Crazing is usually a fit problem between glaze and body. If the body expands more on heating and cooling than the glaze, the glaze ends up in tension and cracks. A cheaper body from a new supplier is a likely candidate because its thermal expansion can differ even when the forming looks the same. Your glaze recipe being unchanged does not prove the glaze behaves the same on the new body.
- Controlled cooling (Kiln B) can help some defects, but it is not a standard cure for glaze-fit crazing. Reduction firing (Kiln C) changes the glaze chemistry and surface, and would not fix a fit problem by itself.

I am inferring this from the timeline and the test-kiln data. I have not seen your firing logs, body or glaze data sheets, or the failed pieces, so treat this as a strong hypothesis to test, not a diagnosis.

**What to check this week (cheap, and before any capital spend)**

1. Ask the new clay supplier for the thermal expansion coefficient (COE) of the new body and of the old one, if you still have data for it.
2. Run a controlled comparison: the same glaze on the old body and the new body, fired in the same test kiln under the same schedule. If the old body does not craze, the body is the cause.
3. Run a boil or dunk test on fired test tiles to check glaze fit before pieces ever reach a restaurant. This would catch the defect at the kiln rather than two or three weeks later.
4. Check whether the glaze raw materials came from a new supplier or batch around May, since that is the other shared change.
5. Depending on what the tests show, the fix may be a body change back to the old one, a glaze adjustment, or a negotiated body spec with the supplier. None of these costs €15,000 or more.
