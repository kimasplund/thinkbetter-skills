**Short answer: don't buy any of the three kilns before Friday.** The evidence points to the clay body switch in April as the most likely cause of the crazing, not the kiln. Spending €14,900 to €22,100 on a kiln would probably leave the rejects where they are.

**Why the kiln is unlikely to be the cause**

- **Timing.** Rejects went from about 2% to 9% in May, a month after you changed clay body suppliers. Your glaze recipe hasn't changed in six years, and the kiln's elements were replaced in January, before the problem started.
- **The test kiln shows the same problem.** Your two-year-old test kiln has about 8% crazing since May, close to the main kiln's 9%. A fault that is specific to the old kiln would usually produce a clearly different rate in a second kiln. A problem that shows up in both points upstream, at the body or the glaze fit.
- **Crazing is usually a mismatch in expansion.** The glaze ends up under too much tension relative to the body, so it cracks. A cheaper clay body is often different in its quartz or feldspar content, which changes how much it expands and contracts when fired and cooled. That would explain why pieces pass the kiln and fail after dishwashers, when the thermal stress is repeated.

**What each kiln would actually change**

- **Kiln A (€18,400, larger chamber):** solves capacity, which is a real problem, but not crazing. It is above €15,000, so all three partners must agree.
- **Kiln B (€14,900, controlled cooling):** slower, more controlled cooling can reduce some stress-related crazing, so it may help a little. It is the only option under €15,000, so Ines can decide it alone. But it is unlikely to fix a body mismatch on its own.
- **Kiln C (€22,100, gas, reduction):** reduction firing changes how the glaze develops colour and surface. It would need new glaze tests, and it does not address crazing. It is above €15,000, so all three partners must agree.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. **Ask the kiln supplier to hold the quote for 3 to 4 weeks.** Say you are checking the cause of a quality problem before committing. Most suppliers will agree to a short extension. If not, the quote's expiry is a reason to be careful, not a reason to rush.
2. **Get the expansion data.** Ask the new clay body supplier for its thermal expansion figure (coefficient of thermal expansion), and compare it with the old body's spec sheet. If the new supplier can't provide it, that is itself useful information.
3. **Run a side-by-side test.** Fire the same glaze on old-body and new-body test tiles in the same load, in both the main kiln and the test kiln. Put them through a few dishwasher cycles and count crazing. Two to three weeks should give a clear answer.
4. **Decide the kiln after that test.** If the body is the cause, your options are to go back to the old body (if the price difference is manageable), to adjust the glaze with a supplier or glaze chemist so it fits the new body, or to ask the body supplier for a matching formulation. If the main kiln is clearly worse than the test kiln in the side-by-side test, then the kiln becomes a real factor, and Kiln B's controlled cooling is the most sensible choice of the three.
