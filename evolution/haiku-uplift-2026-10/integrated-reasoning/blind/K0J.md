## Why the kiln is probably not the problem

Here is what your own numbers show:

- **Timing.** Rejects were about 2% for a year and went to 9% from May. The only change you list just before that is the April switch to a cheaper clay body. The glaze recipe stayed the same.
- **The test kiln.** It is a different, newer kiln, and it crazes at about 8% since May. If the old kiln were the problem, the test kiln should look better. It looks about the same.
- **The elements.** They were replaced in January. Your rates were fine from January to April, so they don't fit the timeline.
- **The failure mode.** Crazing that shows up after weeks and dishwasher cycles is a classic sign of glaze fit. The glaze shrinks more than the clay body on cooling, or the body expands and takes on moisture over time. A new body with an unchanged glaze is the textbook way to break a fit that worked for six years.

My rough read is that it's about 80% likely that the clay body and glaze no longer match, and only about 10% likely that the kiln is a main driver. Cooling speed can matter at the margins, which is the one real argument for B's controlled cooling. But it can't explain a jump that appears in two kilns at once.

**What this means for the decision.** If you spend €15-22k and the crazing stays at 8-9%, you will still be remaking about 1 piece in 11 for free, and you will have committed the cash for nothing.

## Cheap tests to run this week

These cost little and can be done before Friday or soon after:

1. **Get the clay supplier's data.** Ask for the thermal expansion figure and recommended firing range for the new body, and compare it with the old one.
2. **Fire a small test batch of the old clay body, if any is left, with the current glaze in the current kiln.** If those pieces don't craze and the new-body pieces do, the question is settled.
3. **Run a quick crazing stress test on test tiles.** For example, repeat heating and plunging into cold water, or an autoclave or steam test, on new-body and old-body tiles. You get answers in days rather than weeks.
4. **Adjust the glaze for the new body.** Typical fixes are changing the silica or alumina content, or using a flux with lower expansion. Your glaze supplier or a ceramic consultant can help with this. You could also try a slightly different firing schedule or a slower cool in the existing kiln as a secondary test.
5. **If none of that works,** go back to the old clay body and accept the higher price. At 9% remakes, the cost of the old clay is probably lower than what you're paying now.
