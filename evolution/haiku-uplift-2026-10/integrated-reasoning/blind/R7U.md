## Why the kiln is probably not the problem

- The change lines up with the clay, not the kiln. Rejects were about 2% for years. You switched clay bodies in April, and the jump to 9% started in May. The glaze recipe, the kiln and the elements (replaced in January) were all unchanged or already in place before that.
- Your two-year-old test kiln shows about 8%. If the old kiln were the cause, a different, newer kiln should be fine. It isn't, so the two kilns share the cause, and the thing they share is the new clay body.
- Crazing that shows up weeks later, after dishwashing, is the usual sign of glaze that is under tension on the body. The glaze shrinks more than the clay as it cools, or the body expands too little, and the glaze cracks over time. A cheaper body with a different thermal expansion would produce exactly this with an unchanged glaze.

I'd put roughly 80% on "clay body and glaze no longer fit". The remaining 20% is things like firing or cooling effects that make a marginal fit worse. Controlled cooling (Kiln B) could help a little there, but it doesn't fix a real expansion mismatch.

## What to do before Friday

1. **Ask the Kiln A seller for an extension** (a week or two, or a refundable deposit that holds the price). Sellers often agree, and a hard deadline is a reason for caution, not for speed.
2. **Ask your new clay supplier for the body's thermal expansion figure** (and the old supplier's, if you still have it).
3. **Run a quick comparison test this week.** Glaze and fire a few pieces on the new body and a few on remaining old-body stock in the same load. Put them through the dishwasher or an accelerated test (a few cycles, or the boiling-water-then-cold-water shock test). If old-body pieces hold and new-body pieces craze, the cause is confirmed.
4. **If it's confirmed**, choose one of these: go back to the old clay and absorb the price rise (compare it to 7% remakes plus free replacements plus lost accounts), find a different body with a matching expansion, or adjust the glaze to fit the new body (usually by lowering its expansion, for example less sodium/potassium flux or more silica). Your glaze supplier can advise.
5. **Check the numbers on the price.** The old clay's price rise is probably small next to the cost of 7% extra remakes, kiln time and goodwill with 40 restaurants.
