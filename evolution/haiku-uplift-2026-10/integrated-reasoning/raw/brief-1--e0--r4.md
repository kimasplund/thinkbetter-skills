Here is how I'd compare the three kilns, and what I'd take to Ines and Tomasz.

**The main point: the crazing may not be a kiln problem.**
Your timeline points at the clay body. You switched bodies in April, crazing started in May, and the glaze hasn't changed. Your small test kiln shows about 8% crazing since May, and it fires the same new clay. A kiln fault would not usually show up in a second, different kiln at the same time. So the most likely cause is a fit problem between the new body and the satin white glaze (the body expands or contracts differently), and it shows up after dishwashing. Buying any of these kilns may not fix it.

**The three quotes against your needs**

| | Kiln A | Kiln B | Kiln C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | €18,400 | €14,900 | €22,100 |
| Size | 30% larger | Same as current | Not stated |
| Controlled cooling | Not stated | Yes | Not stated |
| Reduction firing | No | No | Yes |
| Lead time | 6 weeks | Not stated | Not stated |
| Approval needed | All three partners (over €15,000) | Production lead (under €15,000) | All three partners (over €15,000) |

- **Capacity:** only A adds room. That matters because you're turning accounts away. A bigger chamber helps only if your firing schedule can use it; a larger kiln also takes longer per load.
- **Crazing:** B's controlled cooling is the only feature that plausibly affects crazing, because slow, even cooling can reduce stress in the glaze. It's a reasonable thing to test, but it isn't proven for your glaze and body.
- **Reduction glazes:** C is the only kiln that does reduction. That is a product decision about two restaurant requests, not a fix for crazing, and it's the most expensive option. I wouldn't buy C for this problem.

**My recommendation**

Don't let Friday's deadline decide this on its own. In order:

1. **Ask the supplier to extend the Kiln A quote** (or hold B's price) for two to three weeks. Ask for B's lead time too, which the quotes don't give. A reasonable supplier will often agree.
2. **Run a quick test before you commit.** Fire test tiles with the new body and the old body (if you have any left), both with the satin white glaze, in the same load. Then run them through a dishwasher cycle and check for crazing. If the old body is clean and the new one crazes, you have your answer, and the fix is the clay or glaze, not the kiln. If you can't get the old body, try a glaze adjustment on the new body with a small test batch.
3. **If you must decide by Friday, I'd pick Kiln B.** It's the cheapest, same size as your current kiln, and it's under €15,000, so Ines can approve it without a partner vote. Controlled cooling is the one feature that might help with crazing. Choose A only if you've confirmed that capacity is your real bottleneck and the crazing is solved another way. A is over the €15,000 threshold, so it needs all three partners to agree.

**Assumptions I made**
- Your test kiln fires the same glaze and clay under similar schedules. If its firing differs a lot, the 8% comparison is weaker.
- The 9% rate is measured on the same products and the same restaurants, so it's comparable to the 2% baseline.
- I don't know the lead times for B and C, or the throughput of A. Check these with the suppliers.

**What to take to Ines and Tomasz:** the crazing started when the clay changed, not when the kiln aged. Buying a kiln before testing that is a risk. Propose the clay test this week, and ask for a quote extension so the kiln decision can wait for its result.
