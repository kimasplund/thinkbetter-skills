The timing of the crazing points away from the kiln, so I would not treat this as a kiln-purchase decision yet. Here is the reasoning, then a recommendation you can take to Ines and Tomasz.

**What the evidence says**

- Rejects were about 2% last year and jumped to 9% in May. The clay body changed in April, a few weeks before that jump. Your glaze recipe is unchanged.
- Your 14-year-old main kiln has had new elements since January, so its firing behaviour changed earlier than the problem did.
- Your small test kiln, which is two years old, is also crazing at about 8% since May. A kiln that is 14 years old and a kiln that is two years old both showing the problem makes the kiln an unlikely single cause.
- Crazing is usually a fit problem: the glaze contracts more than the body under it, and the glaze ends up in tension. A change in the body's expansion, or in how it fires and cools, is the most likely trigger. That points at the clay body first.
- Restaurants see the crazing after dishwashing, so it is a stress-driven failure that shows up late. This is consistent with a body/glaze fit problem.

**How the three kilns relate to this**

- **Kiln A** (€18,400, 30% more chamber, six-week lead time): solves a capacity problem, not a crazing problem. You have said capacity is what is costing you accounts. It is the only option that adds throughput, but it is above the €15,000 threshold, so all three partners must agree.
- **Kiln B** (€14,900, same size, programmable controlled cooling): the only option that addresses the cooling side of stress in the glaze, which is relevant to crazing. It is just under the threshold, so Ines decides alone. It does not add capacity.
- **Kiln C** (€22,100, gas, reduction): reduction firing would change how the glaze matures, and that could change the fit with the new body in ways you cannot predict. It is above the threshold and addresses a sales request, not the defect.

**Recommendation**

1. **Before Friday, ask the kiln A supplier to extend the quote** by two to four weeks. A six-week lead time means the quote is not what sets your real timeline anyway. If they refuse, that is useful information about how much they want the sale.
2. **Run a short controlled test now.** Fire a batch of pieces with the old clay body (you may have stock or can buy a small lot) and a batch with the new body, both with the same satin white glaze, both in the same kiln. Check them after a dishwasher cycle, since that is when crazing shows. Two weeks of this will tell you more than any kiln spec.
3. **If you must decide by Friday**, the least risky choice is **Kiln B**. It is the cheapest, it is within Ines's authority, and controlled cooling is the only feature that touches the mechanism. Be clear that buying it is a bet that cooling is part of the problem, not a fix you have confirmed.
4. **Do not buy Kiln C for the crazing.** If the restaurants want reduction glazes, that is a separate product decision, and it needs its own testing before you commit €22,100 to it.
5. **Kiln A is the right answer only if capacity is the binding constraint** and you are willing to commit above the threshold. Given that you turned down two accounts, that may well be true, but it is a separate decision from fixing crazing and should be judged on its own.

**Assumptions I have made**

- The old clay body is still available or can be bought in a small lot for testing.
- The test kiln and main kiln fire to similar temperatures and cooling profiles. If they do not, that difference matters and is worth checking.
- The crazing rate you see is consistent across glaze batches. If you have mixed batches, glaze preparation is worth checking too.

**Bottom line for Ines and Tomasz:** the defect started with the clay change, not the kiln. Buy nothing above €15,000 until a short test shows whether the body is the cause. If a purchase is forced by Friday, Kiln B is the lowest-risk option, and it is Ines's call under your agreement.
