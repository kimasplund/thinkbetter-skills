## Why I don't think the kiln is the cause

Three facts in your message point away from it:

- **Timing.** Rejects were about 2% for a long time and went to 9% in May. You changed clay body in April. The kiln elements were replaced in January, which is four months before the problem began.
- **The test kiln.** It is a different, newer kiln, and it is also running about 8% since May. If the main kiln were the problem, the test kiln should look healthy. It doesn't.
- **The failure mode.** Crazing is a fit problem between glaze and clay body. The glaze shrinks more than the body as they cool, and the glaze cracks, often days or weeks later. Dishwashers speed this up, which matches restaurants reporting it two or three weeks after delivery. Your satin white has been fine for six years on the old body. The new, cheaper body probably expands and contracts differently, so the same glaze no longer fits it.

I'd put roughly 75-80% on "the new clay body doesn't fit the glaze" as the main cause. The rest is mostly firing or cooling effects that make a marginal fit worse. I can't rule those out from here, but two kilns showing the same problem makes them secondary.

Controlled cooling on Kiln B can help a marginal fit. It is unlikely to rescue a real mismatch, and it would not explain the test kiln.

## What the crazing is costing you

Using your numbers: 2,400 pieces a week at 9% is about 216 remakes, against about 48 at 2%. That is roughly 170 pieces a week, or about 7% of your capacity, going into free remakes, plus the cost of lost goodwill with restaurants. Fixing the fit could free much of that capacity. It may not fully replace a bigger kiln, but it changes how big a kiln you need. Right now you are sizing the purchase against a capacity number that the crazing is inflating.

## What I'd bring to Ines and Tomasz

1. **Run a quick diagnostic this week.** Fire the same satin white glaze on tiles or plates of the old clay body and the new one, in the same load. Then run an accelerated test: repeated cycles of boiling water and cold water, or a few dishwasher runs. If only the new body crazes, that settles it. It costs almost nothing and takes days, not weeks.
2. **If the body is the cause**, you have two options: revert to the old supplier at the higher price, or adjust the glaze to fit the new body. Adding silica or reducing a high-expansion flux usually lowers the glaze's expansion, but a ceramic technician or your glaze materials supplier should confirm the changes. Compare the cost of the old clay against 170 remade pieces a week and the lost accounts. The "cheaper" body may be costing you more than it saves.
3. **Then decide on the kiln** with a clean view of real capacity. If you still can't fit new accounts after the crazing is fixed, Kiln A is the logical choice, and you'd have the unanimous approval it needs because the case is clear.
4. **Ask the seller for an extension now.** Explain that you need a few weeks to finish testing. Many sellers will extend, particularly for a larger sale. If they won't, quotes can usually be re-issued, perhaps at a slightly different price. Losing this particular quote costs far less than buying the wrong kiln.
