My recommendation is not to buy any of the three kilns on Friday. I'd first fix the crazing, because the evidence suggests the kiln isn't causing it.

**Why the kiln is probably not the cause**

- Crazing jumped from 2% to 9% starting in May. You switched clay body suppliers in April, and the glaze recipe stayed the same. Crazing is a glaze-fit problem: the glaze shrinks more than the clay body as it cools, or the body expands differently than before. A new body with an unchanged glaze is the classic cause.
- Your two-year-old test kiln is a different kiln, with new elements and a different size, and it shows about 8%. If the main kiln were the culprit, the test kiln should look much better. It doesn't, so the common factor is the clay body (or the glaze applied to it), not the kiln.
- The main kiln's elements were replaced in January, and rejects were fine until May. That timing also points away from the kiln.
- Delayed crazing that appears after dishwasher cycles is typical of marginal glaze fit. Moisture expansion in a more porous or under-fired body makes it worse. That also fits a body change.

This is strong circumstantial evidence rather than proof. You haven't isolated the variable yet.

**What the three kilns would and wouldn't do**

- **Kiln A (€18,400, 30% larger):** This addresses your capacity problem (two turned-down accounts), not crazing. It needs all three partners to agree because it's over €15,000. A bigger chamber can also make even heating and cooling harder, so don't assume it helps quality.
- **Kiln B (€14,900, programmable controlled cooling):** This is the only option that could plausibly help crazing, since cooling rate affects glaze fit. It is also under the €15,000 threshold, so Ines can decide it alone. But it adds no capacity, and slower cooling usually only partly compensates for a mismatched glaze and body.
- **Kiln C (€22,100, gas, reduction):** This is a different product line. Reduction glazes would change your firing, your glazes and your staff's skills, and it comes with gas installation and ventilation costs. Two restaurants asking is thin demand for a €22k bet. It would not fix crazing in your satin white, and it would force recipe redevelopment.

None of them tests the real cause. A kiln purchase would also lock in about €15-22k before you know whether it is needed.

**What I'd do this week, cheaply**

1. Fire a controlled test: the same glaze on the old clay body and the new body, in the same load, in the same kiln. If the old body craze rate drops back near 2%, you've found the cause. If you've run out of the old body, ask the old supplier for a small batch.
2. Ask your clay supplier for the new body's thermal expansion data and compare it with your glaze's. Often a small glaze adjustment (more silica, or less of a high-expansion flux such as sodium or potassium) fixes the fit. A ceramics glaze consultant or the glaze material supplier can help quickly.
3. Check firing: the peak temperature and soak of the new body may differ from the old one. An under-fired body is more porous and more prone to delayed crazing.
4. Run a dishwasher or boil-and-chill test on test tiles from each variation, so you do not wait two to three weeks for restaurant feedback.

**The Friday deadline**

- Ask Kiln A's seller for a 2-4 week extension or price hold. Quotes are often extendable, and a six-week lead time means the purchase isn't urgent in the way the deadline implies.
- If they refuse, remember that a missed quote costs you at most a re-quote, perhaps a few percent. Continued 9% rejects cost far more. At about 2,400 pieces a week, the extra 7% is roughly 170 remakes a week, plus the goodwill with 40 restaurants.
- Do not let the expiry push you into an unplanned purchase of more than €15,000, and remember it requires all three of you.

**If you do want to decide on capacity**

Capacity is a genuine, separate decision. If the glaze fix works, your effective output also rises, because you stop remaking about 7% of pieces. Revisit then whether you need A's extra space, with remake losses removed from the numbers. If you do still need capacity, A is the sensible one, and you should check that its chamber's cooling is even. Revisit C only if reduction demand becomes real, for example through signed commitments.
