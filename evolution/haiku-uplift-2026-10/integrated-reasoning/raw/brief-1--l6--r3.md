My recommendation is not to buy any of the three kilns by Friday. Let the quote lapse, or ask Kiln A's supplier for a 2-4 week extension. The evidence says the kiln is probably not what's causing your crazing.

**Why the kiln is probably not the cause**

- The crazing jumped from 2% to 9% in May, right after you changed clay body suppliers in April. Your glaze recipe didn't change. Crazing is a glaze-fit problem: the glaze shrinks more than the clay body as it cools. A new body has a different thermal expansion, so a glaze that fitted the old body can stop fitting the new one.
- Your two-year-old test kiln shows about 8% crazing over the same period. A different, newer kiln gives nearly the same result as your 14-year-old main kiln, so the common factor is the clay and glaze, not the equipment. Your elements were also replaced in January, before the problem began.
- The delay fits too. Crazing often shows up weeks later, after thermal cycling in dishwashers.

This is strong circumstantial evidence, not proof. Other things could have changed in May, such as a glaze batch, a raw material lot, firing schedules or kiln loading. Still, the timing and the test kiln match point the same way.

**What this means for the three kilns**

- A new kiln will probably not fix a body-glaze mismatch. You could spend €15-22k and still be at 8-9% rejects.
- Kiln B's controlled cooling is the one feature that could matter, since cooling rate affects crazing. But slower cooling usually only reduces crazing marginally. It doesn't fix a real expansion mismatch.
- Kiln C (gas, reduction) changes the glaze chemistry and colour response. Your satin white would need re-developing, and you'd be taking on that risk during an existing quality problem. Two restaurants asking about reduction glazes is a thin case for €22k.
- Kiln A addresses your real capacity limit (you've turned down two accounts). That is a valid reason to buy, but it's a growth decision, separate from the crazing. Buying more capacity while 9% of output comes back would multiply your remake costs.

**What to do this week (cheap and fast)**

1. Ask your old clay supplier for a small batch, or use any remaining stock, and fire a test run of the same glaze on old body versus new body in the same kiln load. This is the key experiment. If the old body doesn't craze and the new one does, you have your answer.
2. Ask the glaze supplier or a ceramics technician about glaze fit on the new body. Typical fixes are adjusting the glaze (for example, adding silica or reducing the flux or sodium content to lower its expansion) or changing the body.
3. Run a thermal shock or autoclave test on fired samples, or put test pieces through your own dishwasher cycle, to get results in days rather than weeks.
4. Compare the cost of the cheaper clay against the remake cost. At 9% rejects versus 2%, on 2,400 pieces a week, you're remaking roughly 170 extra pieces weekly, which may wipe out the saving on the clay.
