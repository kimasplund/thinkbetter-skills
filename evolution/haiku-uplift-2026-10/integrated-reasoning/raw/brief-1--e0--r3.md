I'd push back on buying any of the three kilns before Friday. The evidence in your message points at the clay body, not the kiln, and I'd sort that out first.

**What the timeline shows**

- Crazing was about 2% last year and jumped to 9% from May.
- You switched clay body suppliers in April. The new body is cheaper. The glaze, which is the same satin white you've used for six years, hasn't changed.
- Your small test kiln is also running at about 8% since May. It is a different kiln, so the kiln is unlikely to be the common cause.
- Most of the defects only show up two or three weeks after delivery, after dishwashing. That fits a glaze-body fit problem, where the glaze is under tension from the body and the stress shows up over time. It is less typical of a firing-schedule fault, which would usually show at the kiln.

The strongest reading is that the new clay body doesn't fit the glaze well (different shrinkage or thermal expansion). That's a hypothesis, not a proven cause, but it matches the timing and the test-kiln result better than any kiln explanation does.

**How the three quotes compare on crazing**

- **Kiln A (€18,400, 30% larger):** Adds capacity, which matters for your turned-down accounts. It does nothing specific for crazing, and it's the only option that needs all three partners to agree.
- **Kiln B (€14,900, same size, controlled cooling):** The most relevant to crazing. Crazing is partly driven by how the glaze cools and contracts, so a controlled cooling profile could reduce it. But that is a plausible benefit, not a demonstrated one, and it is the same size, so it doesn't add capacity. It is just under the €15,000 threshold, so it's Ines's call as production lead.
- **Kiln C (€22,100, gas, reduction):** Reduction firing would change your glaze and surface results, so it's a product decision more than a fix. It's the most expensive, needs all three partners, and does nothing for crazing on its own. Two restaurants asking about reduction glazes is a sales signal, not a reason to change your main production kiln now.

**What I'd recommend**

1. Don't sign for any kiln by Friday on the strength of this problem. Ask the supplier for a short extension on the quote, or ask whether it can be held. If A is clearly the right capacity buy, the six-week lead time gives you a reason to decide soon, but it still shouldn't be decided on crazing.
2. Start with a cheap test: fire a batch of the satin white on the old clay body and on the new one, in the same kiln and schedule, and check for crazing after a dishwasher cycle. Two to three weeks of that will tell you much more than any quote.
3. If the test confirms the body is the cause, the fixes are on the material side: go back to the old body, adjust the glaze for the new body, or ask the new supplier for a body that matches the glaze. Each of these costs far less than a kiln.
4. If you still want a kiln for capacity, B is the one to consider. It's the cheapest, it stays under the three-partner threshold, and controlled cooling is useful if the test shows cooling stress is part of the problem. A is the choice if space is the real constraint, but it should be justified by capacity and not by crazing.

**For your conversation with Ines and Tomasz**

- Ines is right that cooling control is the only kiln feature that touches the crazing problem, though it hasn't been proven.
- Tomasz's reduction glaze interest is a real market question, but it's separate from the production problem and should be decided on its own.
- Your own lean toward A is about capacity, which is a valid reason, but it doesn't solve the rejects.

**Assumptions**

- I'm assuming the test kiln and the main kiln fire similar schedules. If they differ a lot, the comparison is weaker.
- I'm assuming the crazing you're seeing is the classic glaze-body fit type and not a different defect. A quick look at the failed pieces would confirm this.
- I don't have data on your kiln's current cooling profile, so I can't say how much B would help.

Short version: the crazing started with the clay body change and shows up in the test kiln too. Don't commit €15,000 to €22,000 of kiln to a problem that probably sits in the clay and glaze. Get an extension, run the body test, and decide the kiln for capacity reasons on its own.
