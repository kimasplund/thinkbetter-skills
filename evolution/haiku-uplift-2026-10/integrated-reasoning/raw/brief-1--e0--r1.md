Short answer: don't buy a kiln on the crazing argument. The evidence points at the new clay body, not the kiln. A is the only option that solves the problem you can measure today, which is capacity, so if you buy anything, buy A, and get all three partners to sign off on it before Friday.

**Why the kiln probably isn't the cause**

- Crazing started in May, one month after you changed clay body suppliers in April. Your glaze recipe is unchanged. Crazing is usually a mismatch between the glaze and the body's thermal expansion, so a cheaper body that expands differently is the most likely trigger.
- Your two-year-old test kiln shows about 8% crazing since May, on the same new body. A different kiln showing the same fault suggests the cause is upstream of the kiln.
- The main kiln's elements were replaced in January, four months before the problem started. That doesn't prove it's unrelated, but it's not the obvious trigger.
- Failures show up two or three weeks after delivery, after dishwashing. That fits a glaze under stress from a body mismatch, which is worsened by thermal cycling.

So none of the three kilns is likely to bring crazing back to 2%. Kiln B's controlled cooling may reduce crazing somewhat, because cooling rate affects glaze stress, but that is an assumption until you test it.

A gas kiln also usually needs gas supply, flue and permit work that the quote may not include. Don't choose it on this basis without that cost confirmed.

**Recommendation for Friday**

1. Ask the supplier for a two- to three-week extension on Kiln A's quote. Most suppliers will agree if you ask now, and the six-week lead time already makes this a deadline you can negotiate.
2. Take A to Ines and Tomasz as the capacity decision. Make it clear that it doesn't fix crazing, so nobody expects it to.
3. Start the crazing investigation now, without waiting for the kiln decision:
   - Fire a set of test tiles with the current glaze on the old clay body and on the new one, in the test kiln, side by side. Include a few that go through a dishwasher cycle, since that's where the failures appear.
   - Ask the new clay body supplier for its thermal expansion coefficient and compare it with the old body's.
   - If the new body is the cause, the options are to go back to the old body, reformulate the glaze to suit the new body, or negotiate a body price that makes the old one viable.
4. If the tests show the body is the cause, you can cut the 9% rejects back toward 2% without buying any kiln. That's roughly 168 pieces a week out of 2,400 (7 points of the output), which is capacity you already own.

**Assumptions I've made**

- Rejects are a share of fired output. If they're a share of something else, the 168-piece figure changes.
- The test kiln is fired with the same glaze and body as the main kiln.
- Kiln B's "controlled cooling" is a real cooling-rate control and not just a fixed program.
- The quotes don't include any gas or electrical work I haven't been told about.
