Short answer: the case does not hold up as written. It has several errors that all push in Petra's favour, and once they are corrected the payback is close to your four-month limit, not comfortably inside it. Whether it passes depends on two facts you can check before Thursday.

Assumptions I've used: six trading days a week, so four months is about 104 trading days. Demand is about 150 turned-away loaves a day, and those loaves sell at full price. Nothing else changes (labour, downtime, installation are not in the case). The €54,000 is the amount the board tests for payback.

**Step by step**

1. **Capacity (overstated).** The proofing cabinet holds 60 loaves and proofs in 40 minutes, and dough goes straight from proofer to oven. The D9 takes 72 loaves per load, but the proofer only feeds 60 per load. Unless the proofer is upgraded or a second batch can be proofed without breaking the 40-minute timing, the realistic gain is 60 − 48 = 12 loaves per load, or 108 loaves a day, not 216. Her 648 figure needs a proofer she hasn't costed.

2. **Extra profit (overstated).** €6.00 is the VAT-inclusive shelf price. Net of 10% VAT a loaf brings in €5.45. Her €900 is gross revenue with no ingredient cost. Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 per loaf, so each extra loaf contributes about €3.75 before energy.

3. **Energy (wrong sign).** Current energy is 9 × €4.00 = €36 a day. The D9 at 9 loads × €5.20 = €46.80 a day, so energy rises by about €10.80 a day. The 1.1 cent per-loaf "saving" is applied to all 648 loaves, including loaves that don't exist today, and it is not a cash saving.

4. **Financing (overstated).** 6% × €54,000 × 5 years is flat-rate interest. On a standard amortising loan the monthly payment is about €1,044, the total repaid is about €62,640, and interest is about €8,640, not €16,200. Her €70,200 total is therefore too high. Using a total cost in the payback test is also not what the board's rule usually means; it tests the outlay.

5. **Payback (recalculated).** Net daily gain = extra loaves sold × €3.75 − €10.80 energy.

| Scenario | Extra loaves sold/day | Net gain/day | Payback (trading days) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petra's case as written | 216 (gross, no costs) | n/a | 77 |
| Proofer-limited (60 per load) | 108 | about €395 | about 137 |
| Full 216 baked, only 150 sell | 150 sold (216 baked) | about €440 | about 123 |
| 150 baked and sold, proofer upgraded | 150 | about €552 | about 98 |

The first three rows fail the four-month test. Only the last passes, and only if the proofer can handle about 582 loaves a day and the 150 turned-away figure is accurate. Using the amortised loan total (about €62,640) instead of €54,000 lengthens every payback by roughly 15%, which would push even the last row close to 113 days.

**What to check before Thursday**

- Proofer capacity: can the cabinet hold 72 loaves, or can a second proofing batch run without delaying the oven? Get a quote for that as well as the oven.
- Demand: where does the 150-a-day turned-away figure come from? If it is an estimate, measure it for a week (sales-outs and customer requests) before presenting it.
- Quote: include installation, commissioning, downtime and any ventilation or gas changes in the €54,000.
- Finance: state which payback basis the board uses (capital only or total repaid), and show both.

Bottom line: the oven may still be worth buying, but the four-month payback is not established. The current case overstates the gain by roughly 2 to 3 times, and it depends on a proofer upgrade Petra hasn't priced.
