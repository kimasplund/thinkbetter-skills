Short answer: no, not as written. Petra's case misses the proofer limit, counts VAT as profit, and understates the loan cost. Corrected, the oven pays back in about five to six months, not inside four. Here is her reasoning in order, with the corrections.

**1. Capacity (the main problem).** The D9 bakes 72 loaves per load, but the proofing cabinet holds 60. Dough can only go straight from proofer to oven if it fits, so each load of the D9 realistically carries 60 loaves, not 72. Current output is 48 × 9 = 432 loaves a day. With the D9 and the existing proofer it is 60 × 9 = 540 a day, an increase of 108 loaves, not 216. That still covers most of the 150 turned-away loaves, but leaves about 42 a day unserved. (If you also upgrade the proofer, the 216 figure becomes possible, but that cost is not in the €54,000.)

**2. Extra profit.** The €6.00 shelf price includes 10% VAT, so the bakery keeps €6.00 ÷ 1.10 = €5.45 per loaf. After the €1.70 ingredient and packaging cost, each extra loaf contributes about €3.75, not €6.00 (assuming the €1.70 is ex-VAT, which I could not confirm). On 108 extra loaves that is about €405 a day, not €900.

**3. Energy.** Petra's per-loaf comparison (7.2 vs 8.3 cents) is correct arithmetically, but the €7 a day saving is not a real saving. At 9 loads a day, energy goes from €36.00 (9 × €4.00) to €46.80 (9 × €5.20), an increase of €10.80 a day. The per-loaf improvement does not outweigh the extra loads' total cost.

**Net gain:** about €405 − €10.80 ≈ **€395 a day**, against Petra's €907.

**4. Financing.** Her €16,200 is simple interest on the full principal for five years. A standard amortising loan at 6% over 60 months has a monthly payment of about €1,044, so total repayments are about €62,640 and interest about €8,640. Her figure overstates interest, but the bigger point is that loan interest is not part of a payback test on the €54,000 outlay. The board should be told which basis it uses.

**5. Payback.** Assumptions: six trading days a week, and four months ≈ 17.3 weeks ≈ 104 trading days.
- On the €54,000 outlay alone: 54,000 ÷ 395 ≈ 137 trading days, about 23 weeks, or **5.3 months**. Over the limit by roughly 33 trading days.
- On total loan repayments (€62,640): about 159 trading days, or **6.1 months**.
- Best case, if all 150 turned-away loaves sell and the proofer is upgraded at no cost: about €563 − €11 ≈ €552 a day, so 54,000 ÷ 552 ≈ 98 trading days, or **3.8 months**. That only works if every assumption goes right.

Petra's "77 trading days" is 70,200 ÷ 907, which rests on the three errors above.

**Not included, and each would lengthen payback:** installation, changeover downtime, maintenance and service contracts, staff time, and any gap between demand you turn away and loaves that actually sell. The one item that could shorten payback is the resale or trade-in value of the current deck oven, which I don't have figures for.

**What I would take to the board:** the case does not meet the four-month test on current numbers. Before Thursday, ask Petra to (a) price a proofer upgrade alongside the oven, since the proofer is now the bottleneck, (b) re-run revenue net of VAT, (c) state whether the payback test uses the outlay or the financed cost, and (d) confirm that the turned-away demand is real and would sell at the shelf price. If the proofer upgrade is needed, add its cost and recalculate.
