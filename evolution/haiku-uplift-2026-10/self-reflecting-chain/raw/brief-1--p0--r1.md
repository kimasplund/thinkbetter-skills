Short answer: Petra's case does not hold up as written. Its arithmetic is mostly right step by step, but three of its inputs are wrong: it treats revenue as profit, it counts energy as a saving when it is a cost increase, and it ignores the 60-loaf proofer limit. Corrected, the oven may still pass the four-month test, but only under conditions that need checking before Thursday.

**Step by step**

1. **Capacity (648 loaves/day).** Arithmetic is right (9 × 72 = 648, up 216 on today's 432). But the proofer now holds 60 loaves, so a 72-loaf load cannot be fed from it. With the current proofer, output tops out at 540/day, up 108, not 216. Reaching 648 needs a proofer upgrade or a second proofing stage, and that cost is not in the €54,000.

2. **Extra profit (€900/day).** This is revenue, not profit, and it has two errors.
   - VAT: the €6.00 shelf price includes 10%, so net revenue per loaf is €5.45, and 150 loaves give €818, not €900.
   - Ingredients and packaging: €1.70 per loaf is ignored entirely. That is €255/day for 150 loaves.
   - Contribution from 150 extra loaves is therefore about €563/day, before energy.

3. **Energy (+€7/day saving).** The per-loaf figures are right (8.3c now, 7.2c on the D9). But the 1.1c saving is applied to all 648 loaves, including the 432 already baked, so it is not incremental. The real change is energy cost: 9 loads at €5.20 is €46.80/day against €36.00 now, an increase of about €10.80/day.

4. **Financing (€70,200 total).** The €16,200 interest is a flat 6% × 5 years, which is not how a monthly repayment loan works. On an amortising loan at 6%, the monthly payment is about €1,044, total repayments about €62,600, and interest about €8,600. Petra's method and her loan description are inconsistent. Her total is overstated, but the bigger question is which figure the board's four-month test should use: the €54,000 purchase price, or the cost including interest.

5. **Payback (77 trading days, about 13 weeks).** This uses the wrong daily figure (€907 instead of about €552, after the corrections above and at full 72-loaf capacity). It also mixes in interest, which I have not confirmed the board wants.

**Corrected figures** (assumes 150 extra loaves/day are sellable, 6 trading days a week, four months ≈ 104 trading days)

| Scenario | Net extra cash/day | Payback on €54,000 | Payback on total cost with interest (about €62,600) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 72/load, proofer upgraded, 150 sold | about €552 | about 98 trading days, roughly 3.8 months (pass, narrow) | about 113 days, roughly 4.4 months (fail) |
| 60/load, current proofer, 108 sold | about €395 | about 137 days, roughly 5.3 months (fail) | about 159 days, roughly 6.1 months (fail) |

To pass on €54,000 alone, the bakery needs about €519/day of net benefit, which is about 141 extra loaves sold per day at 72 per load. Including interest, it needs about 163 extra loaves per day. Demand of 150 is therefore very close to the break-even line.

**Assumptions I made**
- The 150 turned-away loaves a day are real, sold at full price across the three shops, and not already partly served by spare capacity.
- The oven runs 9 loads a day as stated, and the energy price per load stays at €4.00 today and €5.20 on the D9.
- Labour, maintenance, install costs, waste and any trade-in value are excluded because the brief gives no figures.
- The €54,000 may be ex-VAT. If the input VAT is recoverable, the true cash cost is lower, which would help the case. Please check this with your finance person.

**What to ask Petra for before Thursday**
- Rerun the case on incremental contribution: net price, ingredients and packaging, and the energy change. Drop the 1.1c-on-everything saving.
- A proofing plan that supports 72 loaves per load, with its cost, or a confirmed figure of 60 per load.
- Evidence for the 150 turned-away loaves a day, such as till or order logs by shop.
- A real loan quote (APR, term, monthly payment), and a decision on whether the board's test uses purchase price or total cost.
- Whether the €54,000 is ex-VAT, and whether delivery, installation and staff training are included.

**Bottom line for the board:** on the purchase price alone, with the full capacity upgrade and the demand figure holding, the oven passes four months, but narrowly. If the proofer is not upgraded, or the loan interest counts, it fails. I would not present the 13-week figure as it stands.
