Short answer: no, it doesn't hold up. Once the errors are fixed, payback is roughly 5.3 months (about 6 months if you count financing), not 13 weeks, so it misses the board's four-month test. Here is each step.

**Step 1: capacity (error)**
The D9 takes 72 loaves, but the proofing cabinet holds 60 and proofs in the same 40 minutes. Dough goes straight from proofer to oven, so each load is capped at 60.
- Current output: 48 x 9 = 432 loaves/day.
- With the D9: 60 x 9 = 540 loaves/day, up 108, not 216.
- That is still below the ~150 loaves of unmet demand, so all 108 should sell.
- The 648 figure only works if the proofer is also upgraded, and that cost isn't in the case.

**Step 2: profit per loaf (error)**
Petra counts the full €6.00 as profit. Two things are missing:
- The €6.00 includes 10% VAT, which goes to the tax authority. Net revenue is 6.00 / 1.10 = €5.45.
- Ingredients and packaging cost €1.70 a loaf.
- Margin is about €3.75 a loaf, before energy.
- 108 extra loaves x €3.75 = about €405/day, against the €900 claimed.

**Step 3: energy (error in direction)**
The per-loaf figures are right at full load: €4.00 / 48 = 8.3 cents now, and €5.20 / 72 = 7.2 cents on the D9. But the savings argument fails for two reasons.
- The proofer limit means the D9 runs at 60 per load, so its energy cost is €5.20 / 60 = 8.7 cents a loaf. That is worse than today, not better.
- Total energy spend rises. It goes from 9 x €4.00 = €36 to 9 x €5.20 = €46.80 a day, so +€10.80 a day.
- Petra books +€7 a day of saving. The correct figure is a cost of about €11 a day.

**Corrected daily gain:** about €405 - €11 = **€395/day**.

**Step 4: financing (error)**
Interest of 6% x €54,000 x 5 is simple interest on the full balance for five years. A standard monthly repayment loan amortises, so the balance falls each month.
- Monthly rate is 0.5% over 60 payments, so the payment is about €1,044.
- Total repaid is about €62,600, so interest is about €8,600, not €16,200.
- Petra's total cost of €70,200 is therefore overstated.
- Mixing financing into a payback calculation also isn't the usual approach. Payback is normally measured on the purchase price, with financing shown as a separate cash-flow point.

**Step 5: payback (arithmetic fine, inputs wrong)**
70,200 / 907 = 77 days is correct arithmetic, but both the numerator and denominator are off. Four months at six trading days a week is about 104 trading days.

| Basis | Cost | Days (at €395/day) | Months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price only | €54,000 | about 137 | about 5.3 |
| Including real interest | about €62,600 | about 159 | about 6.1 |

Both fail the four-month rule. To pass on the purchase price alone you'd need about €519/day of net gain.

**Possible rescue, to check before Thursday**
If a proofer upgrade to 72 loaves is cheap, the D9 could cover the full 150 loaves of unmet demand.
- Gain would be about 150 x €3.75 = €563/day, less extra energy, so roughly €550/day.
- Payback on the purchase price would be about 98 days, which passes.
- Adding the proofer cost, or financing interest, pushes it to roughly 105-115 days, which is borderline or fails.

**Other items missing from the case (assumptions, not checked)**
- Resale or scrap value of the old oven.
- Installation, ventilation, and any extra labour for the higher output.
- Whether the three shops can actually sell 108-150 more loaves a day at full price.
- Whether the "150 turned away" figure is a daily average or a peak.

**For the board:** present the corrected number, about 5 months or more, and say it doesn't meet the four-month rule as the case stands. If you want to make a case anyway, the route is a quoted, cheap proofer upgrade plus evidence of the extra demand. That would be an exception request, not a payback claim.
