**Short answer:** The most likely cause is that outpatient specimens at Ridgeway and Millbrook are sitting before they are centrifuged, and the cold-hold rule is chilling them. Chilled, unspun whole blood releases potassium from the cells, which inflates the result without any real change in the patient. The September 7 courier change is what made those waits long. Analyzer A and Tomas are unlikely to be the main cause, so quarantining the lot would probably not fix this.

**Why the timing fits**
- Before September 7, Ridgeway and Millbrook had a courier loop about every 90 minutes, so almost no specimen waited 30 minutes and the August 1 cold-hold rule rarely applied. Rates stayed near 1.5%.
- Since September 7 there has been one pickup a day at 3:30 pm. Morning draws now wait several hours, get put in the fridge under the cold-hold rule, and come back with high potassium.
- The time-of-day split fits. At those two sites, 11% of morning draws flag high against about 1% of afternoon draws.
- Harbour uses the same contractor but spins its samples on site before pickup, and it accounts for only 5% of the high results. The variable that matters is the delay before centrifugation, not the courier company.
- Hemolysis is normal on 93% of flagged samples. That does not rule out this mechanism, because cold-induced potassium leak happens without hemolysis. The two ED patients with normal repeat results fit the same picture.

**Why the other explanations are weaker**
- **Analyzer A lot:** Analyzer A runs about 60% of volume and 57% of flagged samples, so it is not over-represented. A 0.08 mmol/L shift that is still inside QC limits cannot by itself move the rate from 1.5% to 6%. The data do not support quarantine.
- **Tomas and the 23-gauge butterfly needles:** Millbrook has the same spike (39% of high results) and has no Tomas. Normal hemolysis argues against traumatic draws. Retraining may still be worth doing for draw quality, but it will not explain the outpatient rise.
- **The other two clinics (12% of high results):** I don't have their volumes or transport details, so I can't yet tell whether they are affected by the same handling delay.

**Assumptions I made:** Ridgeway and Millbrook specimens are going into the fridge after the change, and the cold-hold rule applies to potassium tubes as well.

**What to check before Friday**
1. Pull the draw-to-centrifuge time and the fridge-hold flag for flagged and unflagged specimens at Ridgeway and Millbrook since September 7. If the flagged ones are mostly fridged or waited more than about 2 to 3 hours, that is the cause.
2. Run a paired-tube test on a few morning draws. Split each sample: spin one tube within 30 minutes, hold the other per the current policy (fridge, then room temperature). Compare potassium. The fridged tube should read higher.
3. Re-run a set of retained flagged specimens on another analyzer or on the old reagent lot. If results match, the analyzer question is closed.
4. Compare Tomas's draws with other Ridgeway draws in the same time window. If there is no difference, drop retraining as the explanation.
5. Get transport and volume data for the other two clinics.

**What to bring to the meeting**
- A timeline: courier change on September 7, pickup times, and the flag rate by draw time at each site.
- The paired-tube result, once available.
- Recommendations:
  - Exempt potassium specimens from the cold-hold rule. Centrifuge on site, or hold at room temperature with a maximum time to spin.
  - Restore more frequent pickups at Ridgeway and Millbrook, add a midday pickup, or set up on-site spinning like Harbour's.
  - Pause recalls for high-potassium outpatient results from morning draws at those two sites. Retest from fresh draws instead.
  - Keep the Analyzer A lot in service while the cross-analyzer check runs. Quarantine only if that check shows a bias.

**Confidence:** About 75% that delayed, chilled handling is the main cause. Most of the remaining uncertainty is about the other two clinics and any analyzer bias, and steps 1 and 2 should resolve most of it.
