**Short answer:** This looks like a pre-analytical problem at the two sites that lost their own courier, not an analyzer or reagent problem. Outpatient specimens from Ridgeway and Millbrook are sitting for hours before centrifugation, and some of that time is in the clinic fridge. Potassium leaks out of cells when blood is cold, so the tube reads high while the patient's real potassium is normal. That fits both ED patients, who had normal repeats. I can't see your raw data, so this is the most likely explanation from the pattern you've described, not a confirmed one.

**Why the evidence points there**

- **Location:** Ridgeway and Millbrook produce 83% of the high results (44% + 39%) but send only about 30% of outpatient volume. Harbour is on the same contractor but spins samples on site before pickup, and its rate is low (5%). The contractor itself is not the problem. The unspun, cold wait is.
- **Time of day:** At Ridgeway and Millbrook, samples drawn before 11 am flag high 11% of the time, against about 1% after 1 pm. Morning samples are the ones waiting longest for a single 3:30 pm pickup, so they spend the most hours cold. This is the strongest clue you have.
- **Start date:** The cold-hold rule dates from August 1, but the rise starts September 8, the day after the courier change. Under the old 90-minute loop, samples reached the lab sooner, so cold exposure was short. One daily pickup turns a short cold wait into a long one.
- **Hemolysis:** Normal on 93% of flagged samples. That rules out the most common cause of a high potassium reading, red cells breaking in the tube, and leaves cold-induced leak as the better fit.
- **Inpatient and ED unchanged:** They run on the same analyzers and reagent lot, and their specimens are processed quickly. A reagent or analyzer fault would show up there too. This is the main reason the lot is unlikely to be the cause.
- **Analyzer A:** It runs about 60% of volume and 57% of flagged samples ran on it, so it is over-represented by nothing. The QC shift of 0.08 mmol/L is within limits. The evidence does not support quarantining the lot.
- **Tomas and butterfly needles:** Difficult draws can cause hemolysis or tube problems, but hemolysis is normal, so draw technique is not the main explanation. A quick check of tourniquet time and fist clenching is reasonable, since both raise local potassium, but neither explains the time-of-day effect or the Harbour contrast.
- **Calcium and sodium normal:** Consistent with a potassium-specific effect rather than a general sample or instrument fault.

**What to bring to Friday's quality meeting**

1. **Transit data for Ridgeway and Millbrook:** draw-to-receipt and receipt-to-centrifuge times, plus time in the clinic fridge, before and after September 7. Compare flagged with unflagged samples. This is the test that can confirm or reject the explanation.
2. **A paired cold-storage check:** At each of the two sites, draw paired tubes from a few patients. Hold one at room temperature and one refrigerated. Centrifuge at the same time points and measure potassium at about 0, 2, 4 and 6 hours. A clear rise in the refrigerated tube confirms the mechanism.
3. **Interim fix that does not need to wait:** Exempt potassium from the cold-hold rule, or have clinics spin samples before they go cold, as Harbour does. Ask the contractor for a second or earlier pickup at these two sites. Consider plasma (lithium heparin) for potassium if spinning on site is not possible.
4. **Recalled patients:** Recheck with a fresh sample processed promptly, and add a comment to outpatient potassium results when transit time is long.
5. **Analyzer A:** Keep the lot in service. Bring the QC trend and a comparison of Analyzer A results against the other analyzers to the meeting to show the lot is not the cause.
6. **Tomas:** Retraining is not supported by this evidence. Revisit only if hemolysis rises at Ridgeway.
7. **Clinicians:** Tell ordering physicians which outpatient potassium results are suspect and why, and that the two ED repeats support this.
