The most likely cause is pre-analytic: specimens sitting too long before analysis, with the cold-hold rule making it worse. The evidence points away from the analyzer and away from Tomas.

**Why I think it's the specimen handling**

- **Cold storage raises measured potassium in whole blood.** Chilling slows the sodium-potassium pump in red cells, so potassium leaks out. Samples held in a fridge for hours can read high even when the patient is normal. Your two ED patients with normal repeats fit this pattern.
- **The timing lines up with the courier change.** On September 7, Ridgeway and Millbrook moved from a 90-minute driver loop to one pickup at 3:30 pm. Specimens from those sites now wait many hours, and the August 1 cold-hold rule sends them to the fridge. Harbour uses the same contractor but spins samples on site, which cuts the wait. That matches Harbour being at only 5%.
- **The morning pattern fits too.** Samples drawn before 11 am at Ridgeway and Millbrook have the longest wait before the 3:30 pickup, so they spend the most time cold. That is 11% flagged against about 1% for afternoon draws.
- **Hemolysis is normal on 93% of flagged samples.** That argues against a bad draw causing real potassium release, and it is the reason to look at storage time instead.

**Why the analyzer lot and Tomas are less likely**

- **Analyzer A:** It runs about 60% of volume and carries 57% of flags. That is roughly proportional, so the lot is not over-represented. A QC mean shift of 0.08 mmol/L is inside limits and too small to explain a jump from 1.5% to 6%. Quarantining the lot is hard to justify on this data. It is still worth checking whether flags on A differ from flags on the other analyzer, but only after you split by collection-to-analysis time.
- **Tomas:** Butterfly needles and difficult draws can cause hemolysis or a discarded first tube, but hemolysis is normal here. Retraining him does not address the cold-storage mechanism. A draw-quality review can run in parallel without blaming him.

**What I would bring to Friday's meeting**

1. **Pull per-sample data for September 8–30 and August:** collection time, receipt time, analysis time, whether the tube was refrigerated, and the analyzer. Compare flag rates by time-to-analysis and by refrigeration status. This is the single most useful analysis, and it can confirm or rule out the hypothesis within a day.
2. **Run a paired test if you can.** Split a few draws from the same patient, keep one at room temperature and one in the fridge, and measure potassium at about 0, 2, 4, and 6 hours. Also run a few on the other analyzer. This gives a direct answer.
3. **Look at the specimen type.** If the lab uses serum, clotting time adds to the effect. Lithium heparin plasma is less affected by cold, so compare tube types for the same draw.
4. **Interim containment, which does not need a quarantine:** consider exempting potassium-sensitive whole-blood specimens from the cold-hold rule, or set a maximum wait before analysis. Ask the courier contractor for an earlier or second daily pickup at Ridgeway and Millbrook, or have the phlebotomy team bring specimens to a central spin point. Confirm with the clinical leads that the recall list can be narrowed to flagged patients with a time-to-analysis over the threshold.
5. **Hold the lot decision and the Tomas retraining** until the data in step 1 is back. If the data points to storage time, both actions are unnecessary. If it still points to Analyzer A after the time split, quarantine the lot then.
