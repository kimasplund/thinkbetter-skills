Short answer: don't sign with Hollis in its current form. Three of your existing constraints block it as offered, and a 15 kg roaster can't arrive before a February start. The better three-year path is to grow margin and non-Alto Merida volume first, and to renegotiate the Hollis deal rather than sign it by December.

**Why Hollis doesn't work as offered**

I've assumed your 21 tonnes is roasted output, that 5 kg is the green charge per batch (about 4.25 kg roasted after the 15% loss), and that you roast about 50 weeks a year. Please check these, because the numbers move with them.

1. **Your importer cap blocks it.** The contract caps Alto Merida green coffee at 26 t a year until 2028. You're already buying roughly 21 t roasted, which is about 25 t green, so you have about 1 t of headroom. Hollis needs about 18 t roasted, or roughly 21 t green, so you'd be about 20 t over the cap. Even if you read the 18 t as green, you'd still be over. Any Alto Merida growth (including Varnland, subscriptions or a café) draws on the same 26 t.

2. **Your roasting hours can't absorb it.** At 20 minutes a batch over 10 hours a day, five days a week, the machine runs at most about 150 batches a week, or roughly 7,200 to 7,500 a year. You're probably already running about two-thirds of that. Hollis adds around 4,200 batches, which takes you to roughly 9,000 a year, about 20% over the ceiling. A contract roaster would fix this, but not the cap or the café clause below.

3. **Your café promises conflict with it.** Your café agreements say you won't sell the Alto Merida blend through supermarkets in the county. Hollis has six stores in your county, so a private-label Alto Merida line there would very likely breach that promise, and a solicitor should confirm it. Also, if you free up green by cutting café volume to supply Hollis, the profit gain shrinks, because you'd be swapping one customer for another.

4. **Organic certification.** Your certificate covers your unit only. A contract roaster would need its own organic certification, and the Hollis product would need to be covered by that certificate. Check this before any contract-roasting option.

5. **Timing.** A 15 kg roaster costs £68,000 and takes seven months, so from October it would arrive around May, after a February start. Check the lease and the unit's power, gas and ventilation before ordering anything this size, since the 8 am to 6 pm rule probably applies to it too.

**Growth options, roughly in order**

- **Web subscriptions.** Probably your best first move. Subscribers are recurring, high-margin, and can be served from existing roasting. Each kilogram of Alto Merida sold this way still counts against the 26 t cap, so keep an eye on it.
- **Price and mix.** Raising prices or shifting volume toward higher-margin formats often beats adding tonnes. This needs no capacity or cap headroom.
- **Own café.** Possible, but it's capital-heavy and it's a second business to run. It also needs care with your 40 café customers, since it may compete with them. Treat it as a later option.
- **Varnland distributor.** Attractive only if it uses a different origin or a blend outside the cap. Check whether your café agreements cover Varnland before you commit.
- **Contract roaster.** Useful as a bridge for any volume you do win, but it needs certification and doesn't solve the cap.
- **Bigger roaster.** Only worth buying once you have volume the cap allows and the unit can host it. Right now the cap is the constraint, not the machine.

**What I'd do before December**

1. Tell Faye not to sign the current terms. Better to lose the deal than to breach the importer contract or the café promises.
2. Get a solicitor to read the café agreements, the importer contract's cap wording (does it cover green bought for private label, and is there a consent route for more?), and the Hollis contract.
3. Go back to Hollis with a counter-offer. Options: a different origin or blend for the organic range (this avoids both the cap and the café clause); a smaller Alto Merida volume that fits your headroom; a later start date; or a letter of intent with conditions (cap consent, café clearance, certification). A buyer who wants organic private label should accept a reasonable counter. If they walk away, you've lost a deal you couldn't have delivered cleanly.
4. Ask your importer now whether they'd lift the cap, even partly. The answer tells you how much Alto Merida growth is possible before 2028.
