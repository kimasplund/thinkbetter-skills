**Short answer:** Don't sign the Hollis deal as written, and don't let the December deadline push you into it. As offered, it conflicts with your green coffee cap, your roasting capacity and your café contracts. All three can probably be worked around, but only if you renegotiate. My advice is to go back to Hollis with a smaller, different deal, and to grow subscriptions and café wholesale in the meantime.

## Why the deal doesn't work as written

I've assumed your 21 t is roasted output and that the 5 kg is the green charge per batch. The conclusions hold if either assumption is off by a little.

**1. The green coffee cap.**
- Roasting loses about 15%, so 1 t of roasted coffee needs about 1.18 t of green.
- Your current 21 t needs about 24.7 t of green. You are already within about 1.3 t of the 26 t cap.
- Hollis's 18 t needs about 21.2 t of green. Together the two come to about 46 t of green against a cap of 26 t.
- If you took Hollis and nothing else, you'd have about 4.8 t of green left. That is about 4 t of roasted coffee for 40 cafés and the web shop, down from about 21 t.
- The cap runs to 2028 and the Hollis deal runs three years from February, so it covers at least the first two years.
- So you would have to drop most of your existing customers to serve Hollis. That doesn't double profit, and it swaps loyal accounts for one buyer who can walk away.

**2. Roasting capacity.**
- At 5 kg per 20 minutes in a 10-hour weekday window, the theoretical limit is 30 batches a day. Allowing for cooling, cleaning and loading, 20 to 24 is more realistic.
- Your 24.7 t of green is about 4,900 batches a year, which is already around 80% or more of realistic capacity.
- Adding 21 t of green means about 4,200 more batches. That is not possible on one machine under your lease hours.
- The 15 kg roaster costs £68,000 and takes seven months to arrive. If you ordered today it would land around May, after the February start, and you would still need space, power or gas, and landlord consent.

**3. Your café agreements.**
- You promised the cafés you wouldn't sell Alto Merida through supermarkets in the county. Hollis has six stores in the county.
- Signing as offered would likely breach those agreements. That puts your core wholesale base at risk, and it is the base that gives you stable margins.
- Check the exact wording with a solicitor. Does it cover private-label products? Does it cover a differently named blend?

**4. Organic certification.**
- The certificate covers your unit. A contract roaster would need to be certified organic itself, and your certifier would have to approve the arrangement.
- Private-label packs for Hollis's organic range also need certifier sign-off on the labelling and the supply chain.
- A contract roaster does not fix the green cap either. It only fixes capacity.

## What I'd do

**Counter-offer to Hollis (the buyer leaves in December, so open talks now):**
- **A different product.** Offer a separate organic blend or origin for them, not Alto Merida. That avoids the café exclusivity and spreads your importer risk. It also needs its own green supply, so ask your importer or a second organic supplier for a separate allocation.
- **A smaller volume.** Start around 4 to 6 t a year, with options to step up. Check that this still pays at their price after the cost of green coffee and any contract roasting.
- **A later start.** Propose a staged ramp from spring or summer, once supply and capacity are sorted.
- **Protections for you.** Ask for volume commitments or minimum purchase terms, a break clause, and price review linked to green coffee costs. A three-year private-label deal is hard to exit, and they can switch roaster at renewal.
- **Do the profit sums on the real numbers.** "Roughly double our profit" assumes you can supply 18 t without losing your current sales. Redo it for the smaller deal, including contract roaster fees and extra green cost.

**Alongside that:**
- **Ask your importer** whether the cap can be lifted or a second allocation added. That is the real ceiling on Alto Merida growth.
- **Talk to your certifier** about the contract roasting and private-label requirements.
- **Take legal advice** on the café clause. If Hollis really is the best option, you could ask the affected cafés for a written waiver, perhaps with something in return such as pricing or exclusivity on a premium lot.
- **Contract roaster** is a sensible bridge for extra volume only if it is certified organic and the green supply is solved. It's cheaper and faster than a £68,000 machine with a seven-month wait. Buy the 15 kg roaster only once volumes are proven and your landlord agrees.

## Other growth paths

- **Web subscriptions:** This is the best fit for you. Margins are usually higher than wholesale, it doesn't touch the café clause, and it needs little extra capacity. It can also use the green headroom you have. It won't double profit quickly, but it is low risk.
- **Café wholesale:** Adding a few more cafés outside the supermarket issue is safe, but you are capped on green coffee.
- **Varnland distributor:** This has the same cap problem for Alto Merida, plus export logistics, organic import rules and payment risk. Park it for now.
- **Your own café:** This means a different business with new capital, staff and lease, and it competes with your wholesale customers. Not for the next three years.

## Bottom line

Don't sign the 18 t Alto Merida deal. Counter with a smaller private-label deal using a different organic blend, a later start and exit protections. Ask your importer for more green allocation, and grow web subscriptions in the meantime. If Hollis won't move, losing the deal is better than breaching your café agreements and running out of green coffee in the first year.

One caution. I haven't seen your contracts or your margins, so check the café clause and the importer cap wording with a solicitor before you reply to Hollis.
