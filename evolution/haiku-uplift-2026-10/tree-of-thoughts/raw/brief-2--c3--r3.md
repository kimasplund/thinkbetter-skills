Short answer: on the figures you've given, the proposals that can actually be done are the dry slope on the lower land and a renegotiated long-term lease on the upper slopes. The consultant's snowmaking plan breaks your water licence as written, and the bike park breaks the lease as written. Whichever direction the board picks, securing the upper-slope lease is the first thing to do, because almost every option depends on it.

**Snowmaking (€2.1M, consultant's favourite)**

- 9 ha × 4,500 m³/ha = 40,500 m³ per season. Your licence allows 40,000 m³, so the consultant's plan alone exceeds it before the existing guns are counted.
- The beginner-area guns used 31,000 m³ last winter. That leaves 9,000 m³ of headroom, enough for about 2 ha at 4,500 m³/ha, not 9 ha.
- Even if the beginner guns were folded into one new system, the total would have to stay under 40,000 m³, which is about 4,400 m³/ha across the whole 9 ha. That leaves no margin.
- Action: ask the consultant to re-run the plan against the licence. Ask the water authority whether the licence can be increased or whether the abstraction can be averaged across seasons. Treat the €2.1M figure as unreliable until then.
- Funding: the lower land is yours, so the tourism fund's 50% would apply. That would cut a €2.1M project to about €1.05M, but only for a scheme that fits the licence.

**Summer bike park (€850k)**

- The upper slopes are leased under a clause limiting use to winter sports between 1 December and 15 April. A summer bike park on those slopes is not permitted under the lease as it stands. This is the most serious blocker. You'd need the Grazing Commons Trust to amend that clause in writing before spending anything.
- Funding: the lease was renewed for ten years in 2025, so about ten years remain. The tourism fund excludes leased land unless at least 15 years remain. The bike park would therefore get no grant and would cost the full €850k.
- The consultant's claim that it could double annual revenue is unsupported in what you've given me. It also depends on the Summit chairlift, which sits on the same leased land.

**Dry slope and tubing lane (€1.4M)**

- This is on land you own, so it is eligible for the 50% grant. Net cost is about €700k.
- It needs no water, so it avoids the licence problem entirely.
- It doesn't bring back snow sports, but it is the only option here that is grant-eligible, licence-safe and on land you control.

**Upper-only operation with shuttle and €600k chairlift refit**

- Upper slopes produced 71 skiable days last winter, so this is your strongest asset. It is also the one you are at risk of losing.
- The lease has about ten years left, so the operation would run to the lease's end with no security beyond it. The chairlift refit (€600k) would be spent on leased land with no grant, because of the 15-year rule.
- The shuttle from the car park is an operating cost you haven't costed.

**Ardane Resorts approach**

- The 2019 buyout grant (€1.2M) must be repaid if the land is sold within ten years of 2019. That window closes in 2029. A sale before then would trigger repayment on top of whatever Ardane pays, so any offer needs to be assessed net of that.
- A sale after 2029 avoids repayment, but the community would lose control of the hill.

**Recommendation**

1. Open talks with the Grazing Commons Trust now. You need a longer lease (ideally 15 years or more, which would make the upper slopes grant-eligible) and, if the bike park is wanted, an amendment to the winter-only clause.
2. Re-run the snowmaking plan against the 40,000 m³ licence, or drop it to the roughly 2 ha that fits the headroom.
3. If the Trust won't extend, the upper-only plan has the best skiing and the least capital at risk, but you would be running a business with a fixed end date. Keep the Ardane approach open but defer it until the 2029 window closes.
4. The dry slope is the only proposal that is fully fundable and water-free today. It is a reasonable diversification, but it won't by itself bring back the lower-run skiing.

**Assumptions**

- The 9 ha and the beginner area are part of the lower runs.
- The 40,000 m³ licence is per season and not averaged.
- The 15-year rule applies from the date the grant is decided, so a lease extension signed before that date would count.
- The consultant's 4,500 m³/ha figure is the only water estimate available. If a more accurate figure is available, the snowmaking numbers should be redone.
