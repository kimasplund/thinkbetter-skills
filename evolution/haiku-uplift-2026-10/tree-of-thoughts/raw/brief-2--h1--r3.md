**Short answer:** As written, neither of the two options your members are arguing about works. Each one fails on a constraint that is already in your own paperwork. The direction with the best chance of still being here in ten years is a bike-park-led, upper-mountain plan. That plan is conditional on renegotiating the Grazing Commons Trust lease first. I would make that negotiation the main item for the November vote.

## Why snowmaking fails

- **Water.** The consultant's own figure is about 4,500 m³ per hectare. Across 9 hectares that is about 40,500 m³ a season. This is already over your whole 40,000 m³ licence, before anything else is counted.
- **The existing guns.** The beginner-area guns used 31,000 m³ last winter. Adding the lower runs would take total demand to roughly 71,500 m³, nearly double the licence. You would need a new or expanded abstraction licence. That is a regulatory risk the €2.1M price does not cover, and I would not assume it will be granted.
- **Climate risk.** The lower runs had 18 skiable days against 71 on the upper runs. Snowmaking at 650–900 m depends on enough cold hours, and those are the hours that are disappearing. You would be putting €2.1M into the least reliable part of the hill.
- **Funding.** It is the one big project on land you own, so it would qualify for the 50% grant. That makes it tempting, but a grant does not fix the water problem.

## Why the bike park fails as proposed

- **The lease clause.** Your lease limits you to winter sports between 1 December and 15 April. A summer bike park on the upper slopes breaks that clause, so it needs a written amendment from the Trust. Check this with your solicitor.
- **The grant.** The tourism fund excludes assets on leased land unless at least 15 years remain. You renewed for ten years in 2025, so about nine remain. The upper-slope bike park trails, and probably the chairlift work, would not be eligible. You would be paying the full €850k yourselves.
- **The revenue claim.** "Could double annual revenue" is the consultant's estimate. Ask for the assumptions: visitor numbers, price, season length and operating costs. Bike parks need staffing, insurance and trail maintenance, so revenue is not the same as margin.

## The other options

- **Dry slope and tubing (€1.4M).** It is on land you own, so it is grant-eligible. A 50% grant would cut your share to about €700k. It works in all seasons and does not depend on snow or the lease. The risk is that dry slopes and tubing lanes are modest earners and are not a destination on their own. I would treat it as a possible supporting piece, not the main plan.
- **Upper-only operation with a €600k Summit refit.** This fits the data best, because the upper runs are where the snow is (71 days). But the chairlift is on leased land, so it has the same grant problem. It also leaves the lower land, which you own, with nothing to do. It could be combined with selling or developing the lower land, which is where the Ardane approach could matter.
- **Ardane.** The €1.2M buyout grant is repayable if the land is sold within ten years of 2019, so until about 2029. Repayment would come out of any sale price. Community ownership would also end. I would not entertain this seriously until the other options have been properly tested. If the board wants to know what Ardane would pay, it can listen without committing, but I would not treat it as a plan.

## What I would do

1. **Open lease talks with the Trust now.** Ask for two things. First, an extension so that at least 15 years remain, which is to roughly 2041 or later. That should make the upper-slope assets grant-eligible. Second, an amendment allowing summer recreation, such as biking, outside the winter window. The Trust may want something in return, such as a rent increase, trail maintenance or a share of revenue. Whatever it asks, this is a far cheaper way to unlock money than any of the capital projects.
2. **Make the plan conditional.** If the Trust agrees, go with the bike park and the upper-mountain operation. That is roughly €850k plus up to €600k for the chairlift, with grants of up to 50% on eligible costs, so your net spend could be about €725k. If the Trust refuses, the bike park and the upper-only plan both lose their funding, and you would fall back on the dry slope and tubing on your own land, with a smaller upper operation. The board should vote for that sequence, not for a single project.
3. **Check the numbers before the vote.** Get the consultant's revenue and cost assumptions for the bike park. Confirm with the fund that the 50% rate and the 15-year rule apply as I have read them. Ask your solicitor to read the lease clause and the buyout-grant terms.
4. **Drop large-scale lower-run snowmaking** unless a water licence increase is confirmed in writing. If members still want some snowmaking, ask the consultant for a scaled version that fits within the 40,000 m³ licence alongside the existing 31,000 m³, which leaves about 9,000 m³.

## Assumptions

I assumed the 15-year test is counted from the grant decision, that the lease terms are as you describe, and that the 4,500 m³ per hectare figure is a seasonal total. If any of these is wrong, the conclusions on funding and water would change. Please check them with the fund and the consultant.

**Bottom line:** the members arguing for snowmaking and for the bike park can both be right about what they want. Neither can be funded or permitted on the current facts. A longer lease with summer use on the upper slopes is the step that makes the best option possible.
