**Short answer:** don't sign the Hollis deal as written, and don't let the December deadline push you into it. As offered, it can't be delivered, and part of it would breach your café agreements. There is a version you could sign, and I describe it below. For the three years, the best growth is a Hollis deal on a different blend plus web subscriptions, in that order of effort.

## Why the deal as offered doesn't work

**1. The green coffee cap rules it out on its own.**
- Roasting loses about 15% of weight, so your 21 tonnes roasted already uses about 24.7 tonnes of green.
- Your importer caps Alto Merida at 26 tonnes until 2028. That leaves about 1.3 tonnes of green, or roughly 1.1 tonnes roasted, of headroom.
- Hollis wants 18 tonnes roasted, which is about 21.2 tonnes of green. Together with your current business that is about 46 tonnes of green against a 26 tonne cap.
- A bigger roaster or a contract roaster doesn't help, because neither one creates more Alto Merida beans.
- Taking the Hollis volume means starving your cafés or breaking the importer contract.

**2. It breaches your café agreements.**
- You promised the cafés you wouldn't sell the Alto Merida blend through supermarkets in the county.
- Six of Hollis's 30 stores are in your county.
- Signing as written puts your wholesale base at risk. That base is most of your current profit, and "doubling" it is not worth losing it.
- Read the exact wording with a solicitor. It may be possible to supply only the 24 stores outside the county, or to ask the cafés for a waiver in return for something such as exclusivity on a premium blend. Don't assume either will work.

**3. You can't roast it in time anyway.**
- Your 5 kg machine can run about 3 batches an hour inside your 8 am to 6 pm weekday window. That is a ceiling of roughly 30 batches a day, or about 37 tonnes of green a year.
- That ceiling is theoretical. Cooling, cleaning, and gaps between batches cut it, and you use about two thirds of it already.
- Adding 21 tonnes of green on top of your current 24.7 doesn't fit.
- The 15 kg roaster has a seven-month lead time. Ordering now means it arrives around May or June at best, and then there is installation, a lease check on power and extraction, and commissioning. February is out of reach.
- A contract roaster would have to hold organic certification, because your certificate covers only your own unit. You would also be handing them your core product and your margin.

## What I would do instead

**Counter-offer to Hollis before December.** Faye is right that the buyer's departure sets a deadline. But a buyer who wants a three-year organic private-label range will usually negotiate, and a deal they can't be supplied under is worse for them as well. Propose:
- **A different organic blend, not Alto Merida.** Source it from a second importer or origin, with its own green contract. This removes the cap problem and the café clause (which names only the Alto Merida blend) at the same time. Check that your café agreements say nothing broader, such as "any blend we sell to cafés".
- **A phased volume.** For example 5 to 6 tonnes roasted in year one, rising as capacity arrives. That is about 6 to 7 tonnes of green, which your current machine can just about absorb at roughly 28 tonnes total on the rota, and it gives you time to decide on equipment.
- **A later start**, such as April or May, which also gives you time to set up the second green supply.
- **A review clause** tied to volume and green price, so you aren't locked into a fixed price for three years while green coffee costs move.

**Check the numbers before you commit.** "Roughly double our profit" is based on their price at 18 tonnes of Alto Merida. Recalculate at the phased volume, with the new green cost, and with either the extra shifts or a contract roaster included. If the margin only works at full volume on your existing blend, the deal isn't as good as it looks.

**Equipment decision.** Don't order the £68,000 roaster on the strength of a verbal deal. Order it only once Hollis has signed a contract with committed volumes, or if the numbers justify it without them. If you need extra capacity in the meantime, a certified organic contract roaster for the overflow is a short-term bridge, not a three-year plan.

## The other growth routes, briefly

- **Web subscriptions (do this alongside Hollis).** It needs no new machine and doesn't touch the café clause. It sells at a much higher margin per kilo than wholesale or supermarket pricing. The limit is the roughly 1.1 tonnes of Alto Merida headroom, so feed growth with a second origin as well. This is the safest source of profit growth.
- **Varnland distributor.** The same Alto Merida cap applies, and export brings extra paperwork and organic certification questions. Revisit it after 2028, or with the second blend.
- **Your own café.** It takes capital and your time, and adds a different business to run. It doesn't use much green coffee, but nothing here makes it the best use of the next three years.
- **Contract roaster for the Hollis volume.** This is a bridge, not a plan. It solves the machine constraint but none of the bean or café constraints.

## What I'd do this month

1. Ask your importer whether the cap can be raised, and what a second origin would cost. A higher cap would change the picture.
2. Have the café agreements checked for the exact scope of the supermarket clause.
3. Tell Hollis you want to supply them, but under a revised structure: a different blend, phased volume, later start.
4. Start recalculating the profit case at the phased volume.

## Assumptions

- I don't have the exact wording of your café agreements or the importer contract. If either allows more than I've assumed, the picture improves.
