**Short answer:** neither side of the split is right as the proposals stand. The two leading options each hit a hard constraint in your own paperwork. ## Why the two favourites fail as written

**Snowmaking on the lower runs**
- 9 ha at about 4,500 m³/ha is roughly 40,500 m³ a season. Your licence allows 40,000 m³ in total, and the beginner-area guns already used 31,000 m³ last winter.
- That leaves about 9,000 m³ of headroom, which covers about 2 ha, not 9. The consultant's plan only works if the licence is raised, and that is a regulatory decision outside your control, with no guarantee and probably a long timeline.
- Water is also the weak point in a warming-sensitive plan. The lower runs had 18 skiable days. Snowmaking needs cold nights as well as water, so it will not turn 18 days into a reliable season on its own.
- The money is the one good part. You own the land, so the tourism fund should pay 50%, which brings the €2.1M down to about €1.05M. That only matters if the water problem is solved first.

**Summer bike park on the upper slopes**
- The lease has two problems.
  - It was renewed for ten years in 2025, so about 8.5 years remain. The fund excludes assets on leased land unless at least 15 years remain, so the €850k would get no grant and would be paid in full.
  - The lease limits you to winter sports between 1 December and 15 April. A summer bike park is outside what you are currently allowed to do.
- You would be spending €850k of your own money on trails and lift operation on land where the landlord could say no to the use, and where you hold the lease for a shorter time than the investment needs to pay back.
- The consultant's "double annual revenue" is a forecast. Ask for the visitor numbers, season length and operating costs behind it before anyone leans on it.
- The idea is not bad. It is blocked, and it can be unblocked only by the Trust.

## The other three

- **Dry slope and tubing on the lower land (€1.4M).** You own the land, so it should qualify for the 50% grant, leaving about €700k. It does not depend on snow or on the water licence. It can run in all seasons and it uses the part of the hill that currently earns only 18 days a year. Check the grant rules for dry slopes and tubing, and check what a dry slope earns in practice, since these facilities are usually modest earners rather than game changers.
- **Upper-only operation (€600k chairlift refit plus a shuttle).** The upper runs give you 71 skiable days, which is where the winter income is. The refit is on leased land, so it gets no grant. It would also make you more dependent on one lease that ends in 2035, which is almost exactly your ten-year horizon. Treat the chairlift work as maintenance you will probably need anyway, not as a strategy.
- **Sale to Ardane.** The €1.2M buyout grant has to be repaid if the land is sold within ten years of the 2019 purchase, so any sale before 2029 starts roughly €1.2M in the hole. The approach is informal, so you have no price. Keep the contact warm, but do not let it set the timetable.

## What I would put to the board in November

1. **Open lease talks with the Grazing Commons Trust now.** Ask for two things: an extension to at least 15 years remaining (so upper-slope projects become grant-eligible), and a variation of the winter-only clause to allow summer use. This costs very little and decides whether the bike park is real. Do it before committing money to anything on the upper slopes.
2. **Commit to the lower-land project that does not depend on snow or the lease: the dry slope and tubing lane,** applying for the 50% grant first. Make approval of the grant a condition of going ahead.
3. **Keep the upper winter operation alive on a minimum-spend basis.** Do the chairlift work that safety and reliability require, and defer the full €600k refit and shuttle until the lease position is clear.
4. **Treat snowmaking as a small, staged option, not the flagship.** With the current licence you could cover roughly 2 ha of the lower runs, a learner strip close to the base. Use that if it supports the tubing and learning offer. Separately, ask the licensing authority what it would take to raise the abstraction limit. Do not approve the €2.1M scheme until that answer is in writing.
5. **Make the bike park a conditional second phase.** If the Trust agrees to a longer lease and summer use, the project becomes grant-eligible and much less risky. It can then be reconsidered with proper revenue figures.
6. **Do not sell before 2029,** unless the offer clearly beats the repayment and what the community loses. By then you will know whether the lease talks and the lower-land project worked.

## Why this gives the best chance of surviving ten years

It follows where you hold secure rights. Your strongest asset is the land you own, which carries grant eligibility and no landlord. Your biggest vulnerability is one leased upper slope and a ten-year lease that expires just as the plan matures. The options that look biggest (snowmaking, the bike park) are each blocked by a constraint someone else controls: the water licence and the Trust. The recommended plan spends your money first where you control the outcome, and uses the lease negotiation to find out whether the bigger ideas are possible.

## What would change this advice

- The Trust refuses to extend or vary the lease. Then the bike park is dead, and the upper-only operation carries real risk after 2035. Focus entirely on the lower land and look harder at sale terms.
- The licensing authority will raise the water limit quickly. Then full snowmaking becomes much more attractive, with the 50% grant.
- The consultant cannot support the bike-park revenue numbers, or the dry slope and tubing economics look weaker than assumed.
