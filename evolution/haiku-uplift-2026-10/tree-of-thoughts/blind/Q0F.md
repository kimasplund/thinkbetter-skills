**Short answer: don't sign the Hollis deal as offered.** It conflicts with two of your existing commitments, and at 18 tonnes it would take you well past the importer's cap. A modified version could still work, and the bigger roaster is probably the right growth step regardless.

## Why the Hollis offer doesn't fit as proposed

**Your café agreements.** You promised the cafés you won't sell the Alto Merida blend through supermarkets in the county. Hollis has six stores in your county, so signing would likely put you in breach with some of your 40 wholesale accounts. Losing them would wipe out much of the profit the Hollis deal is meant to add.

**Your importer's cap.** The contract limits you to 26 tonnes of Alto Merida green coffee a year until 2028. Assuming your 21 tonnes is roasted weight, that is about 24.7 tonnes green after a 15% loss, so you have roughly 1.3 tonnes of headroom today. Hollis's 18 tonnes roasted is about 21 tonnes green, which would bring the total to roughly 46 tonnes green. That breaches the cap by a wide margin. Contract roasting doesn't avoid this, because the cap applies to the green coffee you buy, not to who roasts it.

**Capacity.** On your 5 kg machine, a 20-minute batch yields about 4.25 kg roasted. Running 20 batches across a 10-hour weekday window gives roughly 85 kg a day, or about 21 tonnes over 250 working days. You are already close to the machine's ceiling, so 18 more tonnes can't be roasted in-house on the current setup.

**Timing.** A 15 kg roaster has a seven-month lead time. If you order this month it arrives around May, after Hollis's February start. A contract roaster could cover the gap, but it doesn't solve the cap or the café clause.

I'm assuming the 21 tonnes is roasted output and the importer cap is on green weight. If either is wrong, the numbers shift, but the café clause and the cap would still need checking.

## What I'd do over the next three years

1. **Get a solicitor to read the café agreements and the importer contract this month,** before anyone signs anything. Ask specifically whether a private-label deal breaks the county clause, and whether the importer allows any increase before 2028.

2. **Tell Hollis you're interested in a different product.** The strongest version of the deal is a private-label organic blend from a different origin, or a different blend, rather than Alto Merida. That sidesteps both the café clause and the importer cap. Your organic certificate covers your unit, so check whether it also needs to cover the new blend or private-label packaging. Ask Hollis for a short extension past December. A buyer who wants a real supply deal should accept a few weeks' delay.

3. **Order the 15 kg roaster now, if the unit and lease allow it.** Check the floor loading, ventilation, and smoke or chaff requirements, and confirm the 8 am to 6 pm weekday limit still works. At similar cycle times, a 15 kg machine roughly triples your output, to something like 60 tonnes a year on paper. That gives you room for the web subscriptions and new café accounts, plus a second product line.

4. **Grow within the 26-tonne cap.** Build web subscriptions and new cafés on Alto Merida up to the cap, which leaves little room for Hollis-scale volume on that coffee. Put the growth in other products and accounts. Plan the 2028 renewal with the importer early, because that's when a larger Alto Merida allocation becomes possible.

5. **Treat the distributor in Varnland and the contract roaster as secondary.** Both are worth a look only if the new contracts and cap allow it. The café clause could also apply to a distributor, so check that before any agreement. Opening your own café adds a lot of operating risk and doesn't address the roasting bottleneck.

**Assumptions to check:** that the café clause covers private-label sales through Hollis, that the importer cap is green weight, that a 15 kg roaster runs on a similar cycle, and that the unit can take it. The first two decide whether the Hollis deal is viable in any form.
