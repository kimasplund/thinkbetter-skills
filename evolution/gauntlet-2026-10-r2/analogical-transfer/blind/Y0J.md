**Short answer:** As proposed, no. Her programme would not fix your problem, and parts of it could make it worse. A reworked version of her idea would work, because paying people to use less water when supply is short is a sound instrument. What needs to change is the design, because your valley differs from a power grid in ways that matter.

## Where the grid analogy holds and where it breaks

Demand response works on a grid because power can't be stored, the shortage lasts hours, and the system can react within minutes. Your situation is the opposite on each count.

- **You have a store and a long lag.** Tarrow is a reservoir, and a release takes 3 days to reach the Lower intake and up to 5 to reach the end of the canal. An hourly price can't steer anything. By the time a grower reacts to a price, the water that matters was released days earlier. Any signal has to go out at least 5 days ahead, and a weekly rhythm fits better than an hourly one.
- **Your shortage is a season, not a peak.** An 18% deficit across July and August is a sustained shortfall in total volume, not a spike to shave. The grid precedent that fits is a hydro-dependent system in a dry year. Those systems use forward-contracted reductions and quotas agreed before the dry spell. They don't use spot prices.
- **Your shortage has a position.** On a grid, a megawatt saved anywhere helps everywhere. In your valley, whether a cubic metre saved helps the tail depends on where it is saved. That is the biggest flaw in the proposal.

## What the geography does to her programme

**Savings by Upper Canal growers are worth about 60% of their face value to the Lower Canal.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back above the Lower intake. So if an orchard cuts 1 m³, the Lower intake gains roughly 0.6 m³, because the orchard's drainage return shrinks too. Paying a flat rate per cubic metre overpays Upper growers by about two-thirds relative to what they deliver downstream.

**A rough scale check, using my assumptions.** I don't have your per-hectare water use, so I assumed it is similar across both canals, which is probably generous to the orchards. On that basis the Lower Canal uses about 1.6 times what the Upper does (6,800 ha against 4,200 ha). An 18% shortfall on the Lower Canal would then need the Upper Canal to cut about half of its own diversion, since only 60% of each cut reaches the Lower intake. Orchard trees can't absorb that. Cutting during a critical fruit-sizing or pre-harvest period risks the crop, and a lost season can damage the trees themselves. Replace my assumption with your metered figures before the board meets. Even if I'm off by a third, the Upper Canal can be a partial lever, not the main one.

**Tail shortage may be a distribution problem as much as a supply problem.** The tail farms took nearly all of the shortfall. That suggests the sharing rule inside the Lower Canal puts the shortage on whoever sits last, whatever the river carries. If so, the largest and cheapest improvement is a rule that spreads a shortage across the whole canal. Examples are proportional cuts, a rotation, or shorter turn times at the head. A tail farm that gets cut by decree is not "demand response", and it shouldn't be paid or counted as one.

**The "below your own three-year average" baseline will misfire in your valley.**
- Two of the last three summers were short, and tail farms were short in both. Their average is already depressed, so they have little to sell and gain nothing. Head farmers have high averages and the most to sell.
- Growers who were forced to take less last year would show up as "reductions" and collect payment for water they never had.
- Growers who already use water carefully earn less than heavy users, who have more to cut.
- Growers can inflate this summer's diversions to raise next year's baseline.

## What I would put in front of the board

I would put forward a smaller, differently built programme that keeps her core idea of paying for reductions and adds what the valley needs.

1. **Forward options, not an hourly price.** Before the season, the board buys the right to call a reduction from willing growers. Each grower is paid a standing fee for being available, plus an exercise payment per cubic metre when the board calls an event. Growers submit sealed offers (volume, price, which weeks) and the board accepts the cheapest per effective cubic metre until its target is covered. The target should be the expected 18% plus a margin of perhaps a quarter, because not every call will deliver in full.
2. **Value reductions by where they land.** Pay for water delivered at the point of need. An Upper Canal cut counts at about 0.6. A reduction by a head-reach Lower Canal grower, passed along the canal to the tail, counts at close to 1 (less canal losses). Your 40% figure is an average, so measure the return fraction during a shortage this summer and use that number.
3. **Buy cheap water first.** Hay, grass seed and barley can be fallowed, dried off early, or given a short deficit at modest cost. Orchard water should be bought only in agronomically safe windows, such as post-harvest or early-season deficit, and priced to reflect the cost of a damaged crop. On those terms the Lower Canal head reaches are likely to be your best source.
4. **Replace the three-year average with a declared plan.** Each grower files a cropping plan and a volume schedule before the season, and reductions are measured against that plan. Make payment conditional on metered diversions. This depends on the metering you already have, so check how many offtakes are measured and add meters where they aren't.
5. **Fix the sharing rule in parallel.** Whatever the board can lawfully change inside the Lower Canal, such as proportional shortage sharing or rotation, should apply from the start of next season. It is cheap and quick and goes straight at the tail problem. It may reduce how much you need to buy.
6. **Fixed trigger dates and 5-day notice.** The board decides on set dates (for example 1 June and 1 July) from Tarrow storage and the forecast, and gives at least 5 days' warning of a call. This matches the 3-to-5 day travel time.

## What to ask the board to approve in five weeks

Approve a pilot, with a capped budget, covering: the option offers (point 1), the declared-plan baseline (point 4), and the sharing-rule change if it falls within the board's powers (point 5). Also approve a short study of the Upper Canal return fraction and the metering gaps, and agree beforehand what result at the end of next summer would trigger expansion or abandonment. That keeps the commitment small and lets next summer test the design.

## What I am unsure about

- My scale check rests on an assumption of similar per-hectare use, and I haven't seen your volumes or the cost of lost production.
- I don't know whether the board has the legal power to change the sharing rule, or whether your water rights restrict paid reductions or transfers. Both could change the order of the steps.
- The 40% return may vary with the weather and with how much the orchards irrigate, so treat it as an estimate.
- Growers may not take part. Participation depends on price and trust, so you should expect to adjust the first season's design.

My confidence that the original programme would fall short is high, about 85%. My confidence in the redesigned package as a whole is lower, about 65%. The direction is sound, but the numbers need your data.
