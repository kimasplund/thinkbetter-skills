**Short answer:** Tell the committee that Dr Messer's instinct is right about the front door but wrong about what an emergency department's triage actually does. Triage reorders a queue. It does not shrink one, and your queue is growing by about 50 cases a month. Pilot a front-end assessment that sorts cases by what they need (settle early, decide on the papers, or hear), with a narrow and verified hardship fast lane. Do not pilot a five-category urgency ladder driven by the free-text box.

## Why reordering won't fix the wait

- Your wait is just the backlog divided by throughput: 9,400 cases at roughly 590 finished a month (640 in, minus 50 net growth) is about 16 months, which matches what you see. Triage changes who waits, not how long the average case waits.
- An ED survives with a "wait as capacity allows" category because those patients eventually leave or recover, and because the department's total demand is roughly matched to capacity. Your category 5 would sit behind a permanently growing line of higher categories. In practice it becomes "never", and the 14-month median will become the category 5 experience.
- Moving hearings from 14 months out to 3 weeks for category 1 means those cases displace others. Unless the category 1 and 2 share is small, you will just shift the delay onto the people who didn't tick the right box.
- The shortfall is about 8.5% of throughput (50 of about 590). That is a modest gap, and the improvements below could close it without any prioritisation at all.

## What ED practice offers that matters more than the five categories

1. **Streaming at the front door.** Modern EDs send minor cases to a "see and treat" stream staffed separately, so they never queue behind the sick. Your equivalent: at the 48-hour review, route each filing to a stream rather than ranking it. Candidate streams are (a) a landlord response and consent or default order, where the landlord does not contest or the amount is agreed; (b) a short mediation or settlement conference within about 4 to 6 weeks; (c) a full hearing. Because 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn, the point of the front end is to make that happen early and cheaply, not to rank cases that mostly never need an adjudicator.
2. **Clinics that book for no-shows.** Outpatient clinics and airlines know a fixed share of bookings won't be used, and they overbook against that rate and keep a short-notice standby list. Your 60% attrition, concentrated in the last four weeks, is exactly the pattern of wasted slots. Two moves follow: schedule hearings with deliberate overbooking calibrated to the historical rate in the final four weeks, and keep a ready-to-go list of cases whose parties have agreed to attend at 5 to 10 days' notice, to fill slots that open.
3. **Deadlines cause the late settlement.** Parties settle when the date becomes real. A hearing date 14 months out, issued at filing, gives them no reason to settle until the last month. Shorter lead times and an early settlement conference with a firm date pull the settlements forward, so the slots they free are available to cases still waiting. Hospital pre-admission clinics work the same way: the commitment point is moved earlier.
4. **Treat the bleeding first.** EDs give interim treatment while a case waits. The tribunal's version is interim release of any undisputed part of the deposit, or a short-form order to release it if the landlord offers no deduction particulars within a set time. In a jurisdiction where the landlord holds the deposit until ruling, the landlord has no incentive to hurry. Delay costs tenants and benefits holders, so the biggest lever is giving landlords a reason to settle or particularise early.

## Where the ED parallel misleads you

- **Harm isn't clinical and isn't visible in the form.** Patients deteriorate in ways a nurse can observe. A tenant's urgency is hardship, which is self-reported and which a 48-hour paper review cannot verify. The free-text box is also completed by about 70% of tenant filers, so the filers least likely to complete it are the ones who are less articulate or unrepresented, and landlords (about 15% of claims) are not served by it at all. You'd be rewarding good writing.
- **Gaming.** Patients rarely exaggerate to be seen faster, because everyone can see the waiting room. Filers will learn within a few months which phrases produce category 1. Expect category inflation unless criteria are tied to documents.
- **ED triage assumes capacity can be flexed.** EDs call in staff and open beds when surges hit. Six adjudicators cannot be surged in the same way.
- **Your cases are about money held by the other side, not about a deteriorating condition.** Waiting is bad for tenants generally, but a case's urgency doesn't grow with time the way a patient's does, so the argument for strict prioritisation is weaker than in an ED.

## Recommended pilot design

1. **Scope.** Run it on new filings from January, not the existing backlog. Say now what happens to the 9,400 already waiting, because every fast-lane hearing displaces one of them.
2. **Front-end review within 48 hours, by streams.** Triage officer assigns a stream (consent/default, settlement conference, hearing). Hardship fast lane limited to a short list of document-verifiable triggers, for example a notice to quit, a documented homeless-risk situation, or a benefits-dependency letter. Cap it at a stated share of hearing slots, say 15%, so the rest of the queue keeps moving.
3. **Ageing guarantee.** Any case reaching a set age (say 9 months) moves up automatically. This is the ED equivalent of re-triage, and it removes the "as capacity allows" trap.
4. **Fill the slots.** Overbook hearings, run the short-notice list, and move the settlement conference to a fixed date 4 to 6 weeks after filing.
5. **Measure against a comparison.** Alternate filing weeks between the pilot stream and the current process for the first six months, or phase by registry, so you can tell whether any change comes from the pilot rather than the season. Track: median and 90th-percentile wait by stream, share of settlements reached before the hearing notice, hearing slots left unused, net monthly backlog change, the share of filings claiming urgency, and the waits of cases outside the fast lane.
6. **Stop rules.** Define in advance what ends or revises the pilot, for example if the fast lane exceeds its cap for two consecutive months, if the 90th-percentile wait outside the fast lane worsens by more than a set amount, or if category assignment is reversed on review in more than a set share of cases.

## What to say to Dr Messer directly

Frame it as keeping what he knows works. His department's real gains came from streaming, fast turnaround for simple cases, and reducing no-shows, not from the five-colour scale alone. Offer him an early assessment with a hardship fast lane and a firm ageing rule as "his" triage, and put the effort into the settlement and slot-filling measures, which have the best chance of closing the 50-a-month gap.

## Where I could be wrong

- I assumed about 590 cases finished a month, derived from filings minus net backlog growth. If your real disposals figure differs, or "hearings held" is far lower than that, the size of the capacity gap changes. Get the actual monthly count of hearings held, settlements and withdrawals before the committee meets.
- I assumed the late-settlement pattern is driven by the length of the lead time. If it is driven by landlords waiting for the hearing notice before they engage, the settlement conference should be triggered by the landlord's response deadline instead.
- The overbooking rate, the 15% cap and the 9-month ageing trigger are placeholders. They need calibrating on your own data.
- I recalled streaming and overbooking practice from health care and airline operations rather than checking sources. The mechanism is sound but the effect sizes will differ for you.
- If your legislation does not allow interim release of undisputed deposits or a default order, point 4 above is not available without a rule change, and the lever shifts to the other measures.
