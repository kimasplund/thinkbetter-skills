Short answer: part of Odile's idea is worth keeping, but the programme as proposed probably would not work well on your system. I'd put a modified version in front of the board, not the grid-style one. The grid analogy breaks in several specific places.

**Where the electricity playbook doesn't carry over**

1. **Delay.** Grid demand response works because a price signal changes load within minutes, and the saving reaches the person who needs it almost at once. Your water takes about 3 days to reach the Lower Canal intake and up to 5 days to reach the tail. An hourly price app is the wrong tempo. A shortage event has to be called about a week ahead, and saving water on a given day has to be planned and contracted before that. An hourly price adds noise without adding any lever.

2. **Return flow cuts the benefit.** Your own figure is that about 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. If an orchardist diverts 10 units less, the river loses about 4 units of return flow. The Lower Canal then gains roughly 6 units of net benefit, not 10 (more if the water stays in the reservoir and you simply release less, but then it helps next month, not this week). You should pay for net benefit at the Lower intake, not for diverted cubic metres. A programme paying per metre diverted below baseline overpays Upper participants by roughly two thirds. This depends on the 40% figure holding in July and August, so check it against gauge records for dry months.

3. **Who can actually cut.** The Upper Canal is 4,200 ha of apples and cherries. Those are perennial trees, and a deficit at the wrong stage (fruit sizing, post-harvest for cherries) causes damage that lasts more than one season. Many of those growers will not respond to money in a shortage, or will respond only at stages where they can afford to. The Lower Canal's 6,800 ha are barley, hay and grass seed, which are annual crops that can be deficit-irrigated, cut early or fallowed far more cheaply. But the Lower Canal is where the shortfall lands. The people who can cheaply reduce use are mostly on the side that is already short. Water they save only helps the tail if the system can route it there. The cheapest reductions upstream are probably limited, and the cheapest reductions downstream don't create new water for the tail unless you move the allocation.

4. **The baseline problem.** "Below your own three-year average" invites gaming. Growers who expect a programme can raise their diversions in the baseline period, and anyone whose use was already low or falling gets paid for nothing (this is the classic adverse selection problem: you pay for reductions that would have happened anyway). Also, two of the last three summers were shortage years, so the baseline already contains forced cuts. Electricity programmes handle this with metered, weather-adjusted baselines and a control group. You would need metering by turnout, at least on enrolled farms. Check what metering you actually have before promising anything.

5. **Shortage events differ from peaks.** A power peak lasts hours and then ends, so people shift load. Your shortfall runs for two months. It's a seasonal quantity, not a spike. Paying for a few days of reduced diversion doesn't fix an 18% gap across July and August. What you need is a seasonal volume commitment, not many short events.

6. **Rights and fairness.** If water rights or allocation rules set who gets what, a payment scheme interacts with them. Check whether voluntary non-use affects a grower's right (use-it-or-lose-it) and whether tail-end farmers are entitled to the saved water or whether it just goes to whoever sits next on the canal. I'd ask your counsel before the vote.

**What I would put in front of the board**

- **Don't vote on the price app.** Park it. It adds cost and doesn't address the delay.
- **Vote to pilot a pre-season option contract.** Before the season (say by April), growers sign up to reduce diversion by an agreed volume for an agreed period if the board calls it, with a call notice of at least 5 to 7 days. They get a modest payment for enrolling plus a payment per cubic metre actually not diverted. This resembles how grid operators pay for standby capacity, which is the part of Odile's playbook that does transfer.
- **Set baselines before the season**, from measured area and crop type, not from a rolling average of past use. This reduces gaming.
- **Pay on net benefit.** Discount Upper Canal reductions for lost return flow (for example, pay about 60% of the Upper rate relative to the Lower, or use a gauge-based figure), and pay full rate for reductions that reach the tail.
- **Target the Lower Canal annual crops first**, particularly hay and grass seed, where fallowing or deficit irrigation is cheap. Think of it as the board paying some growers to take the shortfall that the tail currently absorbs for nothing, spreading it. Consider whether the tail farms, who already bear most of the loss, are partly compensated by this.
- **Consider non-price tools alongside it:** rotation or rostering of deliveries, and changes in release timing from Tarrow. With a 3 to 5 day lag, better forecasting and earlier releases may do more than any price signal.
- **Run it as a small pilot** (a subset of growers) with metering and a measurement plan, so you know after one summer what it saved, at what cost, and how much reached the tail. Five weeks is enough to approve a pilot and a budget cap. It is not enough to design a full programme well.

**Assumptions and gaps**

I don't know your water rights regime, how the 18% shortfall is measured, your metering coverage, or what growers earn per hectare. Those numbers decide the payment rates. I'd also want to see gauge data on return flow in dry months.

Odile's experience with grid programmes is useful. Her instinct that voluntary, paid reductions can work is plausible. The trick is to keep the structure (standby contracts, verified baselines, paying for measured results) and drop the parts that depend on fast, fungible delivery, which your river doesn't have.
