**Short answer for the committee:** Dr Messer is right that the tribunal needs a proper front door. But triage by urgency, on its own, will not fix your problem and could make parts of it worse. I would back a pilot that keeps what works in the emergency-department model (an early, skilled look at every case and routing to the right stream). It should add what the ED model leaves out and your numbers point to: an early settlement mechanism and a firm cap on how long any case can wait.

## Why urgency triage alone doesn't fit

**1. Triage reorders a queue. It doesn't shrink one.**
You receive about 640 filings a month and finish about 590, so the backlog grows by about 50. If you put some cases first, the same total wait lands on everyone else. With a backlog of 9,400 and a deficit, "category 5, as capacity allows" means "possibly never". An ED can leave low-acuity patients waiting because the department also has discharge, admission and fast-track streams that drain the queue. Here nothing drains it. Category 4 and 5 claimants (mostly ordinary tenants wanting a deposit back) would see waits well beyond 14 months, with no guarantee they are ever heard.

**2. The urgency signal is weaker than it looks.**
ED triage rests on vital signs and a trained nurse's assessment. Your equivalent is a free-text box that 70% of tenants fill in and that costs nothing to exaggerate. Once the box decides who is heard in three weeks, every filer will have a reason to write a compelling one, and the 30% who skip it are penalised for not knowing the rules. Self-reported urgency also tends to track how well someone writes, not how badly they need the money. Expect category 1 and 2 to swell until they swamp the lanes they were meant to protect.

**3. The cost of delay is different.**
In an ED, waiting makes some patients rapidly and irreversibly worse, which justifies strict priority. In a deposit dispute, most delay is a steady financial cost, with a smaller group facing real harm (a new tenancy that depends on the deposit, arrears, a vulnerable household). So a narrow urgent lane is justified. A five-tier scale for everyone probably isn't.

**4. Delay is not neutral between the parties.**
The landlord holds the deposit until a ruling or settlement, and 85% of claims are tenant claims. Every extra month in a low category is a month of free use of the tenant's money. If you formally push most cases to "as capacity allows", you give landlords a reason not to settle. You also push the cases that most need to be resolved, such as straightforward undefended claims, to the back. There is also a fairness risk: a decision to rank a claim below others, based on an informal reading of a tenant's circumstances, will draw challenge.

## What the ED model gets right (keep this)

- **A prompt, skilled first review.** A 48-hour look at every filing is valuable regardless of urgency. It can check for missing documents, identify undefended or admitted claims, spot cases that need an interpreter or adjustments, and flag the genuinely time-critical.
- **Streaming.** EDs get throughput by sending simple cases down a fast track, not just by ordering the main queue. That is the part to copy.
- **Waiting-time guarantees.** EDs have maximum-wait rules so no one drifts indefinitely. You need the equivalent.

## The lever your own facts point to: settlement timing

About 60% of cases settle or are withdrawn, and adjudicators say most do so in the four weeks before the hearing date, after the notice goes out. That is a deadline effect. Parties settle when a credible, near deadline arrives. Your system sets a hearing 14 months out and delivers the first real deadline roughly 13 months into the wait. Three consequences:

- Much of the 9,400 backlog is probably cases that would settle quickly if they met a deadline early. They sit in the count and take no adjudicator time until the last month. I can't prove this from the numbers you gave me, but it is the first thing to test.
- Late settlements waste prepared slots. The usual fix is to schedule to expected attendance, as airlines and clinics do: if about 60% of cases fall away, list more cases per hearing day than you have slots, and set up a standby list. Do this in a measured way, because overbooking against a 14-month schedule needs accurate settlement-rate data by case type.
- Moving the first deadline from month 13 to about week 4 to 6 could remove many cases from the queue at a fraction of the adjudicator cost. Examples include a required landlord response within 14 days, a short documents exchange, and a brief early settlement conference run by a registrar or mediator. A landlord who has to return or justify the deposit in weeks has far less reason to wait.

Rough arithmetic: to stop the backlog growing you need to close about 50 more cases a month (about 9%). To clear it in three years you would need to close roughly 850 a month, about 45% more than now. Triage can't deliver that. Earlier settlements and fewer wasted slots could deliver much of it.

## What I would recommend the pilot be

**Scope and timing (January start).** Apply to new filings only, for six months. Handle the existing backlog as a separate workstream. Contact backlog parties in date order, asking whether the dispute is still live and offering early resolution. A fair share of those 9,400 may already be settled or abandoned without the tribunal knowing.

**Design: three streams, and a comparison.**
1. *Front-door review within 48 hours* by a registrar or senior case officer, using mostly objective fields: deposit amount, whether the tenancy has ended, whether the claim is defended, evidence provided, any documented hardship, and a short tick-box list of urgency grounds that need supporting evidence. Use the free-text box as supporting detail, not as the sort key.
2. *Streams.* (a) Early resolution track for most cases, with an early deadline and settlement conference. (b) A small priority lane, capped at about 10% of intake, with a three-week target for documented hardship, imminent loss of housing and similar. (c) Standard track for disputes that need a full hearing, with a date set after the early-resolution stage and not at filing.
3. *Maximum wait.* Every case has a long-stop date, such as 9 or 12 months, at which it is moved up automatically. There is no "as capacity allows" category. Cases also age upward gradually.
4. *Comparison.* Allocate filings randomly (for example by week or alternating by case number) between the pilot process and current practice, so you can tell whether any improvement is down to the design or to chance. If that isn't possible for legal or practical reasons, use a clear before-and-after comparison on matched case types. Do not run the pilot on every new filing, or you will have nothing to compare against.

**Measures, set in advance.**
- Time from filing to resolution, by stream and overall, and to hearing for cases that go to hearing.
- When settlements happen (days after filing), and the share of hearing slots wasted or filled by standby cases.
- Monthly closures against filings (the number that matters for the backlog).
- Wait for the slowest quarter of cases, so that speed for some isn't paid for by others.
- Share of cases placed in the priority lane, how many were upgraded or downgraded on review, how consistent two reviewers are on the same sample, and how outcomes compare for tenants who did and didn't complete the urgency box.
- Complaints, review applications and appeal rates.

**Decision rules to agree before launch.** For example: continue if closures rise to at least 640 a month with no worsening in the slowest quarter, drop or redesign if the priority lane exceeds its cap or reviewer agreement is poor.

## Risks to name for the committee

- The ED comparison breaks down on the fact that the tribunal's queue is not a flow with discharge. Without an early-resolution mechanism, triage only redistributes delay.
- Early deadlines assume parties can settle early. If many landlords are unresponsive or the disputes are genuinely contested, the early track will resolve fewer cases than I've suggested. The pilot's measures will show this quickly.
- Pressure to settle early can disadvantage unrepresented tenants. Build in a plain-language process, the right to continue to a hearing, and monitoring of settlement amounts against claimed amounts.
- Changing hearing-date practice may need rule or practice-direction changes and notice to the parties. Check this before January.
- I've worked from your figures only. I don't know the settlement timing distribution, how many hearings per adjudicator you can run, or the case-type mix. Get those before finalising targets.

**What to say to Dr Messer.** Keep his instinct for an early, expert look at every case and a fast lane for genuine urgency. Add a way for cases to leave the queue, which is where emergency departments get their throughput and where your data suggests your slack is. Pilot that combination, with a hard cap on waiting, against current practice.

I'm moderately confident (about 70%) in the diagnosis that triage alone won't close the gap and that settlement timing is the main lever. I'm less sure about the size of the effect, which is what the pilot is for.
