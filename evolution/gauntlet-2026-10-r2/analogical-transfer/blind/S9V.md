Ines's rules are a good fit for the part of your problem that is a lending problem: queues, due dates, a member register and a fair order of access. They are a poor fit for the part that is a living-thing problem. A library book is inert, it doesn't change while it's out, and the same copy comes back. A starter or a grain culture grows, changes and picks up whatever it meets, and what comes back is not what went out. Your own history shows this twice. The kefir loss came from a jar going back onto the shelf after outside material was added. The rye starter has drifted in volunteers' kitchens within months. Both are what "return the jar to the shelf" does to a living thing.

I'd keep the lending machinery and change what is lent, what comes back, and who pays when something goes wrong.

**1. Lend a portion, not the jar, and never put a returned jar back on the shelf.**
A better model than a library is a seed library or a culture collection. Seed libraries lend seed, the borrower grows it out, and the borrower returns seed or passes it on. Culture collections keep a protected master stock that is never used directly. They make working copies from it, and they put a limit on how many generations a working copy can be from the master.

For Mossgate this means:
- The two jars you hold per culture become a master and a working stock. Nothing from outside touches either.
- Members receive a portion made from the working stock, in their own clean container or a new one you supply.
- Whatever a member brings back is quarantined, not shelved. It can be tasted, smelled and tested at the volunteers' discretion, and if it is good it can be used for baking or sent home as a gift. It never goes back into a stock jar.

That one rule would have prevented last spring's loss. It also settles the home-portion question. Volunteers who top up from home are doing the same thing a returned jar would do, so the rule has to bind volunteers as well as members.

**2. The 80 people who want the rye starter are a distribution problem, not a queue.**
A queue of 80 on a four-week loan is about 18 months of waiting for the last person, and one jar can't serve it anyway. If you make portions, you can serve the demand in weeks:
- Make a dried reserve of the rye starter now (spread thin, dry, store cool). This is the usual backup method for sourdough and costs very little. It is also your insurance against another loss.
- Run the first public weeks as release batches: 20 to 30 portions at a time, allocated by sign-up order or by lottery if you'd rather not reward the fastest typist.
- Ask each recipient to give a portion on to someone else, or back to you, in the form of a fresh jar of their own line after they've kept it going for a while. Seed libraries do this and it works. It spreads the feeding work that Teodor is carrying alone, and it adds a living backup for each line.

This changes the answer to Teodor's six hours. Under the library model, demand grows and the feeding burden stays with him. Under a seed-library model, each borrower feeds their own portion, and the collection only has to keep a master and a working stock alive. The workload then drops as you open, instead of rising.

**3. Replace overdue fines and the replacement charge.**
A €3 weekly fine makes sense when a late book denies someone else access. Here the main risk is the opposite: a member whose starter has gone off, or who is away for a month, is tempted to say nothing or return it late and hope. You want them to tell you. A replacement charge for a "dead or spoiled" jar penalises the exact behaviour you want to hear about, and a culture dying in a home kitchen is often not the member's fault (a warm week, a flour change, a holiday). I'd drop both and use:
- A small refundable deposit, or a membership fee, to cover costs and signal seriousness.
- A "tell us early" norm: a dead portion costs nothing if reported, and you replace it from the working stock.
- A real consequence only for something that harms the collection, such as adding material to a stock jar or returning a jar and passing it off as clean.

**4. Be honest about drift in what you promise.**
Many donor families asked that their original line be kept going. A portion that has spent three months in a member's kitchen is a descendant, not the original. It will smell and rise differently, because flour, water, temperature and feeding times shape the microbial community. Your volunteers' observation about the rye is that effect, not a defect. So:
- Treat the master and working stock as "the line" and say plainly that portions will diverge.
- Cap the generations: members who want to carry on past, say, three months come back for a fresh portion from the working stock.
- Tell donors this directly before March. It is better for them to hear it from you than to discover it.

**5. The four kinds of culture shouldn't all follow one rule.**
- Sourdough starters: portions and a dried reserve work well.
- Kefir grains: they can be divided, but they don't dry reliably. They are also where you lost a line, so lend a small portion only, from the working stock, and keep the master in a separate fridge. The wild-yeast takeover is the usual way kefir goes wrong, so keep members' home kefir away from the stock jars.
- Vinegar mothers: portions with a little of the vinegar work, as these are fairly forgiving.
- Koji strains: these are mould cultures that sporulate. They are the hardest to lend safely and the easiest to spread by accident, so I'd keep them volunteer-only for now, or lend only under tighter rules.

**6. What I'd do before the cards go to print in three weeks.**
- Don't print loan terms on the card. Print the member number, a short pointer to the current rules, and the date. Four weeks, two renewals and €3 will change, and a reprint is a cost you don't need.
- Draft the new rules with Ines. Her loan, queue and register structure is the right skeleton. She is likely to accept the change as soon as she sees the cause of the kefir loss as a returns-handling problem, and the library already has equivalent rules for items that can't go back on the shelf.
- Before opening, make the dried reserve of the rye starter and any other culture with a long waiting list, and write down the rule that no outside material goes into a stock jar, for volunteers too.
- Check, with the county or your insurer, whether giving food cultures to members brings any food-safety or liability duty. I haven't assumed an answer. Donation of a portion with a short written instruction is usually lighter than running a loan, but that is worth confirming.

**Where I'm less sure.**
Whether members will in practice pass portions on, and keep them alive long enough to be useful, is an assumption. Seed libraries show it can work, but a fermentation community may behave differently, and you'll learn more from the first release batches than from any plan. I'd start with one or two cultures and extend once you've seen how it goes. I also don't know how long the dried reserve keeps for each of your cultures, so test a revival of each before you rely on it.
