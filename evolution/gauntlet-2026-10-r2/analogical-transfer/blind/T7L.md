I don't think the programme would work as proposed, but parts of it are worth keeping. Your instinct is right. Demand response works on the grid because of specific physical features, and several of them are missing here.

**Where the grid analogy breaks**

1. **Timing.** A grid event is called and load drops within minutes to hours. A release from Tarrow takes about 3 days to reach the Lower intake and up to 5 to reach the canal tail. An hourly price signal is meaningless when the water answers on a timescale of days. Any event has to be called about a week ahead, and by then you are forecasting a shortage, not responding to a peak.

2. **Return flows.** On the grid, a megawatt not used is a megawatt saved. Here about 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. If you pay an orchardist per cubic metre of reduced diversion, the Lower Canal gains only about 0.6 m3 for each m3 you pay for. It could be less if the cut comes out of water that would have drained back anyway. The programme should pay for reduced consumptive use, or at least discount payments by the return fraction. It should not pay for reduced diversion.

3. **The baseline.** Paying against each grower's own three-year average is the standard grid method. Here it is weak for three reasons.
   - Two of the last three summers were short, so the average is already depressed. Some growers will be paid for "reductions" they would have made anyway.
   - Growers can inflate their diversions in the baseline years, or opt in only when they already plan to cut. That is adverse selection.
   - Orchards and annual crops have very different baselines and costs, and the average ignores that.

4. **Who can actually flex.** Grid customers include many flexible loads. Here the Upper Canal is almost all perennial orchards. Skipping irrigation in a critical window can damage next year's crop as well as this year's, so their cost of cutting is high and uncertain. The cheap water is probably in the Lower Canal's barley, hay and grass seed, which can be deficit-irrigated or fallowed at a known cost. The difficulty is that a cut made below the Lower intake leaves water in the river, and that water doesn't help anyone unless you can store it or move it.

5. **The real problem is distribution.** The shortfall is about 18%, and the tail of the Lower Canal absorbs nearly all of it. That points to a delivery and allocation problem (conveyance losses, travel time, and who gets served first) as much as a total-demand problem. A price app does nothing about it. A voluntary payment programme could even make it worse if head-end growers are the ones enrolling and being paid.

6. **Missing price mechanism.** An hourly price needs metered, enforceable, tradeable volumes. I'm assuming you have turnout-level measurement but probably not hourly. Please correct me if not.

**What would carry over from her playbook**

- Paying for verified reductions, instead of mandating them, is sound. It surfaces the growers whose cost of cutting is lowest.
- Pre-enrolment with a capacity-style payment, so you know before the season how much you can call on, is the right structure. Grid operators do this.
- The idea of a defined "event" with clear rules is useful.

**What I would put in front of the board**

Don't vote to adopt it as written. Propose a pilot for next summer with these changes:

1. **Drop the hourly price app.** Use a seasonal or weekly option-style agreement instead: growers commit in advance to a reduction on notice, with a small standing payment plus an exercise payment.
2. **Call events at least 6 to 7 days ahead**, triggered by reservoir and forecast thresholds, not by a daily peak.
3. **Pay per verified reduction in consumptive use**, or per m3 of diversion discounted by the return fraction. Measure at the turnout against a pre-season baseline set from crop type and area, not a rolling three-year average.
4. **Target the cheapest water first.** Start with Lower Canal annual-crop growers (hay, grass seed, barley) and a small Upper Canal group, with caps on how much any one grower can enrol.
5. **Fix the tail separately.** Review the canal's delivery rules, rotation scheduling, and losses between intake and tail. The 18% shortfall landing on the tail is a rule and hydraulics question that no price scheme will solve.
6. **Set success measures in advance** (see below).

**Questions to answer before the vote**

- What does a cubic metre of shortage cost the growers at the tail, compared with what a grower at the head would need to be paid to give one up?
- What is the actual return-flow fraction across the season? 40% is the usual reading, but it may vary by month.
- Can saved water be left in the reservoir or river and credited to someone downstream under your water rights and operating rules? If not, the payments buy nothing.
- How much money is available, and who pays?
- Can you measure at the turnout reliably enough to audit claims?

**Pilot success tests**

- Net water arriving at the Lower intake and the tail, per dollar paid.
- Share of the shortfall carried by the tail compared with the last two short summers.
- Enrolment spread by canal and crop, to check it isn't just windfall payments to people who would have cut anyway.

My overall view is that Odile's instinct to pay for flexibility is good, but the grid design doesn't fit the physics of your river. A slower, option-style, consumptive-use-based pilot is a version you could defend to the board. I've worked from what you described, so I may have missed details such as your legal allocation rules and metering. Those could change some of this.
