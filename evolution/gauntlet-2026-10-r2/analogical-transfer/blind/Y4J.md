**Short answer:** Keep Ines's membership, holds and fee ideas where they fit, but change the core mechanic before the cards print. A jar of culture is not a book. It grows, it changes, and it contaminates what it touches. If borrowed jars go back on the shelf, you will eventually repeat last spring's kefir loss at scale. Move to "members receive a fresh portion and keep it", and keep the original lines in a protected master tier that no member jar ever touches.

## Where the library model fails you

A book comes back as the same object and cannot infect the next reader's copy. A returned jar has spent four weeks in someone's kitchen. Your own evidence says what happens: the kefir line that was lost picked up a wild yeast through home grains topped into shelf jars, and volunteers report that home portions of the rye drift in smell and rise within a few months. A public returns shelf makes both problems routine. With 230 members it also makes them unauditable.

The library model also gets the demand problem wrong. Eighty people wanting one rye starter looks like a holds queue of 80 people times four weeks. But a starter is copyable, so that is 80 portions, not 80 loans. Queueing them would make the rye line the bottleneck for most of a year for no reason.

## Analogies that change the rules

1. **Seed libraries.** These are the closest working precedent. Members take seeds, grow them, and "return" by giving back seeds from the next harvest, or nothing at all. The library never re-shelves a member's packet. Their lasting rule is that the master stock stays separate from what goes out.
2. **Microbial culture collections.** Yeast and bacteria banks keep a master stock and a working stock, and ship subcultures from the working stock only. Customers never send anything back into the master. They also hold a second preserved copy, typically frozen or dried, in a separate place, because one lost line cannot be recreated.
3. **Beekeeping and plant-cutting swaps.** The gift is a division, and the recipient owns it. The norm is "pass one on," not "return it."

## What I would change

1. **Replace lending with portioning.** A member gets a small labelled portion of a culture, to keep. Nothing goes back on the shelf. Drop the four-week term, the renewals and the €3-a-week overdue fee. Those make sense for an object that must come back, and they would penalise people for keeping a living thing alive.
2. **Make two tiers, with a hard wall between them.** Tier 1 is the master line of each culture: your two jars, fed only by trained volunteers, with nothing from outside ever added. Tier 2 is a working jar per popular culture, which is what portions are taken from. Write the rule down: no member material, and no volunteer's home material, ever goes into either tier. That is the exact failure you traced.
3. **Add a preserved backup for every line.** Most sourdough starters and vinegar mothers can be dried or frozen, and some kefir grains can be held that way for a limited time. Check each type, because I am recalling this from general practice and not from your cultures. Keep the copy somewhere other than the back-room fridges. Many families asked that their line be kept going, and today one fridge fault or one mistake is enough to end it.
4. **Handle returns as a service, not a shelf.** Offer a "re-seed" instead: a member whose portion has drifted or died brings it in, and gets a fresh portion from the working jar. Their old portion is discarded, not kept. If you want return flow, ask for a small dried or fresh "pay-it-forward" portion only after you have tested it. That portion goes to a quarantine step, never straight to the shelf.
5. **Cap by production, not by queue.** Decide how many portions Teodor can prepare per week without more hours, and issue portions in batches. For the rye, run dated pickup rounds: for example one batch a week from the working jar. Do the sums with your real numbers: portion size, feeding cycle, and how fast the working jar can regrow after a portion is taken.
6. **Take load off Teodor.** Six hours a week at his limit is a single point of failure. Tiering helps in two ways. Culture types that hold well dormant or dried do not need weekly feeding, and members who keep their own portions feed them themselves. Add a second trained volunteer for Tier 1 so that feeding, and the hygiene rules, do not sit in one person's head. Spend the freed hours on the master jars and on portion handover.
7. **Be honest about drift.** Home rye will differ from yours, which is normal for a living culture and not a failure. Tell members that their jar is a descendant and the commons holds the reference line. That is also your selling point for people who want the 1890s rye: the family's original is safe, and they get a daughter of it.
8. **Fees.** Replace the replacement charge for dead jars. Charging someone who keeps a culture and loses it works against you, because you want them to come back for a re-seed. A small one-off charge or donation per portion covers jars and labels better.
9. **Add basic safety and handling terms.** Give a short hygiene sheet, label every portion with culture, source and date, and tell members not to add anything to your lines. Because you are moving from volunteers to the public, check local food-safety and liability requirements for handing out food cultures. I have not checked what applies in your area.

## For the printer in three weeks

Do not print loan terms on the membership cards. Print the member name, number and the commons's name, and put the rules on a separate sheet or page that can change after launch. Hold the final rules until you have tested one portioning round with the rye.

## Disanalogies and risks

- Seed libraries deal with seeds that mostly survive drying and storage. Your cultures vary: koji and some starters tolerate drying, while kefir grains are harder. Backup methods have to be set per culture type.
- Culture collections have lab-grade sterile technique and staff. You have volunteers and a fridge, so your wall between tiers has to be simple enough to follow without training: separate shelves, separate utensils and no exceptions.
- Giving portions away means the commons stops being the only place a line lives. For some families that is fine, since their line spreads further. For others it may feel like loss of control, so ask donors which they prefer.

## Recommendation

Keep Ines's membership structure, her instinct for fair access, and her drafting. Change the object being managed: a member receives a portion and keeps it, while the master line and a dried or frozen backup stay behind a wall that nothing from outside crosses. Run the rye as scheduled batches of portions, not a queue of loans. Print cards without loan terms.

## Where I could be wrong

- I assume portions can be taken without harming the working jar. If some cultures, such as certain kefir grains, grow too slowly to share, those few may still need a small waitlist and may need a loan-like arrangement with a quarantine check on return.
- I recalled how seed libraries and culture collections handle masters and backups from general knowledge and did not check specifics. Verify them with a collection or a food-safety contact before you fix the rules.
- I do not know which of your 140 cultures can be dried or frozen, or how quickly the rye regrows after a portion is taken. Those two facts decide how many portions per week you can offer and how much of Teodor's time you can free.
- If your insurance or local rules forbid giving cultures to the public, or require the commons to retain lent items, the library model might be forced on you after all. In that case keep the quarantine step and the master wall.
