**Short answer:** As proposed, I don't think it would fix your problem. The core idea, paying people for verified reductions against a measured baseline, is sound and worth keeping. But the two parts as described are built for a system with different physics, and several features would make them pay for water that doesn't reach the tail of the Lower Canal. I'd put a reshaped version in front of the board, and I'd ask them to vote on a pilot with defined tests, not on the app.

## Why a straight port from the grid breaks

**1. Your shortage is a volume problem, not a peak problem.** A power peak lasts hours, and shaving it only means shifting use by a few hours. You were about 18% short across two months. Nobody can shift that use to a later hour, because the crop needs the water or it doesn't get it. A reduction here is real consumption foregone (fallowed fields, deferred cuts, deficit irrigation), so the price has to cover lost crop value, not inconvenience. An hourly price signal also makes no sense when a decision at Tarrow takes three days to reach the Lower intake and up to five to reach the canal tail. Nobody can respond to an hourly price for a resource that moves at that speed. The signal needs to be a notice period of a week or more, with defined start and end dates.

**2. Location matters, and the grid mostly ignores it.** Your two canals are not interchangeable.
- Water that a Lower Canal grower near the head leaves undiverted stays in the canal and passes the next turnouts. That is close to a 1:1 transfer toward the tail, provided it isn't picked up by someone mid-canal.
- Water that an Upper Canal orchard leaves in the river is worth much less. About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back before the Lower intake. If the Upper Canal cuts by X, the return flow falls too, and the gain at the Lower intake is roughly 0.6X, arriving three days later. The programme as written would pay Upper growers for X and deliver about 0.6X.
- Orchards are also the worst place to buy cuts. Trees are perennial and a bad summer carries over into next year's crop. Barley, hay and grass seed are the cheapest water you can free up, per cubic metre and per dollar of lost income.

**3. The baseline is the weak point.** "Below your own three-year average" fails for you in at least three ways.
- You have had two short summers in three, so the average already reflects reduced diversions, and the baseline will drift down every time you use the programme.
- You would pay for cuts that would have happened anyway: fields already planned for fallow, a grower who was going to be curtailed, or an orchard that finished harvest early.
- Growers can raise their baseline by diverting more in non-event periods. Grid programmes spent years fighting exactly this.

The tail growers who bore most of the shortfall already have the lowest averages. They would be paid little or nothing, and they are the ones the programme needs to protect.

**4. Voluntary enrolment selects the wrong people.** The people who enrol are those with the cheapest cuts, not necessarily those whose location helps the tail. Without a rule on where the saved water goes, savings in the middle of the canal can be re-diverted by the next turnout down, and the tail sees none of it.

## What does carry over from the grid

- Pay for measured, verified reductions rather than relying on appeals.
- Contract in advance. Grid operators learned that standing capacity contracts (a fee for being available, plus a payment when called) work far better than spot appeals.
- Set clear trigger rules, notice periods and penalties for not delivering.
- Keep a fallback of fair curtailment for when voluntary supply falls short. The grid uses rotating shedding in that case, and irrigation systems have close equivalents. Time-based rotation of canal deliveries is the best known example.

## What I would put in front of the board

1. **A dry-year option contract, signed before the season.** A grower is paid an availability fee up front, and an exercise payment per cubic metre if the board calls an event. The call needs 7 to 10 days' notice, which covers the travel time, and specifies a start date, an end date and a volume. The baseline is each grower's declared crop and irrigation plan, set in spring and checked against their metered diversions, not a rolling three-year average.
2. **Target the Lower Canal head-end first, and annual crops.** Offer the main product to growers of barley, hay and grass seed in the upper reaches of the Lower Canal. Their cuts reach the tail at close to full volume and cost the least.
3. **A narrow, discounted Upper Canal product.** Limit it to cases where the agronomy allows it, for example reduced post-harvest irrigation on cherries (to be confirmed with your own agronomists). Price it on what actually arrives at the gauge above the Lower intake, so a payment for X is treated as 0.6X. An alternative is to hold the saved volume back in Tarrow for the August peak. That avoids the return-flow loss, but it only works if the reservoir can store it.
4. **A tail-protection rule.** Write into the operating rules how option water is routed. For example, scheduled gate settings, or a share of released savings reserved for tail turnouts. Without this, you are paying for water that mid-canal growers can simply take.
5. **A trigger based on storage and forecast, not an hourly price.** For example, assess on a fixed date in early June and again in mid-June, with set thresholds for Tarrow storage and expected inflow.
6. **A cap on the programme cost,** so the board votes on a known maximum.

If the app is wanted, keep it for sending notices, confirming schedules and showing growers their metered use. It doesn't need to post a price.

## What I need to know before the vote, and what I assumed

- **Metering.** If every turnout isn't metered, any payment-for-reduction scheme is unenforceable. I assumed turnouts are metered or can be before summer. If not, this is the first thing to fix, and it may limit what you can do by next July.
- **The volume of the gap.** You gave 18%, but I don't know of what, or the cubic-metre size. That decides how many growers you need. As a rule of thumb, if the gap is G and you buy mostly Upper Canal cuts, you would need to buy about G / 0.6 to cover it.
- **Costs.** I assumed head-end annual-crop growers would accept a payment well below what an orchard would need. Ask a sample of growers what they would take, before the board sets prices.
- **Entitlements.** I assumed there is a formal allocation system that can be used as a reference. If rights are informal, the baseline becomes harder.
- **The reservoir.** Whether Tarrow can hold water over for August is the biggest unknown for option 3.

## Risks to tell the board

- Payments in a short year could exceed the budget if too many enrol. The cap addresses this.
- Growers who sign and then don't deliver need defined penalties. Without them the option is a free gift.
- If participation is low, there is no fallback. Agree now on a curtailment rule that spreads the shortfall by position, so the tail is not the default victim again.

**The position I'd take with Ms. Brannock:** she is right that the playbook of paid, verified reductions is the best tool you have. The adjustment is that it should be a seasonal option contract aimed at the right growers and the right part of the system, not an hourly market. That version can be piloted next summer, and a pilot gives you the data to expand it afterward.
