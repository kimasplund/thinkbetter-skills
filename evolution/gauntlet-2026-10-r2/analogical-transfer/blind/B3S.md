**Short answer:** Your doubts are justified. As proposed, the programme would probably not deliver the water you are missing, and it could pay your best-placed growers for cuts they would have made anyway. The grid idea of paying for reductions is worth keeping. The hourly price app and the "below your own three-year average" payment are not. I would put a different package in front of the board: advance-notice shortage contracts paid on water actually saved, a written rule for sharing shortages, and metering at the turnouts. Run it as a pilot next summer.

## Why a water shortage is not a slower peak

A peak on the grid is a few hours of excess demand against fixed capacity. Demand that is shaved is usually just moved to another hour. Your problem is different in four ways.

- **It is a volume problem, not a rate problem.** You are about 18% short across July and August, about 60 days. Tarrow is a store. Every cubic metre not diverted in July is a cubic metre still in the reservoir in August. That is useful, but it means the board needs a committed total reduction over a season, not a reaction to an hourly signal.
- **Your system is slow.** A release takes three days to reach the Lower Canal intake and up to five to reach the canal tail. An hourly price, or a shortage event called the same morning, tells growers to act on water that will not arrive for days. The grid works because a price signal and a load response happen within minutes. Here the call has to go out at least five or six days ahead, which is a forecast-and-contract problem, not a price problem.
- **Most of what growers could cut cannot be shifted.** Electricity demand can wait an hour. An orchard cannot skip July and recover in September. Apple and cherry trees are perennial, and a short-term cut can cost fruit size this year and tree health in later years. Barley, hay and grass seed on the Lower Canal can be deficit-irrigated or fallowed with an economic loss but no lasting damage. So the growers who could respond most cheaply are mostly the Lower Canal growers, who are already the ones absorbing the shortfall.
- **A diverted cubic metre is not a consumed one.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. If an orchardist cuts 10 units, only about 6 units of extra water actually reach the Lower intake, because the 4 units of return flow disappear with the cut. Paying per cubic metre diverted overpays Upper Canal reductions by roughly two-thirds relative to what they deliver downstream. The payment should be tied to consumptive use, or at least discounted for return flow.

## Where the grid playbook does carry over

Three things from demand response are worth taking.

- **Pay for reductions rather than mandate them.** Growers who can cut cheaply should be the ones who cut. That is a good basis for sharing a shortage.
- **Pay for being available, not only for acting.** Grid operators pay a standing fee for the right to call a reduction. For you, that means contracts signed in winter, with the right to call a cut in July.
- **Verify against a baseline, and design the baseline carefully.** This is where the grid experience is a warning, not a model.

## Problems with the specific proposal

1. **The three-year baseline is bad here.** Two of the last three summers were short, so the baseline already contains forced reductions. Tail-end growers whose deliveries were cut would show a low average. They would be paid little for cutting, or nothing, while growers with reliable water at the head of each canal would have high baselines and could be paid for reductions that cost them little. Baselines that growers can influence are also open to gaming, for example by deliberately over-diverting in a good year. This weakness is well documented in grid programmes.
2. **Self-selection.** A voluntary programme will enrol first the growers who already plan to fallow, switch crops or retire land. You would pay for cuts that would happen anyway.
3. **The hourly price app.** I see no benefit for irrigation. Canal deliveries are scheduled in days, and fruit growers do not adjust hour to hour. A price that moves hourly invites confusion and complaints. A single advance notice and a seasonal price would be easier to use.
4. **Fairness and legality.** A price that rises in a shortage means the growers with the most cash get water and the tail-end growers lose it. If your rights are allocated by entitlement or priority, selling water by price may also conflict with them. The board's lawyers should look at this before the vote.

## What I would put in front of the board

1. **A written shortage-sharing rule, agreed before summer.** Today the tail takes nearly all of the shortfall. That looks like a priority-by-position and conveyance effect that the board can change directly. A proportional cut by area, adjusted for travel time and losses, costs nothing in incentives and would address the fairness complaint more reliably than any market.
2. **Option contracts, not hourly pricing.** In winter or early spring, offer growers a standing fee for committing to cut by an agreed percentage or area when the board calls a shortage. Set the call date at least six days ahead. A trigger based on reservoir storage on a fixed June date would also work. Pay the call-day amount on measured consumptive reduction, with return flow adjusted for Upper Canal growers. Start with Lower Canal annual crops, then add Upper Canal growers who volunteer.
3. **A baseline based on entitlement or irrigated area and crop**, not on the past three years of diversions. Entitlement is harder to game and does not penalise the tail for past shortages.
4. **Metering at the turnout.** Without a reading from each farm, no pay-for-reduction scheme can be verified. The cost of metering will probably decide the scale of the pilot.
5. **A pilot, not a full launch.** With a vote in five weeks, authorise a funded pilot for next summer, with a defined size (for example one section of the Lower Canal and a few Upper Canal volunteers) and a review in autumn. Report how much water each contract actually moved to the Lower intake, compared with what you paid.

For the board, the sentence I would use is: "Paying growers to use less water when we are short is sound. A grid-style hourly price and a three-year-average baseline are not suited to a slow, storable, perennial-crop system with return flows."

## Where I could be wrong

- **Facts I assumed and you should check.** I assumed that there is no turnout metering today, that water rights allow the board to contract for reductions, and that the 18% shortfall is measured against demand at the tail, not against diversions. If you already have metering, the pilot can be larger and faster.
- **The 40% return flow.** I treated it as the share of water consumed by the Upper Canal. If the return flows are partly seepage that reaches the river late or from an aquifer, the timing of the benefit to the Lower intake will differ, and the discount should be set from your own gauge data.
- **Grid details are from memory.** The baseline-gaming and self-selection problems in demand response are well known, but I have not checked the specific programmes Ms Brannock ran. She may have solved them, and it is worth asking her how.
- **What would change my view.** If a large share of Upper Canal growers have spare capacity, such as alternative crops, storage ponds or groundwater, a price-based tool might work better there than I have allowed. Check this in the winter survey.
