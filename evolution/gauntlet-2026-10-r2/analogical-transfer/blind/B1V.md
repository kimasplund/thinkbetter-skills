**Short answer:** Not as proposed. The hourly price app would do close to nothing for you, and the "paid to divert below your own average" scheme would pay for the wrong water at the wrong time. Odile's instinct is still partly right: paying growers to give up water on a call works. But the version that fits your valley is a pre-season option contract for dry-year cutbacks, measured in water that actually reaches the Lower Canal, plus a shortage-sharing rule so the tail stops absorbing the whole 18%. Put those two things to the board, with the app reduced to a daily information board.

## Analogies Investigated
1. **Grid demand response (Odile's analogy).** It works because peaks are short, forecastable within hours, and most load is deferred rather than lost: the dishwasher runs at 11pm instead of 6pm. A baseline of "your normal use" is also easy to estimate for a factory with a metered history.
2. **Grid capacity and interruptible contracts.** The part of demand response that carries the load in practice is not the spot price signal. Customers are paid in advance to be on call, commit to a volume, and are penalised if they don't deliver.
3. **Dry-year fallowing and leasing deals in southwestern US and Australian river systems.** Irrigation districts there pay farmers, usually in the spring and before the season's water is known, to fallow or cut back in dry years, and the payment is set against water that actually stays in the system, not water "not used".
4. **Rotation and proportional-cut rules (for example, the scheduled rotational delivery systems on large South Asian canals).** Shortage is shared by a published rule, so position on the canal does not decide who goes without.

## Transferable Principles
- Pay for commitment before the shortage, not for a response during it. The call must be made at least five days ahead because of your travel times, so an hourly signal has nothing to act on. You know by early June, from storage and snowpack, whether July-August will be short. That is a seasonal decision, not a price spike.
- Pay for water that arrives where it is needed. Because about 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back, each cubic metre an orchard cuts from its diversion adds only about 0.6 m³ at the Lower intake gauge. To close a shortfall of S at the Lower intake, Upper cuts must total roughly 1.7 times S, and a payment per m³ not diverted would overpay for the 40% that was coming back anyway. A cut by a Lower Canal grower upstream of the tail counts close to 1:1 (less canal seepage), so it should be paid at a higher credit per m³ than the same cut in the Upper Canal.
- The baseline has to be set in advance and from something the grower cannot move. A rolling three-year average of use rewards whoever used the most, penalises the tail farms (whose recent use is already depressed by shortage and who would be "paid" for water they never got), and invites growers to push diversions up before they enrol.
- Shortage needs a sharing rule. Without one, the least-favoured position absorbs the loss, which is what has happened to the tail.

## Proposed Solutions
1. **Dry-year option programme.** Each March or April, growers offer a volume they would give up in July-August if called, at the price they name. The board buys the cheapest options up to a budget cap, pays a modest retainer on signing and a larger payment on delivery, and issues the call in early June once the forecast is clear. Delivery is checked at the turnout meter against a baseline set from the grower's declared crop and area, not their past use.
2. **Credit factors by location.** Pay on water credited at the Lower intake: for example 0.6 of the volume for Upper cuts, near 1.0 for Lower head and mid-canal cuts, and a stated factor for each reach. Publish the factors; they come from the 40% return-flow figure and canal loss measurements.
3. **Shortage-sharing rule.** Set in advance what happens to deliveries when the June forecast is short, for example a proportional cut across both canals, or a rotation of days within the Lower Canal so the tail is not always last. The option programme then covers part of the gap with paid cuts and the rule shares the remainder.
4. **The app, reduced.** Post daily gauge readings, the release schedule, and the expected arrival time at each reach. That is useful to growers on both canals and costs little, but it is not a price market.

## Disanalogies and Risks
- **Lost versus deferred.** A late dishwasher cycle costs nothing. Water not applied to hay in July is a lost cut; a stressed cherry or apple tree can carry the damage into next year's crop. Most of the demand you would be buying is not shiftable, so you are buying fallowing and deficit irrigation, not a delay. Expect prices to reflect that, and the cheapest water to be in the barley, hay and grass seed on the Lower Canal, not in the orchards.
- **Volume over weeks, not hours.** The shortage is about 18% across two months, a stock problem. A price that changes hourly can't move a decision a grower makes every few days.
- **Delays.** A cut at the Upper Canal takes three days to show at the Lower intake and up to five days to reach the canal's end, so any call needs a lead time of at least a week.
- **Fairness.** The tail farms already lose the most. A voluntary programme paid from a fixed budget could end up buying water from them cheaply because they have the least to lose, which makes the position problem worse unless the sharing rule is in place first.
- **Gaming and cost.** Paying a per-m³ rate invites over-claiming; this needs meters at each turnout and an audit.

## Recommendation
Take two items to the board: (a) the sharing rule for shortage years, adopted first because it needs no money and directly addresses the tail; and (b) a capped pilot option programme for next summer, with location credit factors, a baseline from declared crops and area, and a June call date. Limit the pilot to a sum the board can afford to lose, run it on volunteers, and keep a record of what was offered, called and delivered so the second year can be priced from evidence. Tell Odile that the demand response experience is useful in the contract design (capacity-style retainers, verification, penalties for non-delivery) but that the hourly price and the "below your own average" payment should be dropped. Meanwhile start metering turnouts if they are not already metered; five weeks is enough to decide, not enough to install a market.

## Where I could be wrong
- I assumed the shortfall is known by early June and that the board can legally pay for, and count, a reduction in diversions as water left for other users. If your water rights or district rules forbid that, the option programme needs a legal route first.
- The 0.6 credit uses your 40% figure as a summer average; if return flows are lower in July-August (for example drip irrigation in the orchards) the Upper credit should fall, and if they are higher it should rise. Please check this against the gauge record.
- I recalled the southwestern US, Australian and South Asian arrangements from memory and have not checked their current terms.
- I have no data on grower willingness to sell, on turnout metering, or on the budget. If the 18% shortfall mostly comes from conveyance losses rather than demand, fixing the canal would beat any demand programme.
- Post-harvest deficit irrigation of cherries and apples may be cheaper than I assume. Ask your agronomist before treating orchard cuts as expensive.
