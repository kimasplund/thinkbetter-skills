**Short answer:** As written, no. The paid-reduction idea is worth keeping, but the hourly price app and the "below your own three-year average" payment rule would not fix what actually went wrong, which is who absorbs a shortage and when anyone finds out about it. Put a pre-contracted dry-year option programme in front of the board instead, built on an announced seasonal allocation and a shortage-sharing rule for the Lower Canal. Odile's grid experience is useful for the contract design, but not for the hourly price.

## Why the electricity playbook does not carry over directly

- **Grid demand response relieves a short spike on a system with no storage, and the signal acts within minutes.** You have an 18% volume deficit over two months, in a system that stores its supply in Tarrow Reservoir. Shifting use from one hour to another saves nothing. Only a net cut in consumption does. A "slower peak" is really a seasonal budget problem.
- **Your signal takes days to arrive.** Water released today reaches the Lower intake in three days and the canal tail in up to five. An hourly price is meaningless against that. A shortage event needs a week's notice to make sense, and growers irrigate on rotations and crop stage, not by the hour.
- **Your pay-per-cubic-metre rule pays for the wrong quantity.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. If an orchardist cuts drainage losses, the river sees no gain, yet they are paid in full. If they cut real crop use, only about 60% of the cut reaches the Lower Canal. Payment should follow the net effect on the river, not the diverted volume.
- **The three-year average is a poor baseline.** Two of the last three summers were short, so tail-end growers' averages are already depressed and they have little left to "sell". Growers who plan to fallow anyway get paid for nothing, and anyone can inflate their average by over-diverting in a wet year. Odile will recognise this as the customer-baseline gaming problem from demand response.
- **It leaves the real problem alone.** The tail of the Lower Canal took nearly all the shortfall because of position and travel time, not because of price. A voluntary programme does nothing about that unless the sharing rule changes.

## What other systems that ration scarce water do

1. **Seasonal allocation announcements (Australian-style).** Supply is declared as a percentage of entitlement in spring, and updated as inflows firm up. Growers plan crops and fallowing knowing they will get, say, 82%. This turns a July surprise into a May decision, when cutting is cheap.
2. **Paid fallowing under standing options (Palo Verde-style arrangements in the western US).** Growers are paid a retainer to be on call, plus a payment when called. Fallowing is verified by area, not by metered volume. Most of the water comes from low-value annual crops.
3. **Rotational, time-based delivery on canals (warabandi-style).** Shortage is shared by shortening everyone's turn proportionally, so the head-end cannot take its full share while the tail goes dry.
4. **Grid demand response itself, taken more selectively.** The parts that transfer are the retainer-plus-performance contract, a pre-registered baseline, and a held-out control group to catch gaming.

I am recalling the details of 1 to 3 from memory, not checking them. Treat them as design precedents, not as exact rules to copy.

## What I would put in front of the board

1. **A published allocation date.** Announce the season's percentage by a fixed date in spring, and update it on set dates. This costs nothing to run and is probably the most valuable single change.
2. **A written shortage-sharing rule for the Lower Canal**, so that the tail does not carry the cut. For example, shorten delivery turns proportionally, or reserve a share of canal flow for the tail. The board can decide this directly. It depends on your legal entitlements, so check those first.
3. **A dry-year option programme for Lower Canal growers of hay, grass seed and barley.** The terms:
   - A small per-hectare retainer for being enrolled, plus a larger payment if the board calls the option, paid on verified fallowed or curtailed area (satellite or inspection), not on a three-year average.
   - Prices set by sealed-bid offers from growers, not by a number the board guesses.
   - Call notice of at least a week, so the lag in the system is built in.
   - A written statement that water not used under the programme counts as beneficial use for the purpose of preserving their rights.
4. **Scale check.** If the 18% is measured against total demand, covering it from the Lower Canal alone means taking something like 1,500 to 2,000 hectares out of 6,800. That is a large share to recruit voluntarily. Expect to add part-season options (skipping a late hay cut, ending grass-seed irrigation after harvest) and a smaller negotiated element for the orchards, such as post-harvest cherry deficit irrigation, with payment based on net river effect (diversion minus measured return).
5. **A free daily or weekly status page** showing reservoir level, the season allocation and any called events, in place of the hourly price app. Revisit pricing after one season of data.

## Where the analogies break down

- Orchards are perennial. A fallowed or stressed orchard costs years, not one season, so the Upper Canal cannot be handled like barley, and per-cubic-metre compensation for it would be much higher.
- Grid customers are numerous and interchangeable. You have a small number of unequal growers in fixed positions on the river, so a few large enrolments or refusals will dominate the result.
- Buying hay water to give to tail-end hay farms moves water within one crop class. The programme creates net value only if the cut is cheaper than the shortfall losses it avoids, so the board should check that.
- Voluntary schemes can be unfair in a different way. Growers with the lowest cost of fallowing enrol; others stay out and still benefit.

## Where I could be wrong

- I assumed that the 18% shortfall is measured against total demand, and that crop water use per hectare is similar enough across the two canals for the area arithmetic to be a rough guide. Orchards likely use more per hectare, which would shrink the area needed.
- I assumed that most barley and grass-seed irrigation ends around early summer. If July and August demand in the Lower Canal is mostly hay, the pool of cheap reductions is smaller than it looks.
- Everything about legal entitlements, seniority between the canals, metering at turnouts, and whether the board can pay growers is unknown to me. Each of these could change the design. Water-right "use it or lose it" rules are the likeliest to block voluntary programmes.
- If the Lower Canal turnouts are all metered and tail-end shortfall turns out to come mainly from canal losses, not from allocation, then fixing the canal would matter more than any programme.
