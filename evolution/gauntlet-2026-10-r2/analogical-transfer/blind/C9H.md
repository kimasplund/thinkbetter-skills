I'd tell the committee that Dr Messer is right about one thing and wrong about another, and that the pilot should be built to find out which part is which.

**1. Triage doesn't fix this tribunal's problem**

- Intake is about 640 a month and throughput is about 590, so the backlog grows by about 50 a month. Triage reorders the queue but doesn't change either number.
- An ED works because most patients are low-acuity and can be handled quickly, and the sickest are rare. Here, every case is the same kind of dispute over money, and all of them are waiting. If category 1 is seen in three weeks, the wait for the other categories gets longer, not shorter.
- Category 5 ("as capacity allows") is the real risk. With a permanent shortfall, capacity never allows, so those cases wait indefinitely. A hospital can't leave patients in the waiting room forever, but a tribunal can do this without anyone noticing.
- Little's law roughly checks out: 9,400 cases at about 590 a month is about 16 months, close to your 14-month median. Waiting time is a symptom of the stock of cases. To cut it you need more capacity, fewer cases reaching a hearing, or shorter hearings.

**2. The urgency signal is weak and open to gaming**

- The harm here is mostly financial hardship. It is not like a clinical emergency that a nurse can assess in minutes.
- The basis for triage is a free-text box that only about 70% of tenant filers complete. Articulate and well-advised filers will write persuasive urgency statements, so triage would favour the best-resourced tenants. Landlords (15% of claims) have no equivalent box, so their cases may be systematically placed low.
- Once urgency affects timing, everyone learns to write "urgent". Category 1 inflates and the system stops discriminating.
- A 48-hour review of 640 filings a month is about 30 a working day. That needs dedicated, trained staff. It also consumes capacity, so the 48-hour review has to be costed against adjudicator time.
- Use objective, checkable criteria instead of impressions: tenant in a documented hardship situation, deposit above a set amount, vulnerable-person flags, a pending move or eviction, and a case that is simple and ready to hear.

**3. The incentives problem**

- The landlord holds the deposit until a ruling or settlement. Delay therefore benefits the landlord, and the longer the wait, the more pressure a tenant is under to settle for less.
- With 85% of claims brought by tenants, the backlog works as a transfer from tenants to landlords. This is the strongest case for acting, and it is a fairness argument that goes beyond efficiency.
- Triage doesn't change that incentive. Consider whether the deposit could be held by a neutral party, as in a deposit protection scheme, or paid into escrow once a claim is filed. Doing so would remove the landlord's reason to delay. It probably needs legislation, so recommend it as a longer-term step, but raise it.

**4. The 60% settlement rate is a bigger opportunity than triage**

- Settlements cluster in the four weeks before the hearing date, so the prepared slots are wasted. That suggests the hearing date is the first thing that forces parties to engage. Dates set at filing, far in the future, mean no one engages until the date approaches.
- Options to test:
  - **Early settlement step.** Require a short mediation or case-management conference or a compulsory exchange of evidence within 4 to 6 weeks of filing, with a real deadline. This pulls settlement earlier, which is where triage's value lies, because it is where the forcing event happens.
  - **Overbooking.** If about 60% of cases resolve before the hearing and most do so late, schedule more hearings per slot, as airlines do, with a fallback list. The aim is to fill the slots that late settlements free up.
  - **Short-notice lists.** Keep a pool of ready cases that can be heard when a slot opens.
  - **Fast-track the simple cases.** Undisputed or documents-only cases can be decided on the papers.
- If settlements moved earlier or the slots were reused, that could reclaim a meaningful share of capacity. The 60% figure suggests more hearing slots are lost than the 50-a-month deficit.

**5. What the pilot should look like**

Keep what is good in Dr Messer's idea, which is a structured early look at every case. Redesign it around what triage can and can't do.

- **Triage as case-sorting, not queue-jumping.** Sort filings into tracks (settle-ready, documents-only, full hearing, vulnerable or urgent) and route them. Use a small number of categories and objective criteria, not free-text impressions.
- **No open-ended bottom category.** Every category gets a maximum wait. Category 5 should be "within X months, guaranteed", not "as capacity allows". Set an ageing rule so that any case waiting beyond a threshold is promoted.
- **Allocate a fixed share of capacity to urgent cases** (say 20%) so the rest isn't starved, and report on it.
- **Test, don't assume.** Run the pilot as a randomized or staggered comparison, for example by registry, filing week or alternating weeks, against the current first-in-first-out approach. Compare, don't just go live.
- **Pre-register the measures:**
  - Time to hearing, by category and overall (including the 90th percentile, not just the median)
  - Backlog size and monthly net change
  - Settlement rate and how early settlements happen
  - Wasted hearing slots
  - Share of filers in each category, by tenant or landlord, represented or not, and by whether they completed the urgency box
  - Rate of re-categorization and complaints
- **Set stop conditions up front.** For example, stop or revise if the oldest cases wait longer than they do now, or if category 1 grows past an agreed share of filings.
- **Audit categorisation.** Have a second reviewer sample 10% of decisions to check consistency, and check whether the free-text box is being gamed.
- **Size and timing.** January is feasible for a design and baseline stage. Give the trial at least 6 months, since a 14-month wait means effects on hearing times take a while to show. Early indicators such as time to first contact, settlement timing and category mix will appear sooner.

**6. What to say to the committee**

1. The ED analogy contributes a good principle, which is to assess every case early, but the tribunal's underlying problem is capacity and incentives. Triage by itself won't reduce the 14-month median and may make it worse for the lowest categories.
2. The clearest gains are probably earlier settlement and better use of the slots that settlements free up.
3. If the pilot goes ahead, build in guaranteed maximum waits, objective criteria, a randomized comparison, and pre-agreed stop conditions.
4. Separately, ask for the capacity question to be put to the president: 50 more cases closed a month stabilises the backlog, and clearing it in two years would need roughly 390 more a month. Process changes may close some of that gap but probably not all of it.
5. Raise the deposit-holding rule as a policy issue for government.
