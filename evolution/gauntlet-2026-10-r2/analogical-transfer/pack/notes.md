# Pack notes: analogical-transfer, round 2

**Brief 1 (irrigation district, grid demand-response analogy).** The tempting analogy is the chair's: water shortage as a slow electricity peak, handled with hourly prices and paid curtailment against a baseline. Four planted disanalogies break it. k1, transit lag, is the most visible: the three-to-five-day travel time is stated plainly, and the chair's "slower peak" line draws attention to timescale. k2, return flow, is the hardest: the 40% gauge figure sits in a system-layout sentence, and a reply has to work out that paying per cubic metre of reduced upper diversion buys only about 0.6 m3 downstream. k3, one-way flow, only becomes visible once the reply links the shortage location (the Lower Canal tail) to where savings are made. k4, non-deferrable crop curtailment, needs the reply to connect the July-August duration and the orchard/annual crop split to the grid's reliance on shiftable load. A careful reply converges on days-ahead allocation, payment on consumptive use, location-specific value and buying short-season cuts from annual crops near the shortage. framing-hype.md and framing-skeptical.md carry the identical facts in different tones.

**Brief 2 (deposit tribunal, emergency-department triage analogy).** k1, self-reported urgency, is the easy key: the 70% figure for filers completing the urgency box makes gaming fairly obvious. k2 is the decisive and most hidden one: the adjudicators' complaint about late settlements wasting slots frames the hearing date as a nuisance, when it is the deadline that resolves most of the 60% of cases that settle. Removing dates for category 5, or shortening them for category 1, attacks the settlement mechanism. k3 needs the reply to notice that the president's goal is backlog reduction and that reordering a queue that grows by 50 a month redistributes waiting without reducing it. k4 rests on the sentence saying the landlord holds the deposit: unlike a patient's wait, delay here benefits one side, so categorisation shapes outcomes and invites argument.

**Brief 3 (fermentation-culture commons, public-library analogy).** k1, copyability, is the most salient: 80 people waiting for one jar on four-week loans exposes artificial scarcity, and many replies will catch it. k2 is hidden because the kefir contamination is told as a volunteer top-up incident, not a lending problem; the reply must see that "returned jars go back on the shelf" is the same failure path at scale. k3 needs the volunteers' remark about their rye portions behaving differently to be joined to the donors' request to keep original lines going, which points to a never-circulating reference stock. k4, per-item holding cost, rests on the single feeder at his limit and the two jars per culture; Ines's "books that need feeding" line invites treating maintenance as a minor add-on rather than the binding constraint.
