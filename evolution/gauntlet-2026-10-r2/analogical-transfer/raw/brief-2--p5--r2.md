**Short answer:** Tell the committee that emergency-department triage fits only part of your problem. Triage decides who waits. It does not shorten the wait, and your backlog is a small capacity gap plus wasted hearing slots. Pilot the parts of the emergency-department model that fit (assessment at the front door, streams, and managing no-shows), and test urgency categories as a separate, controlled arm that does not rely on the free-text box.

## What an emergency department does that a tribunal can and cannot copy

**Triage reorders a queue. It does not shrink it.** In an emergency department the queue clears because most patients are treated and discharged within hours, and the sickest are seen first. Your arrivals (640 a month) exceed your closures (about 590 a month). Putting category 1 cases at the front means category 5 cases wait longer, and the total waiting does not fall. "As capacity allows" has no limit, and with a backlog growing by 50 a month, category 5 would in practice never be heard. Emergency departments cap this with maximum-wait targets and diversion rules when overloaded. Dr Messer will know this, so ask him what the category 5 equivalent of the four-hour target is.

**Your gap is smaller than 9,400 cases suggests.** If about 60% of filings end without a hearing, you need roughly 256 hearings a month to keep pace (640 × 0.4). You are short by about 50 closures a month, and that is a shortfall of roughly 20 hearings a month, or about 8% of hearing capacity. That is a scheduling problem you can close by recovering wasted slots, and it does not call for rationing. (This is my arithmetic from your figures. Check it against your actual hearing counts.)

**Emergency departments do not triage on self-report.** A nurse assesses the patient, and the patient's own account of urgency is one input. Your design sets the category from a box that 70% of tenants complete. Once the category sets the wait, the box becomes a lever. Expect urgency language to inflate within a few months, and expect the 30% who skip the box to be placed lower. That is a literacy and representation effect, not a difference in need. The triage officer should score from verifiable facts on the file: a tenancy-end or eviction notice, a documented move-in deadline for a new tenancy, the amount held, and evidence of hardship. The free-text box then becomes a prompt for the officer to check, not the basis for the category.

**The deposit rule makes tiering risky.** The landlord holds the money until a ruling or settlement, so every month of delay benefits the landlord. In a tiered system, a landlord facing a category 4 or 5 claim knows the tenant will wait. That removes the landlord's reason to settle and gives them reason to stall. Since 85% of claims are tenant claims, the low categories will be filled mostly with tenants. Landlord-filed claims will rarely look urgent, so they will sit at the bottom and be heard only when capacity allows. Check that against your equality and fairness obligations before January.

## What does carry over: the front-end assessment and the no-show problem

1. **Front-door assessment with streams.** Emergency departments run a fast-track stream for simple cases alongside the main one, so quick cases do not queue behind long ones. A triage officer who reads each filing in 48 hours can do this for you. Sort into straightforward deposit disputes (undisputed condition, a clear amount, short hearing), complex ones (damage, contested inventory) and ones likely to settle. Give each stream its own hearing length. Hearing slots matched to the case length recover capacity that today's identical slots waste.

2. **No-show management, as outpatient clinics and airlines handle it.** Your wasted slots are the same problem as a clinic where 60% of appointments cancel late. Clinics overbook against the expected cancellation rate, keep a standby list that can fill a slot within days, and send reminders that require an answer. For you, that means scheduling more cases per hearing day than adjudicator time covers, and keeping a list of ready-to-go cases that can take a slot released by a late settlement. Because most settlements land in the four weeks before the hearing, that is when the standby list does its work. Date the standby cases from readiness, not from receipt.

3. **Settlement is driven by the date.** Parties settle when a hearing date is near. Today you give every case a date at filing, which is up to 14 months away, and the date then does no work for most of that time. Instead, set an early conference or a response deadline roughly 4 to 6 weeks after filing. Issue the hearing notice only once that has passed and the case is still live. That brings the settlement pressure forward and moves it ahead of the point where slots are prepared.

4. **Clean out the waiting room.** A 14-month queue will contain cases that have already settled or been abandoned without anyone telling the tribunal. A written "confirm you are still pursuing this" step on the oldest cases is the cheapest way to shrink the 9,400 figure. Emergency departments call this "left without being seen" and count it. You should too.

## Recommended pilot design

Run it from January as two separate tests so you can tell which change did the work. Otherwise a drop in waiting will be credited to triage when it came from slot recovery.

- **Test A (scheduling and settlement), applies to all new filings:** early conference, hearing notice issued afterwards, overbooking set against your observed late-settlement rate, a standby list, and stream-based hearing lengths. Start with modest overbooking and adjust monthly.
- **Test B (urgency categories), applies to a subset:** assign filings alternately by week of receipt, or by the last digit of the case number, to a triage arm and a first-come arm. If the committee will not accept that, run the triage arm on one adjudicator's list and compare against the same period last year. Use the officer's scoring on verifiable file facts. Cap the wait for category 5 at a stated figure, say 9 months, and promote any case that has waited past its limit.
- **Measure before January:** the actual split of closures into settled, withdrawn and heard; the share of settlements per week before the hearing date; hearing length by case type; and the current share of tenant filings that mention urgency.
- **Measure during the pilot:** median and 90th-percentile wait by category and by filer type (tenant or landlord); slot utilisation (slots used as a share of slots prepared); the share of filings placed in categories 1 and 2 each month (a rising share signals gaming); settlement timing; and complaints and review requests.
- **Stop conditions:** halt the triage arm if the 90th-percentile wait in categories 4 and 5 passes the cap, if landlord-filed claims wait longer than tenant-filed claims at the same category without a reason you can state, or if categories 1 and 2 grow beyond a share you set in advance.
- **Resourcing:** a triage officer reading 640 filings a month is about 30 a working day. At 10 minutes each that is five hours a day, so it is roughly a full post, not a part of someone's job. Say so in the proposal.

## What to say to the committee

Triage is the right idea for the front door: look at each filing early, sort it, and match it to the right hearing. It is the wrong idea if it means promising some tenants a hearing in three weeks while the rest wait without limit. The backlog grows because 50 more cases a month come in than go out, and rationing the queue leaves that unchanged. Closing roughly 8% of hearing capacity through better scheduling would stop the growth. The remaining backlog can then be cleared with a one-off effort, such as extra adjudicator days for the oldest cases once the confirm-still-live step has cleared the dead ones. Offer Dr Messer his urgency categories as a tested arm, with safeguards, and use the pilot to show whether they add anything beyond the scheduling changes.

## Where I could be wrong

- I assumed the 60% settle-or-withdraw rate applies evenly to filings and to the backlog. If older cases settle at a different rate, the gap of about 20 hearings a month will be larger or smaller.
- I assumed that adjudicator time, not courtroom space, notice periods or statutory deadlines, is the binding limit. If statute fixes notice periods or sets a hearing deadline, the early-conference and delayed-notice change needs checking against it.
- I am recalling emergency-department and clinic practice (maximum-wait targets, fast-track streams, overbooking against no-show rates) from general knowledge. I have not checked the numbers. Dr Messer can correct me on the emergency-department side.
- If many tenants face real hardship (loss of housing, no money for a new deposit), a three-week category 1 may be worth more than I have allowed. Your own data on the hardship share would change how large the fast lane should be.
- If landlords do not stall in practice, because of reputational or licensing consequences, the settlement-incentive concern shrinks. Look at the settlement timing of past long-waiting cases to see.
