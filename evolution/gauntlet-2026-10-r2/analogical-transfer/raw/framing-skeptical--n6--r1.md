Your doubts are justified, but I wouldn't put "reject" in front of the board. The core idea, paying people to use less when water is scarce, is sound. The grid version of it rests on assumptions that don't hold on the Lennick, and as drafted it would probably spend money without moving much water to the farms that run short. I'd take a redesigned, capped pilot to the vote.

**Where the electricity analogy holds**

- Some growers value water at well below what it's worth to others. Hay and grass seed in a dry July are the obvious cases. A payment can buy that water out voluntarily, which is cheaper and less contentious than rationing by decree.
- Paying for measured reductions is more acceptable to growers than a penalty.

**Where it breaks down**

1. **The timing is wrong.** Demand response works because grid load can be cut and verified within minutes to hours, and because the saving is the same wherever it happens. Your system has a three-day lag to the Lower Canal intake and up to five days to the tail. An hourly price can't steer behaviour that only pays off days later. What you need is a call with a lead time of at least five days. For a day-to-day dispatch signal, an app price is the wrong tool.

2. **A shortage isn't a slow peak.** A peak is a few hours of excess demand, and much of the load is later shifted rather than lost. Your problem is a season-long volume deficit: about 18% short across July and August. There is no off-peak period in which a skipped irrigation gets made up, so every cubic metre saved is a real yield cost to someone. That makes the price much higher than grid-style payments suggest, and it means the programme has to be sized in volume against a known gap.

3. **Location matters.** On a grid, a megawatt saved anywhere is a megawatt saved. On the river:
   - Water saved at the Upper Canal stays in the river, but about 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back as drainage. Leaving 1 unit in the river therefore nets the Lower Canal roughly 0.6 units, not 1, before conveyance losses. Paying the same rate per cubic metre in both places overpays for upstream reductions.
   - The Upper Canal is apple and cherry orchards. Perennial trees can't be fallowed, so the room for cuts is small and the risk to the crop is large.
   - The Lower Canal's barley, hay and grass seed are annual crops that can be fallowed or deficit-irrigated. That is where the cheap water is.
   - Water that a Lower Canal grower doesn't divert is only useful to the shortage if it reaches the tail. Reductions near the head of the Lower Canal can do that. A reduction at the tail itself saves nothing for anyone else, because there is nobody below.

4. **The reward baseline is a problem.** Paying for use below a grower's own three-year average invites gaming: inflate this year's early diversions, or reduce slightly and collect. It also pays growers for water they might not have been able to take in a shortage anyway. And because the tail-enders already absorbed nearly all of the last two shortfalls, their three-year averages are depressed. They would be paid little to cut, while the head-end growers with higher baselines would collect the most. Whoever benefits least from the programme is the group that has been hurt most.

5. **Who pays, and who gains, is an equity question the grid doesn't have.** In electricity, the utility pays and the system benefits as a whole. Here, the money comes from the board's levy, which the tail-enders also pay. If the programme is funded from general levies, you are in effect asking short farms to fund the programme that is supposed to relieve them.

**What I would put in front of the board**

- **Replace the hourly price and three-year baseline with a pre-season option.** Before the season, growers (mainly Lower Canal hay, barley and grass seed) enrol a declared volume they would give up on call. They are paid a small standby fee up front, and a larger payment per cubic metre only if the board calls it. Set the baseline by a declared pre-season irrigation plan, verified by metering, rather than historical averages.
- **Trigger on storage, not on hourly price.** Call a shortage event from a reservoir-storage and inflow forecast, with at least five days' notice, so the water released (or not released) from Tarrow lines up with the cut.
- **Price by location.** Pay more per cubic metre for reductions that actually raise tail-end supply (head and middle of the Lower Canal) and discount upstream reductions by the roughly 0.6 net return factor. Consider limiting Upper Canal enrolment to optional deficit irrigation, not fallowing.
- **Cap the pilot.** Size it to a target volume against the 18% gap, for example the volume needed to cut the tail's shortfall by half. Set a spending ceiling and say clearly how it is funded.
- **Fix the cheaper things alongside it.** If the tail is taking nearly all the shortfall, look at the canal's delivery rules and rotation schedule first, and at metering at turnouts. A programme can't work if you can't measure who diverted what. It may turn out that fairer proportional sharing of the shortage does more than a payment scheme, and costs nothing.
- **Define success before the vote.** For example: tail-end shortfall below X% in a comparable year, cost per cubic metre delivered to the tail below a stated figure, and no more than a stated amount of gaming detected.

**What I don't know and would check before the vote**

- The legal position on voluntarily forgoing and being paid for water covered by a right, and whether it could weaken the right.
- The actual volume of the 18% shortfall, and what growers on each canal would accept to give up water.
- The conveyance losses between the intakes, which would adjust the 0.6 figure.
- Whether the board can fund a standby payment from existing reserves.

Odile's experience is a useful asset: the mechanics of enrolment, metering and settlement are things she knows well. The issue is the design, not the idea. I'd present it to her as "your approach, adapted to a river that has delay, geography and a tail", which is a better position than a head-on rejection.
