Short answer: not as proposed, but a reworked version of it could be worth putting in front of the board. Odile's grid experience is useful. Several features of your river make the straight copy a poor fit, though, and I'd rather you hear that now than after the vote.

**Why the electricity playbook doesn't carry over directly**

1. **Timing.** Demand response works because power is delivered instantly and an hourly price changes behaviour within the hour. Your system has a three-day lag to the Lower intake and up to five days to the tail of the canal. A grower who cuts back at 2pm today changes what the tail sees roughly a week from now. An hourly price would mostly generate noise. The decisions that matter here are made days ahead: release volumes from Tarrow and how much each intake takes.

2. **Peak versus gap.** A grid peak is a few hours. Customers shift or skip a load and often make it up afterwards. Your shortfall is about 18% over July and August, which is a sustained volume gap with little rebound. Orchard trees can't "shift" irrigation by a few weeks. Asking orchardists to cut for a sustained stretch is a crop-damage question, not a price question.

3. **Geography.** On a grid, a megawatt saved anywhere is roughly a megawatt available everywhere. On your river, a cubic metre saved only helps the tail of the Lower Canal if it is saved upstream of them, and the Upper Canal's savings are discounted by the return flow. If about 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back, then cutting Upper diversion by 1 m³ nets only about 0.6 m³ more at the Lower intake, because 0.4 m³ of return flow disappears with it. (That assumes the 40% scales with diversion. If much of it is fixed seepage, the news is better. Your gauge records can tell you.)

4. **Baselines.** Paying growers for use "below their own three-year average" is the weakest part. Anyone who expects the programme can inflate use beforehand. Anyone who would have used less anyway (a cool July, a block going out of production, a fallowed field) gets paid for water they weren't going to use. And two of the last three summers were short years, so the average is already depressed. In electricity, baseline gaming is a known and expensive headache even with interval meters. Do you have metering on every offtake? I've assumed you don't have it everywhere.

5. **Who it helps.** Your actual problem is that the tail of the Lower Canal absorbed nearly all of the shortfall. A price signal doesn't address that. It's a distribution problem inside the canal, and a market tends to favour whoever can pay, which is probably not the tail-enders.

**A rough sense of scale (my assumptions, please replace with your numbers)**

Suppose the 18% is of Lower Canal demand, and per-hectare use is similar on both canals. Then the Upper Canal diverts about 0.62 of what the Lower does (4,200 ha against 6,800 ha). Closing the gap through Upper cuts alone would take about 0.18 ÷ 0.6 = 0.3 of Lower demand, which is nearly half of everything the Upper Canal diverts. Even a generous 10% voluntary cut upstream would recover only around 4% of Lower demand. A voluntary programme aimed at the orchards is therefore unlikely to cover the gap by itself, and a half-cut in cherry and apple water is not something to ask for casually.

**An idea that may suit your system better**

The cheapest water to buy is probably the lowest-value water, and that is likely in the Lower Canal itself: barley, hay and grass seed. Hay and grass seed growers at the head and middle of the Lower Canal can be paid to cut back, and that water goes directly to the tail with no return-flow loss and little lag. Compare that with the orchards, where each cubic metre is worth much more and you only get 0.6 of it back. Orchard participation could be a smaller, targeted add-on, for example reduced irrigation after harvest or at stages your agronomist says tolerate stress.

**What I'd put in front of the board**

Rather than "adopt the demand response programme", ask for a decision on a pilot for next summer, designed around four changes.

- **Standby contracts, signed before the season.** Growers enrol in spring and get a small retainer, plus a per-m³ payment if the board calls an event. This replaces the hourly price. It gives you a known volume of response you can plan around, rather than hoping for one.
- **Calls made 5 to 7 days ahead**, tied to the reservoir release schedule and the travel times, not hourly.
- **Baselines that can't be gamed.** Use a declared seasonal allocation or a crop-based water budget, not a user's own history, and pay on metered diversion. Credit Upper Canal cuts at about 0.6 of the volume, or price them accordingly.
- **A sharing rule inside the Lower Canal.** Whatever the programme buys, a fair rotation or proportional-share rule for the remaining shortage stops the tail-enders taking nearly all of it every time. This could be the most valuable single change, and it needs no payments at all.

**Open questions that would change the recommendation**

- Where does the money come from? Lower Canal growers are the main beneficiaries, so is a levy on them acceptable?
- Does a grower who uses less water risk losing rights under a use-it-or-lose-it rule? If so, enrolment will be low unless the board protects them.
- Are all offtakes metered, and at what time resolution?
- What is the real shortfall in cubic metres, and when in July and August does it bite? That decides whether this is about a few weeks (more promising) or the whole period (much harder).

**What to do in the five weeks before the vote**

1. Build a simple water balance for the last three summers from the gauge and diversion records, including the actual return fraction at different diversion levels.
2. Check metering coverage.
3. Sound out a dozen growers on both canals, asking what standby fee and per-m³ price would get them to enrol.
4. Bring the board a capped pilot, with a budget limit, a review after one season, and a decision on whether to scale up.

That keeps Odile's central idea, that you can pay people to use less water at the right time and that this is cheaper than a shortage, while fitting it to a river with a five-day lag and a downstream tail that carries the loss.
