**Short answer:** Ines's rules are a good fit for books and a poor fit for living cultures. Keep her membership structure, her waiting list and her instinct for clear rules. Change the core mechanic: members should receive a portion that they keep, not a jar they return. Returned jars should never go back on the shelf. That one change fixes your three biggest problems (the 80-person queue, Teodor's workload and contamination), and it needs to be settled before the cards are printed.

## Analogies Investigated
1. **Public library (Ines's model).** It works because a book is inert and can't be copied. Lending a single item to a single person is the only way to share it, and returning it unchanged is easy. A culture is the opposite: it grows, it can be split, and it changes while it is out of your hands.
2. **Seed libraries.** Members take seed, grow it, and return some of the next generation instead of the original packet. The collection is kept going by many people, not by one shelf. This is closest to what you are doing.
3. **Microbial culture collections (the kind that supply labs).** They keep a protected master stock that is never used for distribution, plus working stocks that are. Orders are filled from the working stock, and what customers hold is a copy. Nobody sends a used tube back to the master freezer, because one stray organism ruins a line.
4. **Pass-along sourdough and kefir communities.** Starters have been shared as portions for generations. The line survives because many households hold it, not because one institution does.

## Transferable Principles
- **Share copies, not the original.** Cultures replicate, so a queue is only needed if you insist on lending one physical jar. At four weeks per loan, 80 people waiting on the rye starter is about six years of queue. Splitting portions turns it into a few batches.
- **Keep a protected master that nothing re-enters.** This is the lesson of last spring's kefir loss. A jar that has left the building can carry anything back in. The rule has to be absolute: nothing returns to a master or shelf jar, from a member or from a volunteer's home.
- **Many holders are a backup.** Families asked that their line be kept going. A line held by 30 households survives a fridge failure or a contaminated jar far better than two jars in one back room.
- **Treat the master as the reference, and expect copies to drift.** Home portions of the rye smell and rise differently after a few months because kitchen temperature, flour and feeding rhythm select for different organisms. That is normal biology, not a fault. It is a reason to refresh from the master, not to chase the home versions.

## Proposed Solutions
1. **Portion-based "adoption" instead of loans.** A member receives a labelled portion (a spoonful of starter, a few kefir grains, a piece of mother) with a feeding card. It is theirs to keep and feed.
2. **Master and working tiers.** Keep the two existing jars as a sealed master and a backup, fed on schedule and never handed out. Add a working jar per popular culture. Portions come from the working jar, and it is re-seeded from the master every few months.
3. **Split days, not a rolling queue.** Hold a monthly distribution day for each in-demand culture. Allocate the rye by sign-up date or lottery and cap each member at one or two cultures at a time. A healthy rye starter can usually seed many portions from one feeding, so 80 requests becomes two or three sessions.
4. **Replace fines with a restock rule.** Overdue fees and replacement charges make no sense for something the member owns and feeds. In their place: members who lose their portion can ask for a fresh one from the master, and members are asked (not forced) to bring back a labelled starter portion to the quarantine bench if they ever want to donate. Anything they bring back is checked and kept separate from the master.
5. **Dry backups.** Starters and koji can be dried and stored, and kefir grains can be dried for short periods. A few dried reserves per culture mean Teodor does not have to keep every line actively fed in the fridge at all times, and they protect against a repeat of the kefir loss.

## Disanalogies and Risks
- **Seed and lab analogies have stricter hygiene than you do.** Labs have sterile technique and seed is dormant. A fridge in a back room and volunteer hands are neither. Contamination protocols (a dedicated spoon per jar, no home jars in the room, separate handling of anything brought back) matter more for you than for seed.
- **Not every culture splits equally.** Kefir grains grow slowly and can be stressed by moving. Vinegar mothers and koji behave differently. Some cultures may need a smaller allocation or an invite-only list.
- **Portions leave your control.** A member may feed a culture poorly or mix it with their own. Your promise to families becomes "the master line is kept pure", not "every portion stays pure". You should say that on the donor paperwork.
- **A library's fairness feels simple.** Members may like the "return it and the next person gets it" story. Expect questions about why nothing comes back, and put the reason (kefir loss, drift) on the card.
- **Food safety.** Giving out portions is closer to giving out food than lending an object. A short note ("keep like a food; discard if it smells or looks off") and some wording on liability are worth getting reviewed locally.

## Recommendation
Do not print the borrowing terms on the cards. Print membership only, and put the rules on a separate sheet you can still change. Then:

1. Replace "borrow one jar for four weeks, two renewals" with "adopt a portion, keep it, feed it".
2. Delete the overdue fee and the replacement charge. Offer a free re-seed from the master instead.
3. Write the master rule as a hard policy: no jar, from members or volunteers, ever goes back into a master or backup jar. Make the volunteer rule explicit, given what happened with the kefir.
4. Keep Ines's holds queue, but make it a sign-up list for a distribution day. Keep her per-member limits.
5. Pick one culture, probably the 1890s rye, and pilot the portion model before March. It will show you quickly how many portions one feeding really produces.
6. Start a dry reserve for each culture, beginning with the most valued lines, and use it to reduce Teodor's feeding rounds.

Ines's library experience is still useful here: cards, limits, a clear sign-up list and a single set of written rules are exactly what you need. The change is in what moves across the counter.

## Where I could be wrong
- I'm assuming your cultures can be split into portions and survive a few weeks in a household. Starters and most kefir grains do, but I'm recalling this from general knowledge, not from your specific lines. Test each type before promising it.
- I've guessed that one rye feeding yields enough for many portions. If your starter is small or slow, the 80 requests will take longer, and you may need a lottery.
- I don't know your donor agreements. If some families want their line never to leave the building, those cultures need a different rule (viewing or tasting only, or no distribution).
- Drying works well for some cultures and not others, and the methods I mentioned are from memory. If a culture doesn't survive drying, the workload savings shrink, and you would need a second volunteer feeder instead.
- If your local food-safety rules treat giving away cultures as food distribution, you may need registration or labelling that I haven't accounted for.
