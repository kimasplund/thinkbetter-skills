**Short answer:** Not as proposed. Odile's core idea, paying people to use less when water is scarce, is sound and worth keeping. But the grid playbook assumes things that are false on your river, and two of its parts (the hourly price and the three-year-average baseline) would likely make your problem worse. I'd take a reworked version to the board as a one-season pilot, alongside a fix to how shortage is shared.

## Why the electricity analogy breaks

Demand response works because a peak is short, power is delivered instantly, one kWh is as good as any other, and a customer who cuts back changes nothing for anyone else except by freeing up supply. Your system differs on each point.

1. **It is a season-long volume problem, not a peak.** Eighteen percent short across July and August is about 60 days of deficit. A grid curtails for a few hours and customers recover afterwards. Here the water is not recoverable. Your closer analogue is an energy-limited hydro system: the question is who gives up how much volume over the whole season.

2. **Three to five days of lag makes an hourly price meaningless.** Water a grower declines to divert today reaches the Lower intake in about three days and the tail in up to five. An hourly signal can't steer that. Any call has to be made days ahead, as a forecast-based notice, and growers have to be able to plan around it.

3. **Return flows mean an upstream cut is worth only about 60% of its face value.** About 40% of what the Upper Canal diverts comes back to the river above the Lower intake. If an Upper grower leaves 100 m³ in the river, the Lower Canal gains about 60 m³, because the return flow that 100 m³ would have generated is lost. A cut by a Lower grower is worth the full amount to the system. The payment rate needs a location factor (roughly 0.6 for Upper, 1.0 for Lower), or you will overpay for the least effective water. Treat 40% as an approximation. It comes from one gauge, and it varies with the season.

4. **The two groups are not equally able to cut back.** The Upper Canal is almost entirely apple and cherry, which are perennial. Skipping irrigation in a hot July can cost the crop and damage the trees for years, so those growers will either decline to enrol or will demand very high prices. The Lower Canal grows barley, hay and grass seed. These can be fallowed, deficit-irrigated or cut early at a cost that is known and modest. The cheapest water to free up is mostly in the Lower Canal. That is also where the shortfall already lands.

5. **A three-year-average baseline is the wrong yardstick.** Two of the last three summers were short, so the average already contains the shortage.
   - Tail-end growers received little water in those years. Their average is low, so they would earn almost nothing for a real sacrifice, or they would be paid for cuts they were going to suffer anyway.
   - Anyone who knows the scheme is coming has a reason to divert more this summer to lift their baseline. Grid programmes spend a lot of effort on baseline gaming for the same reason.
   - It pays for "reductions" that are not reductions: a grower who planned to fallow a field anyway collects money for it.

6. **Equity.** The tail of the Lower Canal has absorbed nearly all of the shortfall already. A voluntary market layered on top of that could turn it into a permanent arrangement where those with the least water are the ones asked, or pushed by circumstance, to sell more of it. Your board will feel that politically, whatever the economics say.

## What I would put in front of the board

**Recommendation:** Do not adopt the hourly price app. Adopt a pre-contracted, volume-based water-saving option programme as a pilot for next summer, and in parallel agree a written shortage-sharing rule. Treat the programme as a supplement to that rule, not a substitute for it.

**Design, in brief:**

- **Pay for options before the season, then for exercise.** Growers sign up by spring and receive a modest option payment for agreeing to be callable. If a shortage is called, they receive a per-m³ (or per-hectare fallowed) exercise payment. This gives you a known volume on hand in July, which a voluntary "whenever you feel like it" programme cannot.
- **Call it days ahead.** Set a trigger, for example forecast reservoir storage or forecast flow at the Lower intake gauge, with notice of at least 5 to 7 days. Match it to the lag.
- **Replace the three-year average with an agreed baseline.** Use a declared plan for the season (crop, area, expected volume) filed before the season starts, checked against crop area and metered use. Where meters or area records are missing, pay per hectare fallowed or per verified deficit-irrigated hectare, which is much easier to verify than a volume against a history.
- **Weight payments by location and timing.** Use roughly 0.6 for Upper Canal reductions and 1.0 for Lower Canal. Give a higher rate for cuts that land in the weeks with the worst deficit.
- **Aim recruitment at annual crops.** Hay and grass seed are the obvious first targets. Orchards should be allowed to opt in, but don't plan around them. For orchards the better lever may be a small payment for investment in efficiency (drip, scheduling, soil-moisture sensors) rather than a shortage call.
- **Treat the tail-enders as a priority, not a casualty.** Either guarantee them a minimum delivery or give them first claim on any water the programme frees, and consider a rotation or proportional-sharing rule so the shortage isn't automatically theirs.

**A rough sense of scale (assumptions, please check against your own figures):** Suppose per-hectare water use is similar across crops (orchards in fact use more, which makes this conservative). An 18% shortfall on 11,000 ha is about 2,000 ha-equivalent of water. If you got that mostly from the Lower Canal's annual crops at full-value, that is roughly 30% of their area, which is a lot. In practice you'd cover it with a mix of fallowing, deficit irrigation, some Upper Canal participation, and improved delivery efficiency. This is why I'd run it as a pilot with a target well under the full gap in year one, rather than promise to close 18%.

**What to ask the board to decide in five weeks:**

1. Approve a pilot, not a permanent scheme, with a stated target (for example, secure a defined share of the gap, say a third to a half, through contracts) and a budget cap.
2. Approve the shortage-sharing principle, even if the detailed rule follows later.
3. Commission, before the vote if possible, three numbers:
   - Metering coverage: how many turnouts are actually metered, and how accurately?
   - The return-flow share measured over several weeks, rather than the "usually 40%" figure.
   - A short grower survey or a few conversations about what price would get Lower Canal growers of hay and grass seed to fallow or cut back.
4. Confirm legal position: whether water rights and board bylaws allow the board to pay for non-diversion and to reallocate saved water, and whether saved water retains the grower's right (use-it-or-lose-it rules often kill these schemes).

**What to say to Odile:** Her instinct is right on two things: voluntary participation with payment is cheaper and fairer than blunt rationing, and an advance price signal can change behaviour. What differs is the physics. Water has a multi-day lag, return flows, perennial crops and a seasonal volume problem, so the programme has to be an advance-contract scheme with a fixed baseline, not a spot market. Framed that way it is her playbook adapted, which should be easier to get through the board than a rejection.

**Risks to name:** thin participation in the first year; disputes over baselines and verification; payment cost if drought is severe in consecutive years; and the political risk that growers read it as a way to buy water away from the Lower Canal for the benefit of the Upper. A clear published rule about who gets the freed water addresses the last of these.

**Assumptions I made:** that most turnouts are or can be metered; that the board has some budget and legal authority for payments; that the 18% shortfall is relative to full seasonal demand; and that the 40% return share holds roughly in July and August. If any of these is wrong, tell me and I'll adjust the design.
