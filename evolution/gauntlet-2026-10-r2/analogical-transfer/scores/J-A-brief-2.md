- R0I: Gets the main points right: triage reorders the queue rather than shrinking it, category 5 means "never", and the free-text box rewards good writers. Interim release of the undisputed part of the deposit is a strong practical lever. The pilot has caps, an ageing rule, alternating-week comparison and stop rules. It says clearly where it could be wrong and gives the cheapest check to make before the meeting. The claim that "patients rarely exaggerate" is shaky. It does not cost the triage officer post.
- C2F: The strongest diagnosis. It shows that tiering removes landlords' incentive to settle, that landlord-filed claims will sink to the bottom, and that dead cases sit in the backlog. It splits the pilot into two tests so the effect of each change can be told apart, gives baseline measures to collect before January, and costs the triage officer at about one full post. The derived figure of about 20 hearings a month is flagged as an assumption. Long, but each section is useful.
- I1T: Sound and clearly structured, with a pilot arm comparison and leading indicators. It is less specific: no cap values or ageing threshold, and the decision rule is generic. "The queue clears overnight" overstates ED practice, and "probably within what six adjudicators can do" rests on no data.
- P8D: Covers the core points, including an escrow suggestion and a 20% cap. The backlog-clearance figure is wrong: clearing it in two years needs roughly 440 more closures a month, not 390. It assumes, without flagging it, that landlords have no urgency box. The assumptions section is thin.
- M6Q: A good practical pilot: a 10% priority cap, a 9 to 12 month long-stop date, and a concrete continue/stop rule tied to 640 closures a month. It raises a subtle risk, that early-settlement pressure can disadvantage unrepresented tenants. The three-year clearance arithmetic is off: about 900 closures a month are needed, not 850. "Nothing drains it" overstates the case.
- L1I: Good insight on resource-based ED triage and readiness checks. It proposes replacing the free-text box with structured, checkable questions, and suggests that a case where the landlord has not responded should move up. It reserves slots for old cases. There are no explicit stop rules, and the overbooking reasoning is a little muddled.
