Short answer: I would not circulate it as written. The arithmetic is fine, but the conclusion ("clearly worked, approve Kessel Street") goes further than the evidence supports, and one claim is contradicted by the report's own dates. Here is what I found, in the order a member would hit it.

**1. The "12 weeks after" cannot be right**
The lane opened on 3 February and the data stops on 31 March. That is 8 weeks, not 12. A 12-week "after" window would run to about 28 April. Either the after period is shorter than stated, or the end date is wrong. Mara needs to say which, because the 30-minute figure rests on it. The "before" window (roughly mid-November to 2 February) also covers the Christmas and New Year period, which is not like February and March.

**2. The 14% figure is arithmetically correct but not yet attributable to the lane**
35 to 30 minutes is 5/35, or 14.3%, so the number is right. But it is a simple before/after comparison on one route with no comparison group. Winter weather, school terms, roadworks, holiday traffic and any timetable changes all differ between the two windows. To attribute the gain to the lane, the report should show the same period for a comparable route or road without a lane, or at least the same weeks a year earlier. It should also give the spread of journey times (median, and the worst 10% of trips), since reliability often matters more to passengers than the average.

**3. "That rise confirms passengers were getting faster journeys" is the weakest sentence**
A 9% ridership rise does not confirm anything about cause. Ridership normally dips over the Christmas period and recovers in the new year, so a before-period that includes the holidays and an after-period that doesn't will show a rise with no lane at all. Other possible causes include fuel prices, fares, employer return-to-office patterns and weather. Journey-time changes also rarely produce a measurable ridership response within 8 weeks. The most the report can say is that ridership rose over the same period, and that it has not separated the lane from other explanations. This should be reworded or cut, and it needs the same comparison group as point 2.

**4. The comments are treated inconsistently, and the discarded ones matter most for Kessel**
Thirty-eight driver comments are used as support, while 61 shopkeeper comments are set aside as "anecdotal and not representative". Both groups are self-selected written comments, so if one is anecdotal so is the other. Drivers also gain directly from a faster route, so their views are not independent of the result. The shopkeepers' comments are about delivery access, which is the risk that matters most on Kessel, with its 14 loading bays used by the shops along it. Discarding more than the number of comments in favour is not defensible. I would ask Mara to code and summarise the 61 comments (how many report lost deliveries, lost trade, fines, double parking) and put them in front of members.

**5. "Under budget" says nothing about whether it worked**
£410,000 against £450,000 shows the pilot was well managed, not that it delivered benefits. It also says nothing about what a Kessel scheme would cost. Kessel has two routes and loading bays that would probably need relocating or rescheduling, so the Varrow cost is a poor guide.

**6. The gap between Varrow Road and Kessel Street is never examined**
The report has data on one route. Kessel carries routes 22 and 31 and is one lane each way. Members need to know how Varrow Road compares on road width, number of loading bays and how they were handled, bus frequency, and general traffic volume. They also need to know what happened to other traffic and to deliveries on Varrow Road. If Varrow Road is wider or has few bays, its result tells you little about Kessel.

**What the evidence does support**
Route 7 journey times were shorter after the lane opened, by an amount that is plausible and worth following up. That is a reasonable basis for further work, not for a decision on Thursday.

**What I would ask Mara for before circulation**
1. The correct length of the after window, and the figures re-run on a like-for-like basis, ideally against a comparison route or the same weeks last year.
2. Median and worst-decile journey times, not just the average.
3. Removal or rewording of the causal claim about ridership, with seasonal ridership shown for comparison.
4. A proper summary of the 61 shopkeeper comments, treated on the same footing as the driver comments.
5. A short comparison of Varrow Road and Kessel Street, and an estimated Kessel cost including loading-bay changes.
6. A recommendation reworded to match: for example, that the pilot shows promising journey-time gains, and that the committee asks for a Kessel-specific assessment, or approves a time-limited trial with loading arrangements agreed first.

If you need something for Thursday, the last option is the one the current evidence supports.
