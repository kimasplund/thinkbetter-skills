Your instinct is right. The memo doesn't answer the question you asked, and its main capacity claim fails on its own numbers.

**1. Capacity: it doesn't fit.**
Two shifts at 4,500 loaves each is 9,000 loaves a day. You bake about 6,100 now. Adding Pellam's 4,200 gives 10,300, which is about 1,300 a day over capacity (roughly 14%). The memo says Pellam "fits comfortably", but the figures show the opposite. That's the exact thing you asked Tomasz about.

Closing the gap means one of these:
- a third shift,
- more oven capacity,
- overtime, or
- cutting back on your café and restaurant accounts.

The last one is the let-down you were worried about. Any of the others adds cost the memo doesn't count.
- The contract is "every day of the year". That leaves no room for oven maintenance, breakdowns, staff sickness or a bad day.
- Delivery by 6 a.m. to 11 stores is also a logistics job. The memo doesn't mention vans, drivers or start times.
- I'm assuming the 4,500 is per shift as written. If it's really a daily total, the problem is much worse. Please check.

**2. The margin is probably overstated.**
The arithmetic is fine: £0.41 × 4,200 × 365 is about £628,500. But it assumes:
- **£0.97 is the true cost of the extra loaves.** If this is average cost, the extra capacity, overtime, delivery and packaging for 6 a.m. supermarket drops may cost more per loaf.
- **The price is fixed for three years.** If flour, energy or wages rise, the £0.41 shrinks, and the memo doesn't say whether there's a price-adjustment clause.
- **Nothing is lost elsewhere.** If you lose even a few café accounts, their margin comes off the £628k. Cafés probably pay more per loaf than £1.38, so check this.
- **Volume is certain.** The memo doesn't say whether 4,200 a day is guaranteed, or whether Pellam can order fewer.

It's also gross margin. The memo should net out the extra fixed costs and any capital spend.

**3. "Low-risk customer" is an assumption, not a finding.**
A three-year commitment doesn't show Pellam is financially solid. The memo gives no credit check, accounts, payment terms or references. The commitment also doesn't obviously protect you. What matters is what the contract says on:
- whether Pellam must buy a minimum volume,
- how it can terminate and what you get if it does,
- how fast they pay,
- penalties for late or missed deliveries, and
- who bears the cost if you fall short.

Supermarket contracts often favour the buyer. A long term could lock you in while leaving them able to cut volumes or push back on price.

There's also concentration risk. Pellam would be about 40% of your output (4,200 of 10,300), from one customer. Losing or renegotiating it would hurt.

**4. The track record is weak evidence.**
Two friendly bakers who say it was a great decision, plus "I haven't heard of a bakery it went badly for", is survivorship bias. Bakeries that were hurt may not be telling anyone, or may have closed. Two contacts is also a tiny sample. I'd ask Ines and Dmitri specific questions:
- Did they have to add capacity?
- Did they lose any existing customers?
- What do the payment terms look like?
- What happened when costs rose?

**5. What I'd do before Friday.**
1. Fix the capacity question first. Work out what a third shift or extra oven would cost, and whether you can keep all 74 accounts.
2. Rebuild the margin using incremental costs, including delivery, and include a scenario where input costs rise 10%.
3. Get the contract and have a solicitor look at volume guarantees, termination, price review, payment terms and penalties.
4. Check Pellam's finances.
5. If Friday is too soon, ask for an extension, or counter with a smaller starting volume or a phase-in (for example 2,500 a day) that fits within your capacity.

**Bottom line:** I wouldn't sign on this memo. The deal may still be worth doing, but the memo hasn't shown that it is. It makes the opportunity look better than your own figures support and doesn't answer whether you can serve existing customers. I'd treat Tomasz's conclusion as unproven until the capacity gap and the real margin are worked out.
