Don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is right, but the capacity claim is wrong, and that was the question you asked Tomasz. As written, taking Pellam would mean letting some café and restaurant customers down.

**What the corrected picture looks like**

- **Capacity.** Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day. You bake about 6,100 for your 74 accounts and Pellam wants 4,200 more, so you would need about 10,300. That is roughly 1,300 loaves a day over capacity, and it leaves no slack for a breakdown, a busy day or a day when ovens are down. Pellam wants delivery every day of the year by 6 a.m., so a miss is a breach, not an inconvenience. Today, Pellam does not "fit comfortably".
- **What that does to the £628,000.** The figure itself checks out (4,200 x £0.41 x 365 is about £628,500). But it assumes the £0.97 cost holds with the extra volume, and that only holds if you can bake it. Closing a 1,300-loaf gap means one of these:
  - a third shift or extra oven time, with labour, overtime, maintenance and possibly new equipment;
  - dropping or rationing about a fifth of your café and restaurant volume, which is exactly the outcome you wanted to avoid, and costs you their margin too.

  So £628,000 is a ceiling, not the expected profit. The real number is that minus the cost of the extra capacity (or of the lost accounts).
- **Risk.** "They offer a three-year commitment, so they're solid, and the commitment protects us" argues in a circle. A willingness to sign says nothing about solvency, and the memo doesn't say what Pellam is actually committing to. Supermarket contracts often allow volume reductions, price reviews or early exit on notice. The protection only exists if the contract guarantees minimum volumes, payment terms and a penalty for ending early. There is also a concentration issue: Pellam would be about 40% of your output and, at £1.38, roughly £2.1m a year of revenue, so losing or renegotiating it would hurt.
- **Track record.** Two friendly bakeries that did well are not evidence about how these deals usually go. You hear from the ones who are happy to talk, and "I haven't heard of one going badly" mostly reflects who Tomasz asked. It is also not clear that Corrin and Northfold were as capacity-constrained as you are. Treat them as useful for questions, not as proof.
- **Conclusion.** "The margin is big, so sign" answers a different question from yours. Whether it pays is not the same as whether you can do it without hurting existing customers, and on the numbers given you can't, not yet.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Ask Pellam for a short extension, or reply with a conditional yes rather than a signature.
2. Price the capacity gap: what a third shift or a new oven costs per year, and subtract it from £628,000. If the contract still clears a worthwhile profit, it may be a good deal. If not, you have your answer.
3. Ask for these terms: guaranteed minimum volumes, payment terms (and a credit check on Pellam), a penalty or longer notice for early exit, a price-review clause tied to flour and energy costs, and a phase-in (for example starting at 2,000 a day while you add capacity).
4. Ask Ines and Dmitri the harder questions: did they have to add capacity, did any café customers leave, and what were the payment terms?

**Still unresolved**

- Whether your £0.97 cost holds above 9,000 loaves a day. Cheapest check: ask whoever runs production to cost one extra shift.
- Whether the contract is really a volume commitment. Cheapest check: read the draft's minimum-volume and termination clauses, or ask your solicitor to.
- Whether the 4,500-per-shift figure is a true maximum or a comfortable rate. If it's a comfortable rate, the gap may be smaller.
