Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is right, but the capacity claim, which is the one you asked about, is wrong as written. The other two supports are weaker than they sound.

**What the memo should say**

Pellam's 4,200 loaves a day would pay about £628,000 a year at the quoted margin (4,200 x £0.41 x 365 = about £628,500), but only if the cost stays at £0.97. We can't deliver them from current capacity without either adding capacity or short-changing the café and restaurant accounts.

- Two shifts at 4,500 is 9,000 loaves a day. You already bake about 6,100, which leaves roughly 2,900 spare. Pellam needs 4,200, so you'd be about 1,300 loaves a day short. Total demand would be about 10,300 against 9,000 of capacity. "Fits comfortably" is simply not true.
- Closing that gap means one of three things: a third shift or other extra capacity, which raises the cost of those loaves above £0.97 and eats into the £628,000. Or cutting roughly a fifth of the supply to your 74 existing accounts, which is exactly what you asked Tomasz to avoid. Or running the ovens above 4,500 a shift, which is unlikely to be reliable.
- "Every day of the year" leaves no room for oven breakdowns, holidays or maintenance. It also means the 6,100 figure matters. If that is an average, your busiest days are higher and the gap is wider.
- A three-year commitment is not the same as a financially solid customer. A long term tells you Pellam wants a locked-in supplier. It says nothing about their finances, and it protects you only if the contract has volume guarantees (minimum purchase or take-or-pay), payment terms you can live with, and sensible exit clauses. The memo mentions none of these.
- The price is fixed at £1.38 for three years. If flour, energy or wage costs rise, the margin shrinks, and the memo doesn't say whether the contract allows price reviews.
- Pellam would also become a very large share of your output, about 40% of volume at 10,300 loaves a day. Losing them or having them renegotiate would leave you with expensive spare capacity.
- Two happy owners are not a track record. Ines and Dmitri are the ones who are still around to talk to you, and "I haven't heard of a bakery it went badly for" mostly reflects who you'd be likely to hear from. Bakeries that were squeezed on price, or lost their café trade, are less likely to be in your phone. Ask them specifically what it did to their margins, their independent customers and their capacity, and whether their deals had price reviews.
- The conclusion answers a different question from the one you asked. "It pays for itself" is about profit. Your question was whether you can do it without letting your existing customers down. On the figures given, the answer is not without new capacity.

**Still to check before Friday (cheapest first)**

1. Your real daily output ceiling, and your busiest day in the last year, not the average. Ten minutes with your production records.
2. The cost of the extra 1,300 or so loaves a day: a third shift, overtime, a leased oven, or a second site. If extra capacity costs more than about £0.41 a loaf in extra cost beyond your current £0.97, the contract stops making money on those loaves.
3. The contract terms: minimum volumes, payment days, termination rights, price review, and what happens if you miss a 6 a.m. delivery.
4. Pellam's credit position. A credit report is cheap.
5. A second look at the references, as above.

**What to send on Friday**

If you can't verify these in time, ask for a short extension, or counter-offer a smaller volume you can cover (for instance 2,500 to 2,900 loaves a day, which fits in the spare capacity you have) or a phased start while you add an oven line. Either keeps the opportunity alive without risking the 74 accounts that make up your business today.
