I would not send the paper to the board as written. The conclusion may still turn out to be right, but the paper doesn't show it. It also doesn't answer the question the board asked, which was whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red.

**1. The conclusion answers a different question.**
The paper concludes that Calder Red has the lowest yield per hectare. Income per hectare is yield times price, minus costs. Calder Red earns a £38 a tonne premium, so a lower yield doesn't settle the matter. The paper never gives Brannock's price, so the comparison can't be made. Here is a rough check, using an assumed Brannock price p, since the paper doesn't state one:
- Calder Red revenue per hectare is 4.1 × (p + 38). Brannock's is 6.3 × p.
- Calder Red comes out ahead only if p is below about £71 a tonne (4.1 × 38 = £156 per hectare, divided by the 2.2 t/ha yield gap).
- Milling grain usually sells well above that. If p is around £200, Brannock would earn roughly £280 more per hectare before costs. That would support Pieter's conclusion, but the paper has to show it with real prices and costs.
- Costs matter too, as do rotation fit, drying and any contract terms. None of them are mentioned.

**2. The demand argument is circular.**
"It has stayed a niche grain, which is exactly what you would expect of a grain with weak demand" assumes the thing it is trying to prove. A niche grain is equally what you would expect when supply is limited or the variety is hard to grow. Also, a buyer premium is evidence of demand, not of its absence. The claim that the premium "reflects scarcity rather than real demand" needs support. If mills pay extra, they want the grain. The real question is whether they'd keep paying it, or buy more, if you supplied more. The paper needs buyer data on volumes, contracts and whether mills have turned away or asked for more Calder Red.

**3. The hectare figures don't agree with each other.**
610 of 2,400 hectares is about 25%, not 19%. 19 is the number of members growing it (19 of 46, about 41%). It looks as if the member count has been used as the hectare share in the trend sentence. If the current share is 25%, the fall is from 31% to 25%, which is a smaller decline than the paper claims. The yield arithmetic is fine (610 × 4.1 is about 2,500 t). Ask Pieter which figure is correct before anyone quotes it.

**4. The 2022 storm is one anecdote.**
Two farms losing their fields in one storm doesn't show the variety is risky. To make that case you would need to know:
- whether Brannock fields nearby also lodged or were lost;
- how many of the 19 growers were affected, in that year and in others;
- what the variety's lodging record is over several seasons.
"Everyone remembers it" is vivid, but it isn't a risk estimate. If lodging is a real weakness, quantify it, for example as the loss rate across seasons.

**5. The trend is ambiguous.**
Falling share could mean members are choosing better options. It could also mean they were constrained by seed availability, buyer contracts or rotation. Even so, 19 members are still growing it after five years of decline, and they are presumably doing so for a reason. Asking them is the quickest way to find out.

**6. What the board actually needs.**
Because the vote is about next year's member earnings, the paper should say something about the 19 growers. They are the ones who would have to switch, and it should show:
- an estimate per member of the income change if they move to Brannock (or another variety) on the same land, using real prices, costs and yields;
- whether the premium contracts can be replaced or lost;
- whether the cooperative would lose volume or buyer relationships it can't easily win back;
- what the cooperative saves by dropping it (handling, storage, seed multiplication), if that is a motive.

**Suggested next step.** Return the paper to Pieter with these questions: the correct hectare share, Brannock's price and cost per hectare, mill demand data, lodging history across seasons, and the gross margin per hectare for each variety. If Brannock's price is as I assumed, the case for discontinuing is probably stronger than the paper makes it, but it has to rest on margins, not on yield alone. The decision might also be to phase Calder Red out gradually, or to keep it for the growers who earn the premium, rather than stop it outright.
