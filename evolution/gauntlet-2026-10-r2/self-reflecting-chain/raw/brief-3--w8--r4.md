The paper is not ready to go to the board as written. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper answers a different question: whether Calder Red yields less per hectare. Its conclusion doesn't follow from its own argument, and one figure is wrong. I'd send it back to Pieter for a revision, and I'd have the revised paper make the case below.

**What the paper can fairly say now**

Calder Red yields 4.1 t/ha against 6.3 for Brannock, about 35% less. Mills pay £38/t more for it. Whether that premium covers the yield gap depends on the Brannock price, which the paper doesn't give. Per hectare, Calder Red earns more only if 4.1 x (P + 38) is greater than 6.3 x P. That holds only when the Brannock price P is below about £71/t. At any realistic grain price, Brannock earns more per hectare before costs. For example, at £200/t Calder Red would need a premium of about £107/t to break even. So the yield gap does support Pieter's direction, but only once the paper shows revenue and costs. Costs matter because Calder Red may be cheaper to grow (fewer inputs, different harvest timing). Without costs, "lowest-yielding" can't be turned into "lower earning".

The paper also doesn't say what the 19 growers would plant instead, or whether Brannock suits their land and has seed available. Next year's earnings depend on those points. The honest answer today is "probably somewhat less, if the replacement is Brannock at a normal price, but the paper hasn't shown it."

**Issues that change the conclusion, with fixes**

1. **Yield is used as a stand-in for earnings.** The conclusion says "lowest-yielding variety per hectare", which doesn't answer the board's question. Also, only Brannock is compared. "Lowest-yielding" needs the yields of all the varieties you distribute. Fix: give margin per hectare for Calder Red and for the realistic replacement, using the actual Brannock price and each variety's costs.

2. **The demand argument is circular.** "It has stayed niche, which is what you'd expect of weak demand" assumes weak demand to prove it. Nothing independent supports it, and the one real market fact points the other way. Mills pay a premium. Calling that "scarcity rather than real demand" doesn't work either, because a buyer paying extra is demand. Fix: drop the sentence, or replace it with evidence such as mill order volumes, unsold stock, or whether mills would take more at the current premium.

3. **The 2022 storm is one vivid event standing in for risk.** Two farms losing crops in one storm shows that lodging can happen. It doesn't show how often, and it doesn't show that Brannock would have stood up better in the same storm. Fix: give the loss record for both varieties over several seasons, or at least say whether Brannock fields near Ashby and Toller were hit.

4. **The hectare figures contradict each other.** 610 of 2,400 hectares is about 25%, not 19%. The 19% looks like the 19 growers, which is 41% of 46 members, or a different year's figure. Please ask Pieter to reconcile this. If 25% is right, the "fall from 31%" is a smaller drop than the paper implies. The trend then needs restating, and so does anything built on it.

5. **"Members are already moving away" is only partly supported.** A falling share could reflect lower demand, but it could equally reflect seed supply, advice from the co-op, or rotation choices. It is also the strongest reason to hesitate. If members are leaving anyway, the decision may matter less than it looks, and the board should know that. Fix: ask a few of the 19 growers why they planted less.

6. **Stopping distribution could push the premium up.** Calder Red is scarce, and removing the co-op's supply would make it scarcer. Members who keep it, perhaps by buying seed elsewhere, might earn more per tonne. The paper doesn't consider whether growers can get seed elsewhere, or what happens to the 19 if they can't.

**Still unresolved, and the cheapest way to check**

- Brannock price and the cost per hectare of each variety: ask Pieter for last season's actual figures from the 19 growers' accounts.
- Which crop the 19 growers would plant in its place, and whether seed and land suit it: a short call or email to those 19 members.
- Whether the 2022 losses were unusual: the co-op's own claims or loss records.
- The correct share of hectares (19% or 25%): a check of the planting returns.

If the margin figures bear out Pieter's view, the recommendation may well stand, and the revised paper can say so with the evidence behind it. For now, the number most worth watching is the price gap between the two varieties after costs, since that decides how much members would lose or gain.
