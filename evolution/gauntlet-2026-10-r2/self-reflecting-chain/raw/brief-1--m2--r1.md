Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is fine, but the capacity section, which answers your question, doesn't hold up. The risk and track-record sections also don't support the conclusion.

**1. Margin: the arithmetic checks out**
4,200 x £0.41 = £1,722 a day, and x 365 is about £628,500 a year. That matches the memo. Two caveats:
- It is a gross margin at £0.97 a loaf. We don't know whether £0.97 is a fully loaded cost or just ingredients, labour and energy. Any extra shift, overtime, early-morning delivery or van capacity for 11 stores would reduce it.
- It assumes Pellam really takes 4,200 every day. See the risk point below.

**2. Capacity: this is the main error, and it answers your question**
- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day.
- Current demand is about 6,100 a day, so there are about 2,900 loaves of spare capacity.
- Pellam needs 4,200. Total demand would be 10,300, which is about 1,300 loaves a day over capacity.

So Pellam does not "fit comfortably". Taking it on as written means one of three things:
- adding capacity, such as a third shift, more ovens or extended hours, which costs money the margin figure doesn't include;
- shorting someone, which is exactly the letdown of café and restaurant customers you asked about;
- dropping or limiting some of the 74 existing accounts.

Other capacity questions the memo doesn't cover:
- "Around 6,100" is an average. What are the busiest days? Weekends, holidays and December are likely higher.
- The contract is every day of the year. Does the bakery run 365 days now? Where do oven maintenance and breakdowns fit in?
- Are 9,000 loaves a realistic sustained output, or a theoretical maximum?
- The 6 a.m. delivery deadline may collide with when your café and restaurant customers expect their bread.

On your specific question, the memo doesn't show you can take this on without letting existing customers down. On its own numbers, you can't without added capacity.

**3. Risk: the reasoning is circular**
The memo says Pellam is low-risk because it offers a three-year commitment, and that commitment protects us. A willingness to sign doesn't show financial strength. Whether it protects you depends on the terms, which the memo doesn't mention:
- Is there a guaranteed minimum volume, or is 4,200 only an estimate or ceiling?
- What are the termination rights, and can Pellam leave early with notice?
- Is the price fixed for three years, or is there an inflation or ingredient-cost mechanism? A flat £1.38 could erode your margin if flour and energy costs rise.
- What are the payment terms? Supermarkets often pay slowly, which strains cash flow.
- What penalties apply if you miss a delivery?

Also check Pellam's accounts or credit report. Concentration matters too: the contract would be roughly £2.1M a year in revenue (4,200 x £1.38 x 365), and potentially a very large share of your business. If Pellam walks away or squeezes the price at renewal, you may have lost café customers in the meantime.

**4. Track record: two success stories aren't evidence**
Two friendly contacts both said it went well. That is a tiny, hand-picked sample. Bakeries where supermarket contracts went badly are less likely to be talked about, or to still be operating. "I haven't heard of one going badly" shows only that Tomasz hasn't heard of one. Ask Ines and Dmitri specific questions:
- What did it do to their capacity and their existing customers?
- Did the supermarket renegotiate the price?
- How much did they invest to meet demand?

Their circumstances may also differ from yours.

**5. Conclusion: it doesn't follow yet**
"The contract clearly pays for itself" depends on a margin figure that ignores the cost of adding capacity, and on an unproven assumption that Pellam is a safe counterparty. The conclusion may still turn out right, but the memo hasn't shown it.
