Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is fine, but the capacity section is wrong, and that is the exact question you asked Tomasz.

**1. Capacity: the numbers don't fit**

- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day.
- You bake about 6,100 a day now, and Pellam adds 4,200. That makes 10,300 a day.
- You'd be about 1,300 loaves a day over capacity, roughly 14% more than you can bake. It would be worse on your busy days, because 6,100 is an average.
- "Fits comfortably" is therefore incorrect. As things stand, you could only deliver the contract by cutting supply to your 74 café and restaurant accounts, which is what you were trying to avoid.
- Pellam also wants delivery every day of the year by 6 a.m. That leaves little room for an oven breakdown, a staff shortage or a holiday period.

To make it work you would need a third shift, extra ovens or a reduced Pellam volume. Each costs money, and the memo's £0.97 cost per loaf probably doesn't include any of it. Overtime, new equipment and extra staff would all reduce the £0.41 margin.

**2. Margin: the arithmetic is right, but it's the wrong figure**

- 4,200 × £0.41 × 365 is about £628,500, so Tomasz's figure checks out.
- It is a gross margin at your current unit cost. It leaves out the cost of the extra capacity above, the cost of any café customers you lose or fail to serve, and any other new costs such as delivery vehicles and night staff.
- The price is fixed for three years. Flour, energy and wage inflation will eat into £0.41 unless the contract has a price-review clause. The memo doesn't say whether it does.
- If the contract displaces café loaves, you lose their margin too. Cafés presumably pay more than £1.38 a loaf, so each loaf lost could cost you more than each Pellam loaf earns.

**3. Risk: the reasoning doesn't hold**

- A three-year commitment doesn't show that Pellam is financially solid. Plenty of companies sign long contracts and then fail, renegotiate or leave through a break clause.
- The commitment protects you only if the contract actually guarantees minimum volumes, has a meaningful termination penalty, and sets reasonable payment terms. The memo says nothing about any of these. Ask for them, or have a solicitor check them. Pay particular attention to whether Pellam can cut volumes or leave on short notice, because you'd have built capacity for them.
- Concentration is also a risk. This contract would be roughly £2.1m a year of revenue (4,200 × £1.38 × 365) from a single customer. If it ended, you'd be left with extra capacity and cost and possibly damaged café relationships.
- I'd also run a basic credit check on Pellam.

**4. Track record: weak evidence**

- Two friendly contacts who are happy with their decision is a very small sample. Bakeries where supermarket contracts went badly are less likely to be telling that story, so "I haven't heard of one" may just mean you wouldn't have heard.
- Ines and Dmitri may also have had different capacity, contract terms or customers. It's more useful to ask them specific questions. Did they have spare capacity beforehand? Did they have to invest? What happened to their existing customers? Did Pellam-style price pressure or volume cuts follow?

**5. Conclusion: "clearly pays for itself" isn't shown yet**

It may well be a good contract. The unit margin is positive, and the volume is large and steady. But the memo hasn't shown that you can deliver it without letting down the cafés.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Ask Tomasz to redo the capacity calculation with peak-day volumes, and cost the extra shift or ovens needed to close the 1,300-a-day gap.
2. Recalculate the margin per loaf including those costs and the likely inflation over three years.
3. Get the contract terms on minimum volumes, termination, price review, payment terms and penalties for missed deliveries.
4. Check Pellam's finances.
5. Talk to Ines and Dmitri again with the specific questions above.
6. If Friday is too soon, ask Pellam for a short extension, or counter with a lower starting volume (for example 2,500 to 2,800 loaves a day, which fits the spare capacity) with an option to grow as you add capacity.

Assumptions: I took the 9,000 capacity, 6,100 current volume and prices exactly as the memo states them. I haven't seen the contract itself.
