Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is right, but the capacity claim, which is the answer to the question you asked, is wrong. The risk and track-record sections don't support the conclusion either.

**1. Capacity: the memo's central claim fails**

- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves each give 9,000 loaves a day.
- Your current café and restaurant volume is about 6,100 a day, and Pellam adds 4,200. That is 10,300 a day.
- You would be about 1,300 loaves a day over capacity, roughly 14% more than you can bake.

So Pellam does not "fit comfortably within the capacity we already have". If you take the contract on today's ovens and shifts, either you miss Pellam's volume (a breach, on a contract that runs every day of the year) or you cut about 1,300 loaves a day from your existing customers. That is roughly a fifth of their volume, or the equivalent of 15 or so of your 74 accounts at the average order size. That is exactly the "letting down existing customers" outcome you asked Tomasz to rule out.

Other points that make it tighter than the numbers suggest:

- 6,100 is an average ("around"). Your busiest days will be higher, and those are the days you would be shortest.
- Pellam wants delivery by 6 a.m. every day of the year. Check that this works alongside your café and restaurant delivery windows, and what happens on days when an oven is down for maintenance or repair. The memo has no slack for either.
- To close the gap you probably need extra oven capacity, a third shift or outsourced baking. The memo includes none of those costs.

**2. Margin: the arithmetic is right, but it overstates the gain**

£0.41 × 4,200 × 365 is about £628,500 a year, so Tomasz's figure is correct. But it is a gross figure.

- It assumes £0.97 per loaf holds at the new volume. It may not if you add shifts, overtime, ingredients bought at higher volumes or extra drivers and vans for 11 stores.
- It deducts nothing for the capacity fix in section 1, or for penalties if you ever miss a delivery.
- It ignores what you lose if existing customers get squeezed. Café and restaurant accounts probably pay more per loaf than £1.38, so each one you lose costs more than a Pellam loaf earns. Check your own margin per loaf on those accounts.
- Nothing in the memo says what the contract allows Pellam to do on price. Look for price review clauses, volume flexibility and termination rights.

The £628,000 is the best case before costs. The contract isn't shown to "clearly pay for itself".

**3. Risk: the reasoning doesn't hold**

- "They are offering a three-year commitment, which only a financially solid grocer would do" is not evidence of solvency. Large buyers routinely offer long terms because they have the leverage, and a term gives you no protection if the contract lets them cut volumes, reset prices or terminate with notice. Read what is actually committed: a fixed 4,200 loaves, or "up to" that amount?
- The commitment also binds you. You carry the three-year obligation to deliver every day, and the penalties if you don't.
- Concentration. Pellam would be about 41% of your output (4,200 of 10,300 loaves) and a very large share of revenue (about £2.1m a year at £1.38). If they leave or renegotiate, you will have built capacity and costs around them. Their credit position needs checking directly with accounts, credit reports and payment terms, and the memo doesn't do that.

**4. Track record: two friendly data points aren't a track record**

- Two bakers who chose a supermarket contract and are happy with it are a small, self-selected sample. Bakeries where it went badly are less likely to be speaking up or to be in Tomasz's network. "I haven't heard of one" isn't evidence that none exist.
- Neither of them is necessarily comparable to you in size, capacity headroom or customer mix. Ask them what share of output the contract was, whether they had spare capacity beforehand, what it cost them to scale up and what happened to their independent customers.

**5. Conclusion: not yet supported**

The contract may still be worth taking, but the memo hasn't shown it. Before Friday I would:

1. Work out the cost of closing the 1,300-a-day gap (extra shift or oven, staffing, delivery) and take it off the £628,000.
2. Read the contract for volume commitment, price review, termination, penalties and payment terms, and get a basic credit check on Pellam.
3. Compare the net margin with what you'd earn from the café and restaurant volume you'd protect.
4. Ask Ines and Dmitri the harder questions above.
5. If you need more time, ask Pellam for an extension, or counter with a smaller starting volume (for example 2,500 to 3,000 loaves a day) with scale-up rights as you add capacity.

I wouldn't sign until the capacity gap has a funded plan.
