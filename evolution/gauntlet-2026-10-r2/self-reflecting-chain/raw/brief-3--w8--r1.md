The paper does not yet answer the board's question, so I would not send it as written. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper's conclusion is that Calder Red is the lowest-yielding variety. Yield per hectare is not the same as earnings per hectare, and the paper never compares earnings.

**What the paper can fairly say**

Calder Red yielded 4.1 t/ha against 6.3 for Brannock, about a third less grain. The £38/t premium narrows that gap but cannot close it alone. If both varieties sell at the same base price P, they earn the same per hectare when 4.1 × (P + 38) = 6.3 × P. That gives P of roughly £71/t. Milling grain sells well above that, so on these figures alone Brannock probably earns more per hectare. That would be a real argument for the proposal, and the paper doesn't make it. It only holds if three things are true, and none is stated:

- The base prices really are the same.
- Seed, drying and other costs per hectare are comparable.
- The 19 growers could get Brannock's average yield on their own land, rather than the 6.3 t/ha average across all members.

So the proposal may well be right. It needs a short margin-per-hectare comparison for the two varieties, using last season's actual prices and costs, with the 19 growers' own Brannock yields where they have them. The treasurer or Pieter should be able to produce that in a day or two, before the vote on the 18th.

**Problems that change what the paper can claim**

1. **"Weak demand" is argued in a circle.** The paper says weak demand is shown by Calder Red staying niche, and that niche is what weak demand produces. That is the claim restated, not evidence for it. The only demand evidence given is that mills pay a £38/t premium, which points the other way. Calling it "scarcity" is an assertion. Either show weak demand directly (unsold stock, falling orders, mills dropping it) or drop the claim. If a mill premium exists because supply is small, stopping distribution will also end that premium for the members who currently get it.

2. **The 2022 storm stands in for the risk.** One flattened crop at two farms four years ago does not show that the variety is risky. Whether Calder Red lodges more than Brannock depends on how often it happens and whether Brannock fields beside them survived. The paper gives neither. Say what share of Calder Red area has been written off, in how many seasons, and what happened to Brannock in the same storm. Otherwise treat it as an anecdote.

3. **The share figure doesn't add up.** 610 of 2,400 hectares is 25%, not 19%. The 19 looks like the number of growing members. It is not a share of hectares. The earlier 31% is probably from the same source, so check it. Re-derive both figures. The drift away from the variety may be real, but the size of it is unverified. Also, a falling share doesn't show that members want to leave. It could follow your own seed supply, pricing or agronomy advice. Ask the growers.

4. **The conclusion answers an easier question.** "Lowest yield" is also only shown against Brannock. The paper gives no figures for your other varieties, so "lowest-yielding" is not established either.

**Not yet known, and the cheapest way to find out**

- **Net margin per hectare for each variety.** Last season's accounts, or a call to the mills for the price paid.
- **What the 19 growers would do instead.** Brannock may not suit their land or rotation, and they could buy Calder Red seed elsewhere. A five-minute call to a few of them would settle both. If they just switch to Brannock, the aggregate earnings effect is the margin difference times 610 hectares, which is what the board needs in a single figure.
- **Mill contracts.** Whether any mill buys Calder Red on terms that depend on the cooperative supplying it.

Suggested wording for the conclusion once those are in: "Stopping Calder Red is expected to raise (or lower) member earnings by about £X per hectare, roughly £Y across the 19 growers, provided they switch to Brannock at similar yields." If the margin comparison favours Calder Red despite its lower yield, the recommendation should reverse.
