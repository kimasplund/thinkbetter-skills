I would not send the paper to the board as it stands. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper's conclusion answers a different question, which is whether Calder Red has the lowest yield per hectare. Several of the supporting arguments also have problems.

**1. The conclusion doesn't answer the question.**
Yield per hectare is not income per hectare. The paper itself says mills pay a £38/t premium for Calder Red, and it then waves that premium away. A rough comparison per hectare, using the paper's yields:

- Calder Red: 4.1 t x (base price + £38)
- Brannock: 6.3 t x base price

The two break even at a base price of about £71/t (2.2 x P = 4.1 x 38 = £155.8). Grain usually sells well above that, so Brannock probably does earn more per hectare before costs. But the paper never states a price, so the board can't see this. It also leaves out costs (seed, drying, inputs), harvest timing, rotation fit, and whether the Brannock price holds if 19 members' worth of land moves into it. The paper needs a gross margin per hectare for each variety, plus the same figure for any other variety the co-op sells.

The claim that it is "our lowest-yielding variety" is also not supported. Only Brannock is compared with it, and no other variety's yield is given.

**2. The demand argument is circular.**
"It has stayed a niche grain, which is exactly what you would expect of a grain with weak demand" uses the conclusion as its evidence. A grain can be niche because supply is small, because few members grow it, or because it suits particular soils. The only direct demand evidence in the paper points the other way. Mills pay a premium for it. Calling that "scarcity rather than real demand" doesn't hold up, because a premium is what buyers pay when they want something. If you believe demand is weak, show it with unsold stock, falling premiums, buyer numbers or contract volumes.

**3. The August 2022 storm is an anecdote.**
Two farms losing fields in one storm shows that lodging can happen. It doesn't show how risky the variety is. The paper needs to say how Brannock fields at those or nearby farms fared in the same storm, and how often Calder Red has failed over several seasons. Without that comparison it is one event. Also, "everyone here remembers" is a point about memory, not evidence, and it may sway the board more than it should.

**4. The 19% figure looks wrong, or at least inconsistent.**
610 ha out of 2,400 ha is 25.4%, not 19%. The 19 in the paper is the number of growers (19 of 46 members, about 41%). It looks as though the member count has been mixed up with the hectare share. Pieter should check which figure he meant. If the share is really 25%, the fall from 31% is smaller than the paper implies, though still downward. The claim that "members are already moving away" also needs a reason. Members may be shifting for price, for agronomy or for lack of seed supply, and those lead to different decisions. Year-by-year figures would help.

**5. The yield arithmetic is fine.**
610 ha x 4.1 t/ha is about 2,501 t, which matches the "about 2,500 tonnes".

**What I would ask Pieter to add before the 18th**

- Base price for Brannock and for Calder Red, and the margin per hectare for each after costs.
- A members-level view. Would the 19 growers earn less if they switched? Do they have soils or rotations that suit Calder Red?
- Evidence on demand (premium trend, buyer contracts, unsold volume).
- A like-for-like risk comparison, including Brannock in the 2022 storm.
- The corrected hectare-share figure and its yearly trend.
- What happens to the 2,500 t and the premium income if distribution stops. Could members still buy the seed elsewhere?

**Bottom line for you as chair**
The recommendation may well turn out right, because 6.3 against 4.1 t/ha is a big gap. But the paper hasn't shown it, and its own £38 premium is the one number that could reverse it. I would send it back for a revision that answers the board's question directly, and compare income per hectare, not yield per hectare. If the vote can't move, I would at least tell the board these gaps exist.

I only had the paper itself. I haven't seen price, cost or other-variety data, so the £71/t break-even is an illustration based on the paper's figures, not a finding.
