Short version: the paper does not yet answer the board's question, and I would not send it as it stands. The board asked whether members will earn less next year. The paper argues that Calder Red yields less, which is a different question. Its conclusion may well turn out to be right, but nothing in the paper shows it. One figure in it is also wrong.

**What checks out**

- 610 ha at 4.1 t/ha is about 2,500 t (2,501 t exactly), so the tonnage is correct.
- 4.1 against 6.3 t/ha is a gap of 2.2 t/ha, or about 35% less than Brannock, so the yield claim is fair as far as it goes.

**What does not hold up**

1. **The hectare share is wrong or mislabelled.** 610 of 2,400 ha is 25.4%, not 19%. The 19 is the number of growers; 19 of 46 members is 41%. It looks as though a member count has been used as a land share. If the current share is really 25%, the "fall from 31%" is a drop of about 6 points, not 12. The trend could still be real, but the paper has to restate it with the right number, and its "already moving away" wording is stronger than the evidence supports.

2. **Yield is not earnings.** The question was about member income, and the paper never gives a price per tonne or any costs. This is the central gap, and it can be closed with the paper's own numbers:
   - At the £38/t premium, Calder Red earns about £156 per hectare more than the same grain would at Brannock's base price (4.1 × 38). Across 610 ha that is roughly £95,000 a year.
   - Brannock produces 2.2 t/ha more. Calder Red's premium only makes up for that if Brannock sells for less than about £71/t (£156 ÷ 2.2). Above that price, Brannock earns more per hectare before costs.
   - Mill prices for milling-type grain are normally well above £71/t. So a switch probably does raise gross revenue, which would support Pieter's conclusion. But the paper has to show this with the actual Brannock price, and with seed, drying and harvest costs for both varieties. Without that, the board is being asked to vote on an assumption.

3. **The demand argument is circular.** "It has stayed a niche grain, which is exactly what you would expect of a grain with weak demand" assumes the thing it is trying to show. Staying niche is equally what you would expect from a limited supply, a specialist market, or a variety few growers were offered. Weak demand would show up as buyers refusing to buy, falling prices, or unsold stock. The paper gives none of these.

4. **The premium is dismissed, but it works against the paper.** A premium "from scarcity rather than real demand" is still money members actually receive. Mills paying £38 a tonne more is evidence that at least some buyers want this grain. Whether the premium will last if more land goes back to Calder Red is a fair question, but the paper has not raised it.

5. **The 2022 storm is one anecdote.** Two farms losing fields in one storm does not show the variety is risky. The paper needs to say how Brannock fared in the same storm, whether the cause was lodging (flattening) that is specific to Calder Red, and how the losses compare with the premium and yield figures across several seasons. Without a comparison it is a memorable story, not evidence. Insurance or compensation received should also be netted off.

6. **"Lowest-yielding variety" is not supported.** The paper compares Calder Red only with Brannock. If other varieties are in the catalogue, their yields should be shown. Even if Calder Red is last, a low yield does not justify dropping a variety when the price premium or the fit with certain land may offset it.

7. **Possible selection effect.** The 19 growers may have chosen Calder Red because they farm lighter or more exposed land. If so, Brannock would not necessarily reach 6.3 t/ha on their fields. The fair test is a comparison on the same farms, or the same fields, in the same year.

**What the board still needs before voting**

- Brannock's base price, and the net margin per hectare for each variety after costs.
- For the 19 growers, the income change if they switch to Brannock. Ideally this is shown per farm, because the loss may fall hard on a few. The vote affects 41% of the membership, so the paper should say who gains and who loses.
- Multi-year yields and weather losses for both varieties, not just last season and the 2022 storm.
- A correct land-share figure and trend.
- Whether any growers have contracts with mills, or markets that would be lost, since the premium suggests some buyers rely on this grain.
- Options short of a full stop, such as keeping Calder Red for growers who want it, or running it only on suitable land.

**My suggested recommendation**

Ask Pieter to revise before the 18th. The revision should fix the 19% figure, replace the demand and storm arguments with figures, and add a simple table of margin per hectare for each variety. The numbers above suggest the answer may be that most members would earn about the same or more on Brannock, in which case the case is easy to make and will stand up to questions at the meeting. If the Brannock price turns out to be low, or costs differ, the answer could reverse. The board should not decide until that table exists.
