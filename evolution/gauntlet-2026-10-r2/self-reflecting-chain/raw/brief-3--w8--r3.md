The paper should not go to the board as written. Its conclusion may well be right, but it answers an easier question than the one the board asked, and two of its four supporting points do not hold up. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if Calder Red is dropped. Yield per hectare does not answer that.

**What the paper can honestly say, and a version you could send**

Last season 19 of our 46 members grew Calder Red on 610 of the 2,400 member hectares (about 25%). It averaged 4.1 t/ha against 6.3 t/ha for Brannock, giving about 2,500 t. Mills pay a £38/t premium for Calder Red. Per hectare, that premium does not close the yield gap unless the base grain price is below roughly £71/t (at base price P, Calder Red earns 4.1 x (P + 38) and Brannock 6.3 x P, and these are equal at P of about £71). At any base price near current market levels, a member who moves a hectare from Calder Red to Brannock grosses more, before costs. The recommendation to stop distributing therefore rests on the following, which need confirming before the vote: the actual base price, per-hectare growing costs for each variety, and whether the Calder Red land can grow Brannock as well. Calder Red's share of member hectares has fallen steadily over five years, which suggests members are already choosing to move away.

**Issues that changed the conclusion**

- **The conclusion answers a different question.** "Lowest-yielding variety per hectare" says nothing about income. The £38 premium is the missing half. Fix: compare gross margin per hectare, as above. Also, the paper compares Calder Red only with Brannock, so "lowest-yielding of all our varieties" is not shown. Either give the other varieties' yields or say "lower-yielding than Brannock".
- **The weak-demand argument is circular.** "It has stayed niche, which is what you would expect of weak demand" uses the conclusion as its own evidence. Being niche is equally consistent with limited supply or limited acreage. Drop it.
- **The premium is evidence against weak demand, not for it.** Mills paying £38 a tonne over the alternative is what strong demand for a scarce grain looks like. If the real claim is that demand is thin, the paper needs buyer volumes, contract renewals or how many mills would buy more.
- **The share figure does not match the other numbers.** 610 of 2,400 hectares is 25%, not 19%. The 19 looks like the count of growers (which is 41% of 46 members). Check which was meant and what the starting 31% was measured on, and correct whichever is wrong.
- **The 2022 storm is one vivid event standing in for a risk rate.** Two farms lost fields once, in one storm. The paper does not say whether Brannock fields nearby also flattened, how many Calder Red seasons have been normal, or whether 4.1 t/ha already includes bad years. Without that, "risky" is a guess. Fix: give the loss frequency for each variety over the same years, or present it as a possible concern rather than a finding.
- **The falling share shows a trend but not a loss.** Members drifting away may be rational or may be habit. It also cuts the other way: if the 19 growers are still choosing it despite the drift, some may be earning well from it.

**Still unresolved, with the cheapest check**

- Net effect on members' income next year. Ask Pieter Vos for base price, seed and growing costs per hectare for both varieties, and run the margin per hectare for the 19 growers.
- Whether Brannock can absorb 610 more hectares without depressing its own price, or whether some growers would simply have no alternative crop. Ask two or three mills and the larger Calder Red growers.
- Any existing sales contracts or agreed premiums for next season that would be lost. A quick call to the mills settles this.

If the margin figures come out as the numbers above suggest, the paper's recommendation likely stands, but it should rest on income per hectare and not on yield alone. The point most worth watching is the one the paper skips: the premium.
