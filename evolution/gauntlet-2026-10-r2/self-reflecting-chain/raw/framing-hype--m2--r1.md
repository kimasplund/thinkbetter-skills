Short answer: I wouldn't sign on Friday on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is fine, but the memo doesn't answer the question you asked Tomasz, and its capacity section is wrong in a way that matters.

**1. Capacity: the numbers don't fit**
- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day.
- You already bake about 6,100 a day, and Pellam adds 4,200. That comes to 10,300 a day, which is about 1,300 loaves a day over capacity.
- Pellam on its own would take 93% of one shift's worth of output, on top of a business that already uses about two thirds of total capacity. It doesn't "fit comfortably".
- So the answer to your question, whether you can take this on without letting down your café and restaurant customers, is not yet. As things stand, either Pellam or your 74 accounts would be short, unless you add capacity.
- Adding capacity means a third shift, more ovens, or overtime. All of these raise the £0.97 unit cost, and the memo doesn't account for that.
- The contract is also for every day of the year. That leaves no room for oven maintenance, breakdowns, staff sickness or a peak day for your existing customers. Your 6,100 a day is an average, so some days will be higher.
- A 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores may also clash with your current café and restaurant delivery runs.

**2. The £628,000 is gross, not profit**
- The arithmetic checks out: 4,200 x £0.41 x 365 is about £628,500.
- But it assumes £0.97 holds for these extra loaves. It probably won't if you have to add shifts, equipment, vehicles or staff. Check whether £0.97 is a full cost or only a marginal one, and rebuild it for the capacity you'd actually need.
- Ask what happens to the margin if Pellam later asks for a price cut, or if flour and energy costs rise. Does the contract allow price indexation?
- A £0.41 margin on £1.38 is about 30%. Supermarkets often push back hard on price at renewal and in the middle of a term.

**3. Risk: the reasoning runs the wrong way**
- A three-year commitment doesn't show that Pellam is financially solid. A contract is only as good as its terms, and the memo doesn't say what they are.
- The things to check are:
  - Is the 4,200 a day a guaranteed minimum, or only a forecast?
  - Can Pellam end the contract early, or reduce volumes?
  - What penalties apply to you if you miss a delivery or fall short on volume?
  - What are the payment terms? Large grocers often pay in 60 to 90 days, which affects your cash flow.
  - What happens to your unsold or rejected loaves?
- Pellam would become a very large share of your volume (roughly 40% of what you bake now). If they leave or cut volumes, you'd be left with spare capacity and cost you can't shed, and a lot of leverage would sit with them.
- Their financial position should be checked directly, with credit reports and filed accounts, not inferred from the length of the offer.

**4. Track record: two positive references aren't evidence**
- Two bakeries that chose to talk to you, both of which stayed in business, are a small and biased sample. Bakeries where it went badly are less likely to be known to you or to want to talk.
- "I haven't heard of one going badly" is not the same as "none have". Also, those two were supermarket contracts, not necessarily with Pellam.
- Better questions for Ines and Dmitri: What happened to their existing customers? Did they add capacity, and what did it cost? How did the price hold up over time? What share of their volume is the supermarket now? Would they do it again on the same terms?
- Ideally, also speak to someone who already supplies Pellam.

**5. What I'd do before Friday**
1. Ask Pellam for the draft contract and for a short extension if you need more time to answer. A serious buyer should accept that.
2. Work out the capacity plan: what you would add, what it costs, how long it takes, and what the unit cost becomes.
3. Rebuild the margin at the new cost, including a downside case (volume cut, price pressure, late payment).
4. Consider negotiating a smaller start volume, for example 2,500 to 3,000 a day, a phased ramp-up, or maintenance windows and a force majeure clause.
5. Decide in advance which customers you'd protect if you ran short, and tell your café and restaurant accounts nothing until you know you can serve them.
6. Have a solicitor look at exclusivity, termination, penalties and payment terms.

**Bottom line**
This could still be a very good deal. The margin per loaf is healthy and the volume is attractive. But the memo's conclusion that the contract "clearly pays for itself" and fits within current capacity isn't supported. On the numbers given, you're about 1,300 loaves a day short, and that is exactly the risk to your existing customers that you asked about. I'd hold off signing until the capacity plan and contract terms are in hand.

Assumptions: I took the figures in the memo at face value (4,500 per shift, 6,100 current daily output, £0.97 cost) and haven't seen the actual contract.
