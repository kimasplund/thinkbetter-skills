Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin figure is right, but the capacity claim, which is the one you asked about, is wrong. The rest of the reasoning is thin too.

**1. The margin arithmetic holds**

4,200 loaves x £0.41 = £1,722 a day, or about £628,500 a year over 365 days. That matches the memo. Two caveats:
- It is a gross figure. The £0.97 is presumably your current average cost per loaf, not the cost of the extra loaves.
- It assumes you bake and deliver every day of the year, with no allowance for holidays, breakdowns or maintenance shutdowns.

**2. The capacity claim fails**

Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day. Your café and restaurant accounts already take about 6,100, and Pellam adds 4,200:

- 6,100 + 4,200 = 10,300 loaves a day
- Capacity is 9,000
- Shortfall is about 1,300 loaves a day, roughly 14% over your maximum

So Pellam does not fit comfortably within existing capacity. It doesn't fit at all on current equipment. Even without Pellam you run at about 68% of capacity, and 9,000 is a theoretical ceiling, not a day you can run at every day for three years. Only about 2,900 loaves a day of headroom is real, and in practice less.

This is the answer to your question. If you sign as is, one of three things happens:
- You cut supply to existing customers by about 1,300 loaves a day, roughly a fifth of their volume. That is exactly the letdown you asked about.
- You add capacity, such as a third shift, overtime or more oven time. That costs money the memo doesn't count, and it may not be possible with 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores while your café deliveries also need to go out.
- You miss Pellam's volumes, which is a breach on a three-year contract.

**3. "Low-risk customer" doesn't follow from the contract**

A three-year term shows Pellam wants a stable supplier. It doesn't show they are financially solid, and it isn't protection for you:
- The commitment binds you to supply every day at £1.38 for three years, with no obvious room for your costs to rise (flour, energy, wages).
- Check what the contract actually says on volume guarantees, termination rights, price reviews, penalties for short or late delivery, and payment terms. Big retailers often pay late and can reject deliveries.
- Concentration: Pellam would be about 41% of your volume (4,200 of 10,300) from one buyer. If they leave or renegotiate, you have a large hole and may have lost café accounts along the way.

**4. The track record is not evidence**

Two friendly bakeries that did well, plus "I haven't heard of one going badly", is a very small and self-selected sample. Bakeries where it went badly are less likely to be at the dinner table or to get a call. It also isn't clear whether Corrin and Northfold had spare capacity when they signed, which is the thing that matters. Ask them directly: what did they add in equipment or staff, and what happened to their existing customers? Better still, look for a bakery that exited a supermarket contract and ask why.

**5. The conclusion doesn't follow**

"£628,000 a year clearly pays for itself" only works if the extra loaves cost £0.97 each and nothing else changes. Once you're 1,300 loaves short, the question is whether £628,000 exceeds:
- the cost of added capacity (shift premiums, equipment, extra staff, delivery runs), and
- any margin lost from café and restaurant customers you short-change or lose.

That comparison isn't in the memo, so "we should sign" isn't supported yet.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Ask Pellam whether a smaller starting volume is possible. Roughly 2,000 to 2,500 loaves a day would fit, with options to scale as you add capacity.
2. If they won't, get a quote for the extra capacity and work out the real marginal cost per loaf at 4,200. Compare the margin on that basis.
3. Read the contract for volume, termination, price review and payment terms, and have a solicitor look at it.
4. Run the per-account numbers for your 74 customers so you know what you would lose if supply to them were cut.
5. If you need more time, ask Pellam for a short extension rather than signing against a deadline.

My view is that the deal may well be worth doing, but not at 4,200 a day on the current ovens, and not on this memo. I'd go back to Tomasz with the 10,300 versus 9,000 figure first.
