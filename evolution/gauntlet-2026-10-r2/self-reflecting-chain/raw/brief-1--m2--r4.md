Short answer: I wouldn't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is right, but the capacity section, which is the one that answers your question, doesn't hold up, and the risk and track-record sections are weaker than they look.

**1. Margin: the arithmetic checks out**
4,200 x £0.41 = £1,722 a day, and x 365 comes to about £628,500 a year. That matches the memo. The caveat is that £0.97 looks like your average cost per loaf. Whether it holds for these extra loaves depends on the points below, such as overtime, a extra shift, or extra delivery runs.

**2. Capacity: this is the main error**
- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves each gives 9,000 loaves a day.
- You bake about 6,100 for your existing accounts, which leaves about 2,900 spare.
- Pellam needs 4,200. Together that is 10,300 against 9,000, so you'd be about 1,300 loaves a day short (roughly 14% over capacity), even before any bad day.

So Pellam does not fit comfortably in your current capacity. Taking it on would mean one of these:
- Adding capacity (a third shift, longer shifts, another oven or site). That costs money and probably raises the £0.97 unit cost, so the £628k is overstated.
- Cutting back your café and restaurant accounts. That is exactly what you asked Tomasz to rule out.

Other points on capacity:
- "Every day of the year" leaves no room for oven breakdowns, maintenance, holidays or sickness. Do your current 6,100 loaves also run seven days a week, or is that an average? If cafés peak on some days, those days could be well above 6,100.
- A 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores is a logistics commitment, and it may compete with your existing early-morning deliveries.

**3. Risk: the reasoning is backwards**
- A three-year term is not evidence that Pellam is financially solid, and it doesn't protect you. Check what the contract actually binds Pellam to. Many supermarket contracts set volumes as forecasts, allow termination on notice, or let the buyer change specs and prices. Please check for minimum volumes, termination rights, payment terms and price-review clauses.
- If you build capacity for Pellam and they walk away or cut volumes, you carry the cost of that extra capacity.
- Concentration: Pellam would be about 41% of your output (4,200 of 10,300) and probably your largest single customer by far. Losing it, or having it renegotiate you down, would be a serious problem. At that size they also have strong negotiating leverage at renewal.
- Look at their credit directly (filed accounts, credit report, payment history with other suppliers). Don't infer it from the contract length.

**4. Track record: weak evidence**
- Two friendly conversations are a tiny sample. Both people chose to take supermarket contracts, and they're the ones who stayed in business and are willing to talk. Bakeries where it went badly may have closed or may not be taking calls. "I haven't heard of one going badly" is mostly a statement about who Tomasz talks to.
- It's also worth asking Ines and Dmitri better questions. Did they have to add capacity? What happened to their existing customers? What happened to margin after year one? Did the supermarket ever push for price cuts?

**5. Conclusion: not yet supported**
"£628,000 a year clearly pays for itself" assumes the capacity is free and the contract is secure. Neither is established. The real question is net margin after the cost of the extra capacity, versus the risk to your 74 existing accounts.

**What I'd do before Friday**
1. Cost out the capacity gap of about 1,300 loaves a day (third shift, equipment, staff) and recompute the margin on that basis.
2. Have a solicitor read the contract for volume commitments, termination, penalties for missed deliveries, and price changes.
3. Check Pellam's finances.
4. Ask Pellam whether they'd accept a smaller volume (for example about 2,500 to 2,900 loaves, which fits your spare capacity), a phased ramp-up, or a shorter initial term, and ask for the reply deadline to be extended if you need it.
5. Talk to a couple more bakeries, including one that had a bad experience.

If the extra capacity can be added at a reasonable cost and the contract has real volume and termination protections, this could still be a good deal. But as written, the memo doesn't show that you can serve Pellam without letting down your existing customers. On the numbers it says you can't, at least not without investment.

I've assumed that 6,100 is your daily average and that the 4,500 per shift figure is a firm maximum. If either is different, the gap changes, but the check is quick to redo.
