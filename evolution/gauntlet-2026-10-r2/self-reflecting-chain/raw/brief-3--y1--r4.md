The paper is not ready to go to the board as written. Its conclusion may still turn out to be right, but the argument doesn't get there, and it never answers the question the board asked.

**The question the board asked**

The board asked whether members will earn less next year. The paper concludes that Calder Red is "our lowest-yielding variety per hectare". Yield is not income, so that doesn't answer the question. Only Brannock is compared (4.1 against 6.3 t/ha), so "lowest-yielding of all our varieties" isn't shown either. The premium of £38 a tonne is the other half of the income picture, and the paper dismisses it.

**What the numbers say about income**

Your figures do let us run the comparison. The paper doesn't give a grain price, so I've worked it out as a break-even:

- Calder Red earns 4.1 × (P + 38) per hectare, where P is the Brannock price per tonne.
- Brannock earns 6.3 × P per hectare.
- The two are equal at P of about £71 a tonne.

At any Brannock price above about £71, Brannock earns more per hectare before costs. At an illustrative £200 a tonne, Calder Red earns about £976 per hectare against £1,260 for Brannock, a gap of roughly £285 per hectare. That would support the proposal's direction, but only if three things hold, and the paper checks none of them:

1. Seed, input and drying costs per hectare are no higher for Brannock.
2. The Calder Red fields can grow Brannock. If some of those 610 hectares suit Calder Red better because of soil, drainage or rotation, the move is less simple.
3. The mills will take the extra Brannock at current prices. Moving 610 hectares into your main variety adds about 3,800 tonnes of Brannock (610 × 6.3), which could depress its price.

Please ask Pieter for the actual prices, costs and the 19 members' per-hectare margins. That table would be the core of an honest answer.

**Paragraph by paragraph**

- **"Weak demand".** The reasoning is circular. It is a niche grain, and "that is exactly what you'd expect of weak demand", but a niche grain is also what you'd expect from limited supply, from a specialist market or from a variety few members chose to grow. A niche is not evidence of weak demand. The premium points the other way: mills pay £38 a tonne extra, and that is what buyers do when they want something. Calling it "scarcity rather than real demand" doesn't help. A price premium arises when buyers want more than is available, and that is demand. The paper needs real evidence, such as unsold stock, mill order data or whether the premium is falling.
- **August 2022 storm.** This is one event on two farms, out of 19 growers. It shows that the crop can fail in a storm. It doesn't show Calder Red is riskier than Brannock unless we know how Brannock fared nearby in that storm. Other seasons would also help. Without them, it is an anecdote. Also, "written off" losses may have been insured or compensated, and the paper doesn't say.
- **Falling share.** The numbers contradict themselves. 610 of 2,400 hectares is 25%, not 19%. The 19 in the paper is the number of growing members (19 of 46, or 41%), so the figure may have been mixed up with that. Either the 19% is wrong or the 610 hectares is wrong. Pieter needs to say which. If the share really is 25% now, it has fallen from 31% by six points, not twelve, which is a much weaker trend. Even then, a decline in share can have causes other than members rejecting the variety, such as seed availability, rotation, or the premium not being passed on. It is a hint to investigate, not proof that members are leaving.
- **First paragraph.** The arithmetic holds: 610 ha × 4.1 t/ha is about 2,500 tonnes. The two yields are stated clearly.

**What I would do before the vote on the 18th**

1. Have Pieter correct or explain the 19% against the 610/2,400 figure.
2. Add a per-hectare margin comparison, including the premium and costs, for the 19 growers.
3. Say what happens to the 610 hectares and to Brannock supply and price if Calder Red stops.
4. Replace the circular demand argument with data from the mills, or drop it.
5. Put the storm in context against Brannock's performance, or drop it.
6. Consider telling the board plainly that the 19 members who grow it would face the change most. The paper never mentions them, and the board's question was about member earnings.

If the margin comparison comes out as the rough sums above suggest, the proposal may well stand, and it would be on a much stronger footing than the current paper offers.
