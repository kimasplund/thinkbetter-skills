I would not send this paper to the board as written. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper doesn't answer that. Its one firm fact is true but doesn't settle the question, and each supporting argument has a problem.

**What holds up**

The arithmetic is right. 610 ha at 4.1 t/ha is about 2,500 t. The yield gap is also real: 4.1 t/ha against 6.3 t/ha for Brannock.

**Where the argument breaks**

1. **The conclusion answers a different question.** "Lowest-yielding per hectare" is not "lowest-earning per hectare". The paper itself says mills pay £38 a tonne more for Calder Red, and it never sets that premium against the yield gap. You can check this with the paper's own numbers. Let P be the Brannock price per tonne.
   - Calder Red earns 4.1 × (P + 38) per hectare. Brannock earns 6.3 × P.
   - Calder Red comes out ahead only if P is below roughly £71 a tonne (4.1 × 38 = 156, divided by the 2.2 t/ha gap).
   - At any realistic milling-grain price, Brannock probably earns more per hectare before costs. So the paper's conclusion may well be right, but the paper doesn't show it. It needs the actual prices and each variety's growing costs, per hectare.

2. **The demand argument is circular.** "It has stayed a niche grain, which is exactly what you would expect of weak demand" treats the conclusion as the evidence. A niche grain is equally what you would expect of one that is hard to grow, or one that few farms were offered. The premium points the other way. Mills paying £38 more means buyers want it at least as much as the supply allows. Saying the premium "reflects scarcity rather than real demand" doesn't help, because scarcity only pushes price up when someone wants the grain. The real question is how much more Calder Red the mills would take, and at what price. Pieter should ask the mills directly. If volume demand is thin, a small premium on a small tonnage is still a small prize.

3. **The 2022 storm is one anecdote, with no comparison.** Two farms lost crops in one storm. The paper doesn't say whether neighbouring Brannock fields stood, or whether lodging is a known weakness of Calder Red. It also doesn't say whether insurance covered the loss. One event on two farms out of 19 growers is not evidence about the variety. If Calder Red lodges more than Brannock, show the multi-year record for both.

4. **The 19% figure doesn't fit.** 610 of 2,400 hectares is about 25%, not 19%. The 19 is the number of growing members. It looks like the number may have been carried over by mistake, though 19 of 46 members would be 41%, which doesn't match either. Please ask Pieter which figure is correct and what the five-year series is based on. The fall from 31% to roughly 25% is still a decline, but it is a smaller one than the paper shows. A decline in area also doesn't show why members are moving away. It could be the yield gap, but it could equally be rotation, seed supply or price.

**What the board is not being told**

- What happens to the 19 growers' income. Their 610 ha would move to Brannock or something else. Can that land grow Brannock as well as it does elsewhere?
- Whether Brannock prices would soften if members plant more of it.
- Whether the premium is guaranteed by contract, or only paid by the mills as a spot price.
- Whether any members would still want Calder Red, and could source it elsewhere.

**What I would ask Pieter for before the 18th**

1. Gross margin per hectare for Calder Red and Brannock (price including the £38 premium, less seed, inputs and drying), ideally over several seasons.
2. The corrected area share and the source of the five-year trend.
3. A comparison of storm and lodging losses between the two varieties across several years.
4. A short statement from two or three mills on how much Calder Red they would buy.
5. An estimate of the net change in total member income if the 610 ha switched to Brannock, with a range, not a single number.

**Bottom line**

The recommendation may turn out to be right, since the yield gap is large. But as written, the paper rests on a circular argument, a single anecdote and a figure that doesn't add up, and it never compares earnings. I would send it back for the margin comparison before the vote.
