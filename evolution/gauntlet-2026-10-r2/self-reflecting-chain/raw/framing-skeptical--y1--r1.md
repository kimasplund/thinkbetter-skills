Your instinct is right. The memo's arithmetic on the margin is fine, but the capacity section, which is the part that answers your question, is wrong. Don't sign on the strength of this memo.

**Capacity: it does not fit**

The memo says two shifts at 4,500 loaves each, so 9,000 loaves a day at most. You already bake about 6,100 a day. Adding Pellam's 4,200 gives 10,300 a day, which is 1,300 loaves a day more than your ovens can produce. The memo seems to have compared Pellam's 4,200 against a single shift's 4,500, or just against the gap, and concluded it fits. It doesn't.

- Without new capacity, the most you could add is about 2,900 loaves a day, and that leaves no slack at all.
- Pellam wants delivery by 6 a.m., every day. Your café and restaurant accounts presumably also expect early-morning delivery, so both sets of orders compete for the same overnight window. In practice that is where existing customers get let down first.
- 6,100 is an average. If your busiest days run above it (weekends, holidays, events), the shortfall on those days is bigger than 1,300.
- "Every day of the year" leaves no room for an oven breakdown, a maintenance shutdown or a sick-day crunch. Ask what the penalty is if you miss a delivery.

So the honest answer to the question you asked Tomasz is: not with the current setup. Taking this on means a third shift, more ovens, or dropping some existing accounts. Each of those costs money or revenue, and the memo prices none of them.

**The £628,000 is not a profit figure**

The numbers do check out: £0.41 × 4,200 × 365 is about £628,500. But:

- It is only as good as the £0.97 cost. Is that a full cost or just ingredients and direct labour? Extra shifts, overtime, night premiums, extra delivery vehicles, and possibly more oven capacity are not in a £0.97 that was calculated at your current scale.
- The price is fixed at £1.38 for three years. Ask whether it can move with flour, energy and wage costs. If it can't, a few pence of cost inflation eats a large share of a £0.41 margin.
- If any existing accounts were dropped or short-supplied to make room, their margin has to be subtracted.
- Pellam would be roughly 40% of your volume. That is a big concentration on one customer, and supermarkets are known for tough renegotiation.

**Risk: the three-year term does not show what the memo says it does**

"Only a financially solid grocer would offer a three-year commitment" doesn't follow. A contract term is not evidence of solvency. More importantly, a commitment protects you only if it binds Pellam in ways that matter. Check:

- Is there a minimum volume they must take, or is 4,200 a forecast they can reduce?
- Can they terminate early, and on what notice?
- What are their payment terms (30 days or 90)?
- What penalties apply to you for late or short delivery, and do they apply to them if they cut orders?
- Is there an exclusivity or price-matching clause?

If the volume isn't guaranteed, you could invest in extra capacity and then find Pellam ordering less. A credit check on Pellam would be more useful than the inference from the contract length.

**Track record: two friends is not evidence**

Ines and Dmitri are two bakeries that took supermarket contracts and are happy. That's a sample of two, picked by Tomasz because they were reachable. "I haven't heard of a bakery it went badly for" is weak, because the bakeries it went badly for are the ones that closed, were absorbed, or don't talk about it. They are also less likely to be in Tomasz's contacts. It would help to know whether Corrin and Northfold already had spare capacity, what share of their volume it is, and whether their contracts had the same terms. Ask them directly about the first year, the price renegotiations and any missed deliveries.

**Conclusion: it does not follow**

"The contract clearly pays for itself" rests on a capacity claim that is incorrect and a margin figure that ignores the cost of fixing that. The memo may still end up being a good deal, but it hasn't shown it.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Ask Tomasz to redo the capacity numbers with a day-by-day peak, and to cost out what it takes to cover the 1,300-loaf gap (third shift, ovens, staffing).
2. Recalculate the margin with those costs and with realistic cost inflation over three years.
3. Get the contract terms listed above, especially guaranteed volume, termination, price adjustment and payment terms. Have a solicitor read it.
4. Consider counter-offering a smaller or phased volume, for example 2,000 to 2,500 loaves a day at first, with the rest after capacity is in place.
5. If Friday is too soon to do this properly, ask for a short extension. A serious buyer will usually allow it. If they won't, that tells you something about how they will treat you for three years.

If the numbers still work after that, it can be a good contract. Right now the memo doesn't show it.
