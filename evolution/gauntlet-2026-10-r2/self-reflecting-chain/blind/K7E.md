Don't sign yet. As written, the memo doesn't show that you can take Pellam on without letting your café and restaurant customers down. On the numbers in it, you can't, at least not with your current ovens. The deal may still be worth doing once it's fixed, and the margin figure itself is sound.

**What the memo should say**

- **Capacity:** Two shifts at 4,500 loaves each is 9,000 loaves a day. Your 74 existing accounts already take about 6,100, which leaves about 2,900 spare. Pellam needs 4,200, so you'd be about 1,300 loaves a day short (10,300 needed against 9,000). That is roughly a fifth of what your current customers take. Pellam fits only if you add capacity, such as a third part-shift, extra oven time or a second oven, or if you negotiate a smaller starting volume of about 2,900. The other way to fit it is to drop café accounts, and that is exactly what you wanted to avoid. A "every day of the year" commitment also leaves no slack for oven maintenance, breakdowns or a sick-day crunch.
- **Margin:** The arithmetic holds. 4,200 × £0.41 × 365 is about £628,000. But it's a gross figure that assumes the £0.97 cost holds for the extra loaves. Loaves that need extra shifts, overtime, new equipment, a bigger delivery run to 11 stores by 6 a.m., or more flour and packaging may cost more than £0.97 each. Those costs have to come off before you can say the contract "pays for itself". The figure also doesn't count anything you lose if capacity pressure hurts existing accounts.
- **Risk:** Pellam signing for three years tells you they want a reliable supplier. It doesn't tell you they're financially solid, and the commitment doesn't protect you unless the contract does. The contract also binds you for three years at a fixed £1.38, so check for flour and energy price rises and whether the price can be reviewed. This deal would also be about 40% of your total volume (4,200 of 10,300 loaves), so losing it or renegotiating it would hurt badly.
- **Track record:** Two friendly bakeries that are happy are encouraging, but they're a small, hand-picked sample. Bakeries where a supermarket deal went badly (squeezed margins, lost cafés, forced to close) are much less likely to be on your list, so "I haven't heard of one going badly" proves little. Ask Ines and Dmitri the harder questions: did they have to add capacity, what happened to their independent customers, and what did the contract's volume and price terms look like?

**Where that leaves the recommendation:** Pursue the offer, but don't accept it as it stands. Reply by Friday with interest and the conditions you need. These are a volume or ramp-up that fits your capacity (or a costed plan to add it), protection for your existing accounts, and a price review clause.

**Still to check, and the cheapest way**

1. True cost per loaf at the higher volume, and what extra capacity costs. Ask your oven supplier or an equipment lessor for a quote, then rerun the margin.
2. Pellam's finances and contract terms: guaranteed minimum volumes, termination rights, payment days, penalties for missed 6 a.m. deliveries, price indexation. Read the draft contract, check their filed accounts, and get a short review from a solicitor before signing.
3. Whether your 6,100 is an average or includes peaks. Look at your busiest week of the last year, because the shortfall may be bigger than 1,300.
4. How much you'd lose if you had to reduce café supply, in margin and goodwill. This is your fallback figure for the negotiation.
