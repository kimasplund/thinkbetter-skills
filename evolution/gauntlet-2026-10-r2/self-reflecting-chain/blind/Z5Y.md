Don't sign on the strength of this memo. Its key claim, that Pellam fits within your existing capacity, is wrong on your own numbers, and that was the exact thing you asked about.

**What the memo should say instead**

Pellam is worth pursuing, but not as a fit within current capacity. Two oven shifts at 4,500 loaves give you 9,000 a day. Your café and restaurant accounts take about 6,100, and Pellam adds 4,200, so you would need about 10,300. That is roughly 1,300 loaves a day more than you can bake. Without new capacity, you would either fail Pellam or short your 74 accounts by about a fifth of their current volume (Pellam would leave 4,800 loaves for them against the 6,100 they take now). Letting those customers down is the outcome you wanted to avoid.

Taking the contract therefore means adding capacity: a third shift, extra oven time or another oven. That cost has to come out of the £628,000 before the contract can be said to pay for itself. The figure itself is right as arithmetic (4,200 x £0.41 x 365 is about £628,500). But it is a gross margin, and it only holds if the £0.97 cost already includes the extra labour, overtime, 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores, and any equipment. It also ignores that every loaf you cannot bake for a café is a sale lost, probably at a better price than £1.38.

Two other points need fixing before Friday:

- **Risk.** The three-year term does not show Pellam is financially solid, and the term alone does not protect you. What protects you is a guaranteed minimum volume, a payment term you can live with, and limits on early exit and price changes. Pellam would also be about 40% of your output, so losing or renegotiating it would hurt.
- **Track record.** Two friendly bakeries that are glad they did it are not evidence about the typical outcome. You only hear from the ones who stayed in business and are happy, and "I haven't heard of one going badly" is not the same as none having done so. Ask Ines and Dmitri whether they had to add capacity, whether any customers left, and how their contract terms worked.

**Issues that changed the conclusion**

- Capacity: it needs about 10,300 loaves a day against 9,000, so "fits comfortably" is false. The fix is to price in extra capacity or negotiate a lower volume.
- Margin: £628,000 is before the cost of the extra capacity and any lost café sales. The fix is to recalculate net of both.
- Risk: the length of the contract was treated as proof of solidity. The fix is to check Pellam's accounts and the contract's volume and exit terms.
- Track record: two positive anecdotes were treated as the whole picture. The fix is to ask them the harder questions above.

**Still unresolved, and the cheapest way to check**

- Whether the 6,100 a day is the average or the peak. Check your busiest week; if peaks are well above 6,100, the gap is bigger than 1,300.
- Whether the 6 a.m. delivery for 11 stores fits your current bake and van schedule. Ask your head baker and whoever runs deliveries for one afternoon's plan.
- Whether Pellam will accept a smaller start, for example 2,700 a day (which is within the spare 2,900), or a phased ramp while you add capacity. This costs one phone call before Friday and would let you reply with a yes that doesn't put the café and restaurant accounts at risk.
- Whether "every day of the year" has any give for maintenance and holidays. Your ovens will need servicing, and as written there is no slack.

A reasonable reply by Friday would be to say you are interested, ask for a short extension or a phased volume, and send your questions about minimum volumes, payment terms and exit clauses.
