Short answer: I would not send this to the board as it stands. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper never estimates member income. Its conclusion is also about something else (yield per hectare), and several of its supporting claims fail on inspection.

**What holds up**

- 610 ha x 4.1 t/ha is about 2,501 t, so the "about 2,500 tonnes" is correct.
- The yield gap is real on the paper's own figures: 4.1 against 6.3 t/ha, so Calder Red yields about 35% less per hectare than Brannock.

**What does not hold up**

1. **The conclusion answers a different question.** Yield per hectare is not income per hectare. The paper itself says mills pay a £38/t premium, and it then dismisses that premium. To say whether members earn less, you need price x yield minus costs, per hectare, for Calder Red against what those hectares would grow instead.

   The premium does not close the gap on its own, which may be the paper's best argument, but it needs to be shown. Calder Red and Brannock earn the same revenue per hectare only if Brannock's price per tonne is about £71 or less. That figure comes from 4.1 x (P + 38) = 6.3 x P, where P is Brannock's price. If Brannock sells well above £71/t, which is likely, Calder Red earns less revenue per hectare even with the premium. But the paper gives no Brannock price, no seed, drying or input costs, and no margins. Without them nobody can say by how much. Ask Pieter for a gross margin per hectare for each variety.

2. **The demand argument is circular.** "It has stayed a niche grain, which is exactly what you would expect of a grain with weak demand" assumes the thing it is trying to show. A grain can stay niche because the supply is small, because few growers have contracts, or because of how it is marketed. The only direct evidence of demand in the paper is that mills pay a premium, and that points toward demand being at least adequate. Saying the premium "reflects scarcity rather than real demand" is an assertion. Scarcity premiums last only while supply stays scarce. If you drop Calder Red, that scarcity and any premium contracts are what members lose. The paper needs mill order volumes, contract commitments, or evidence that mills would stop buying.

3. **The 2022 storm is one event, not a measure of risk.** Two farms losing fields in one storm, three years ago, says nothing about how Calder Red compares with Brannock. A fair test would be lodging or loss rates for both varieties across several seasons, on comparable fields. Was Brannock also flattened in 2022? Were the Ashby and Toller fields exposed sites? Was the loss insured? "Everyone remembers it" is not evidence. Also check the phrase "written off". Does it mean total loss of those fields' crops?

4. **The 19% figure looks wrong, or at least muddled.** The paper says Calder Red is on 610 of 2,400 hectares, which is 25.4%, not 19%. The number 19 is the count of members growing it. It is 19 of 46, or 41% of members, and the paper may have mixed the two figures. Either the 31% to 19% trend is in different units or measured on a different base, or it is simply a slip. Until Pieter reconciles it, don't quote the trend. If the correct current share is about 25%, the fall from 31% is smaller than the paper implies.

5. **A falling share does not show members want out.** Even if the trend is right, it could reflect members shifting to Brannock because it is easier to sell, or it could mean the remaining growers are the ones who earn most from Calder Red. 19 members are still choosing it, on an average of about 32 ha each. Ask them why. If they are growing it for the premium, withdrawing seed removes a choice they made deliberately.

6. **"Lowest-yielding variety" is not supported.** The paper compares only with Brannock. Nothing is given for any other variety you distribute. If there are others, show their yields. Even if the claim is true, a lowest-yielding variety can still be the best earner if its price and costs are different.

**What the board still needs**

- Gross margin per hectare for Calder Red and for Brannock (and any other variety members would switch to), using actual prices achieved and actual costs.
- The premium contracts: how many mills, what volumes, and for how long. Would the premium survive a halving of supply? Would it survive an end to your distribution?
- Where the 610 hectares would go. If they move to Brannock, estimate the income change per member. With 19 members affected, a table by member would be more useful than an average, since some members may be hit much harder than others.
- Agronomic factors the paper ignores, such as rotation benefit, harvest timing, and whether Calder Red suits particular soils or fields.
- A corrected statement of the share of hectares and its trend.
- A like-for-like loss record for both varieties.

**Bottom line for the board**

On the figures given, a switch to Brannock plausibly lowers revenue per hectare for Calder Red growers' land less than the headline yield gap suggests. Equally it could raise it, because the price premium is not nothing. The paper does not give the numbers to tell which. The honest answer to "will members earn less?" is currently "unknown, and the paper's reasons for thinking they won't are weak." I'd ask Pieter to revise it with the margin comparison and the corrected figures before the vote on the 18th. If that is not possible in time, consider deferring the vote or phasing the change, for example by keeping distribution for the 19 current growers for one more season.
