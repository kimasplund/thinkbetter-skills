It is a big offer, and the margin figure holds up. But the memo doesn't answer the question you asked Tomasz, and its capacity section is wrong. I'd hold off signing until the points below are fixed.

**What checks out**

4,200 loaves x £0.41 is £1,722 a day, which is about £628,000 over 365 days. That arithmetic is right.

**1. Capacity: Pellam does not fit in what you have now**

- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves each gives you 9,000 loaves a day.
- You already bake about 6,100 a day for your 74 café and restaurant accounts.
- Adding Pellam's 4,200 takes you to about 10,300 a day. That is roughly 1,300 loaves a day more than the ovens can produce, about 14% over.

So the memo's claim that Pellam "fits comfortably within the capacity we already have" is the reverse of what the numbers show. On current capacity, you could only take Pellam by cutting back your existing customers. That is exactly what you asked Tomasz to avoid.

It gets tighter than that:
- 6,100 is an average. Your busiest days will be higher, and the gap will be bigger on those days.
- Pellam is every day of the year, with no allowance for holidays, oven maintenance or breakdowns.
- The 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores may collide with the timing of your current deliveries.

The contract is only workable if you add capacity, such as a third shift, more oven time or another oven. That costs money, and the £0.41 margin doesn't include it.

**2. The £628,000 is not profit you can bank**

- £0.97 is presumably your current average cost per loaf. A third shift (labour premiums, overtime), extra delivery runs and any new equipment could push the cost of these particular loaves well above that. Check what the extra loaves really cost.
- The price is fixed at £1.38 for three years. If flour, energy or wage costs rise, the £0.41 margin shrinks, and a few pence per loaf is a large sum at 1.5 million loaves a year. Does the contract allow for price adjustments?
- If you lose or short any café and restaurant accounts to make room, subtract their margin from the £628,000.

**3. "Low risk" doesn't follow from the three-year term**

A three-year commitment shows Pellam wants a stable supplier. It doesn't show they are financially solid, and it doesn't protect you unless the contract does. Before signing, find out:
- whether Pellam guarantees minimum volumes, or whether 4,200 is only an estimate they can reduce;
- whether they can terminate early or on short notice, and what you recover if they do;
- their payment terms and credit standing (supermarkets often pay slowly, and you would be funding the flour and labour in the meantime);
- penalties if you miss a delivery or fall short, which matter with a 6 a.m. daily deadline.

Concentration is the other risk. Pellam would be about 41% of your total volume (4,200 of 10,300 loaves). If it ended or shrank, you would be left with the extra capacity and its costs.

**4. Two success stories are not a track record**

Ines and Dmitri are two bakers who took supermarket contracts and are happy. That is a small, friendly sample, and "I haven't heard of a bakery it went badly for" mostly reflects who you happen to talk to. Bakeries that were squeezed on price or dropped by a supermarket tend not to advertise it. Ask Ines and Dmitri specific questions. Did they have to add capacity? What happened to their margins in years two and three? Did any of their existing customers suffer?

**5. The conclusion doesn't follow yet**

"The contract clearly pays for itself" rests on an uncosted margin, a capacity claim that is wrong, and a risk claim with nothing behind it. It may still be a good deal, but the memo hasn't shown that.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Ask Tomasz to redo the capacity section using 9,000 against about 10,300 a day, including your peak days.
2. Get a cost for the extra capacity (third shift or equipment) and recalculate the margin per loaf and per year from that.
3. Read the contract for volume guarantees, termination, price adjustment, payment terms and penalties.
4. Decide how you'd protect your existing 74 accounts, and put that in writing.
5. If you need more time, ask Pellam for a short extension. A serious buyer of this size will usually give one.

If the numbers still work with the extra capacity and decent contract terms, this could be a good move. Right now the memo doesn't show it, and signing on it risks letting down the customers you asked about.
