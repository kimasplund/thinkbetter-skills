The paper does not yet answer the board's question, so I would not send it as it stands. The board asked whether members will earn less next year if you stop distributing Calder Red. The paper's conclusion is only that Calder Red has the lowest yield per hectare. Yield is not income, and the paper's own figures point the other way.

**What the paper should say instead**

Take the paper's numbers and compare income per hectare. Calder Red yields 4.1 t/ha and earns £38/t more than the base price. Brannock yields 6.3 t/ha.

- If "premium" means £38 over the Brannock price, call that price P. Calder earns 4.1 × (P + 38) and Brannock earns 6.3 × P.
- Calder comes out ahead only when P is below about £71/t, because 4.1 × 38 = £156/ha is the whole premium and Brannock's extra 2.2 t/ha is worth 2.2 × P.
- Milling grain normally sells well above £71/t, so on these figures Brannock probably earns more per hectare. I don't have the actual prices.
- That is a reason to recommend stopping, but only once the paper shows it with real prices and costs. It also has to say what the 19 growers would plant instead. If Brannock does not suit their fields, the comparison fails.
- The paper should show this as a short table: price, yield, income per hectare and growing costs for each variety, for the 19 growers. Then the board can see directly whether members would earn less. Without it, the conclusion does not follow from the evidence.

**Problems in the paper, and how I would fix each**

- **Market demand.** The paper says demand is weak because Calder Red "has stayed a niche grain", and that a niche grain is what weak demand produces. That is the claim restated, not evidence. Niche status could equally come from limited acreage or from limited seed supply. The £38 premium also points toward demand exceeding supply, and the line that it "reflects scarcity rather than real demand" is asserted without support. Fix: cite actual evidence, such as mill orders, unfilled demand, or price trends over time. Otherwise drop the demand argument and rest the case on the income comparison.
- **The 2022 storm.** Two farms losing fields in one storm is a memorable event, not a measure of how risky the variety is. Two of 19 growers is the only count given. The paper needs to say whether Brannock fields also went down that day, how often Calder Red has been lost in other years, and whether the losses were insured. Without those, treat it as one bad year. If the paper has the loss data, put it in as a yield-risk figure.
- **The falling share.** The paper says Calder Red's share of members' hectares fell from 31% to 19%. Its own figures give 610 of 2,400 ha, which is about 25%. Either the 19% is wrong, or it counts something else (or the 19 growers are being confused with a percentage). Fix: check the number and say what is being measured. The trend also does not show that members are right to leave. They may be moving because of seed availability, a rotation change, or habit. Ask a few of the departing growers why.
- **"Lowest-yielding variety."** The paper compares Calder Red only with Brannock. State the yields of all your varieties, or say "lower than Brannock".
- **The conclusion.** "Lowest yield, so stop" skips the income step. Yield per hectare on its own is not a reason to stop. Replace the conclusion with the income comparison above.

**Still unresolved, and the cheapest way to check**

- Brannock's price, and both varieties' growing costs. Ask the mills for the current price list and have Pieter put 2–3 growers' actual margins on one page.
- Whether the 19 growers can switch to Brannock on their land. Ask Pieter and a few of the growers.
- What the real hectare share is. Re-add the 2022 and last-season hectares from the membership returns.

If the comparison confirms Brannock earns more, the recommendation can stand, and it will be much harder for the board to challenge. If it does not, the board would be voting to cut members' income.
