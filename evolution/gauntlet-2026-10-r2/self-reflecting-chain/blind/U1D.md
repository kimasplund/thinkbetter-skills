Short answer: don't circulate it as it stands. The arithmetic is fine, but the recommendation goes further than the evidence supports, and the part that was set aside is the part most relevant to Kessel Street.

**What holds up**

- 35 minutes to 30 is a fall of 5 minutes, or 14.3%. The 14% figure is correct.
- £410,000 against £450,000 is £40,000 under budget (about 9%). That is correct too.

**What doesn't hold up**

1. **The "12 weeks after" doesn't match the data window.** The lane opened on 3 February and the data stops on 31 March. That is about 8 weeks, not 12. Twelve weeks after opening would run to roughly 28 April. Either the after period is shorter than stated, or the data cut-off is wrong. Mara needs to say which. If it is 8 weeks after against 12 before, the two periods are not like for like.

2. **The comparison has no control and mixes in season.** The before period is mid-November to early February, and the after period is February to March. Weather, daylight, school terms, holidays, roadworks and general traffic all change across that span. Nothing in the analysis separates the lane's effect from those. Comparing route 7 with a similar route that had no lane, or with the same weeks a year earlier, would fix this. Without it, "the lane cut journey times by 14%" is an observed change, not an established effect. Averages alone also hide whether reliability improved, which matters as much to passengers as the mean.

3. **The ridership claim is a causal claim with no support.** The report says the 9% rise "confirms" that faster journeys drew the riders. A rise that coincides with the lane doesn't show the lane caused it. Ridership normally moves with season, fares, service changes and events, and it often lags journey-time changes by months. Over a window of at most 8 weeks, the claim should be softened to "ridership rose 9% over the same period; we can't yet say why".

4. **The evidence on people affected is one-sided.** The 38 bus drivers' comments were counted as evidence. The 61 shopkeeper comments on delivery access were set aside as "anecdotal and not representative". The drivers' comments are just as anecdotal, and drivers have an obvious interest in the lane. Dismissing one group and counting the other is inconsistent, and members will notice. Delivery access is also the main risk on Kessel, which has 14 loading bays used by the shops along it. The shopkeeper comments should be summarised properly, covering what they said, how many were negative, and what the concerns were. They should not be discarded.

5. **"Clearly worked" and "under budget" don't follow from the findings.** Coming in under budget says the project was delivered well. It says nothing about whether the lane was worth doing. Whether it "worked" depends on the journey-time result (point 2), the ridership result (point 3) and the effect on businesses (point 4), and the report has no cost-benefit figure. A single pilot on a single route, at a cost of £410,000, also doesn't say what the benefit per pound was.

6. **Nothing shows Varrow Road is comparable to Kessel Street.** Kessel has one lane in each direction, 14 loading bays, and two routes (22 and 31). The report doesn't say what Varrow Road's width, loading arrangements or traffic volumes were, or how many routes used it. The pilot covered one route (7). If Varrow had no comparable loading demand, its result says little about how a lane on Kessel would affect deliveries or general traffic. A lane taking space from loading bays on a one-lane-each-way street is a different design problem.

**What I'd ask Mara for before Thursday**

- The actual before and after dates and the number of weeks in each, plus the data for the full 12-week after period if it exists.
- A comparison with a control: the same weeks a year earlier, or a similar route without a lane.
- Journey-time variability (for example, the slowest 10% of trips), not just the average.
- A fair summary of the 61 shopkeeper comments, and ideally the same for the driver comments, with the numbers positive and negative.
- A comparison of Varrow Road and Kessel Street: width, loading provision, traffic flow, and how many bays or deliveries the lane would affect.
- Any figure for what the pilot cost per minute saved, or per passenger.

**What members could reasonably be told today**

On a short comparison, route 7 journeys were about 5 minutes (14%) faster after the lane opened, and the pilot cost less than budgeted. The evidence on ridership, the effect on shops, and how well this transfers to Kessel Street is not yet strong enough to support a "clearly worked" conclusion.

I would recommend rewording the recommendation to something like "the pilot shows promising journey-time gains; further work on delivery access and comparability is needed before deciding on Kessel Street", or asking for the decision to be deferred until the points above are answered. It may well turn out that the lane is the right call for Kessel, but the report as written doesn't show that, and the committee would be exposed if shopkeepers on Kessel challenge it.
