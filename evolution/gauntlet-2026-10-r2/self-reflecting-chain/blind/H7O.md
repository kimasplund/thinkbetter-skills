Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The arithmetic on margin is right, but the capacity claim, which is the one you asked about, is wrong. The risk and track-record sections also don't support the conclusion.

**1. Margin: the sum checks out**
4,200 x £0.41 = £1,722 a day, or about £628,500 a year. That matches Tomasz's figure.
Two caveats:
- It is a margin per loaf, not profit. It only counts if £0.97 includes the real extra costs of this contract: night labour, 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores, packaging, and any new equipment.
- It assumes Pellam takes all 4,200 loaves every day. The memo doesn't say whether that volume is guaranteed or just a forecast.

**2. Capacity: this is where the memo fails**
Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day. Your current café and restaurant volume is about 6,100. Adding Pellam's 4,200 makes 10,300.
- That is 1,300 loaves a day more than you can bake, with no slack at all.
- "Fits comfortably" is wrong. Even before Pellam you run at about 68% of capacity, and the contract would put you at about 114%.
- The memo also treats "every day of the year" as if it needs no downtime. An oven failure, a maintenance day or a staff shortage would mean missing Pellam's 6 a.m. delivery or shorting your cafés.

So to take the contract you would have to do one of three things:
- add capacity (a third shift or another oven);
- cut about 1,300 loaves a day from your 74 accounts, roughly a fifth of that business;
- or negotiate a lower volume with Pellam.

The first two answer your original question the wrong way round. Either they cost money the memo ignores, or they mean letting down existing customers. Until the cost of extra capacity is set against the £628,000, "clearly pays for itself" isn't established. A third shift also probably costs more than £0.97 a loaf.

**3. Risk: the reasoning is backwards**
- A three-year commitment doesn't show Pellam is financially solid. Large grocers sign long contracts because they get security of supply and a fixed price.
- Read the contract for what it actually protects. Look for guaranteed minimum volumes, payment terms, Pellam's right to terminate or reduce orders, and penalties on you for missed deliveries.
- The price is fixed at £1.38 for three years, so the risk of rising flour, energy and wage costs falls on you. Your margin is £0.41, so a rise of about 10p to 15p in cost per loaf would remove a quarter to a third of it.
- Pellam would be about 41% of your volume (4,200 of 10,300), so losing or renegotiating the contract would hurt a lot.

**4. Track record: two friendly references prove little**
Two bakeries that chose to talk to Tomasz, and who say it went well, are a thin sample. Bakeries where it went badly may have closed or may not be taking calls, and "I haven't heard of one" is exactly what you'd expect to hear. Ask Pellam for current suppliers to speak to, and ask Ines and Dmitri specific questions. What did it do to their margins and their existing accounts? Did they have to add capacity?

**5. Conclusion: not yet supported**
The contract could still be worth taking, but the memo hasn't shown it. Before Friday, I'd:
1. Price the extra capacity needed to cover the 1,300-loaf gap, plus a buffer for breakdowns, and subtract it from the £628,000.
2. Get the contract terms on minimum volumes, termination, price review and penalties.
3. Ask Pellam whether they would start with a smaller volume, or accept an inflation-linked price.
4. If you can't get these answers by Friday, ask for a short extension rather than signing.
