Your instinct is right. The memo does not answer the question you asked, and its capacity section, which is the one that matters most, is wrong. I wouldn't sign on the strength of it.

**What the memo should say**

You can't take Pellam on with your current ovens without letting existing customers down. Two shifts at 4,500 loaves is 9,000 a day. Your 74 accounts take about 6,100, and Pellam would add 4,200, which makes 10,300. That is 1,300 loaves a day more than you can bake, roughly a fifth of what your café and restaurant customers buy now. The memo seems to have compared Pellam's 4,200 with a single shift's 4,500 and stopped there.

The £628,000 is correct arithmetic (4,200 x £0.41 x 365 is about £628,500), but it is not the gain from the deal. To make the loaves you would need one of these:

- A third shift or more oven capacity. That means extra labour, energy, possibly equipment, and a plan for the early hours. Nothing in the memo costs it, and the £0.97 may not include it.
- Rationing your existing accounts to make room. That means short-changing about 1,300 loaves a day of your current business, which is the thing you asked Tomasz not to do. You would also lose whatever margin those loaves earn, and it is probably higher than £0.41, because café wholesale usually pays more than supermarket pricing.

The decision is therefore whether the Pellam margin, minus the cost of the extra capacity and any lost café business, is worth the commitment. The memo doesn't tell you that yet.

**Problems that change the conclusion**

- **Capacity.** Described above. The shortfall is about 1,300 loaves a day on average, and "around 6,100" is an average. Busy days will be worse. Pellam wants every day of the year, so there is no slack for oven maintenance, a breakdown, or a sick shift. A missed 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores is the kind of failure that supermarket contracts penalise.
- **Risk.** The memo says Pellam is low-risk because it is offering a three-year commitment, and that the commitment protects you. The second claim only holds if the first is already true. A long contract shows Pellam wants a secure supply. It says nothing about whether Pellam can pay, and it protects you only if the contract binds Pellam too. The memo doesn't say what Pellam is committing to. Check whether volumes are guaranteed or only a forecast, whether Pellam can end the contract or cut orders on notice, what the payment terms are, and what penalties apply if you miss a delivery.
- **Price.** £1.38 is fixed for three years in the memo as written. Flour, energy and wages can all move over that time. Without a price review clause, a rise in costs comes straight out of the £0.41, and a small rise takes a big share of it. Also check whether £0.97 is the full cost per loaf or only the ingredients and direct labour.
- **Track record.** Two friendly bakers who are glad they did it is not evidence about how often these deals go well. You have heard from the two who made it work and probably haven't heard from bakers who took a supermarket deal, were squeezed on price, and went quiet or closed. "I haven't heard of one going badly" mostly reflects who talks to Tomasz. Ask Ines and Dmitri what their capacity looked like before they signed, whether they had to add shifts, what happened to their existing customers, and how their margin changed in year two.
- **Dependence.** At £1.38, Pellam would be about £5,800 a day, over £2 million a year. That could easily become your largest customer by far. If they leave, cut volumes or renegotiate at renewal, you may have lost café customers in the meantime and be left with an oven shift you built for them.

**Still unresolved, and the cheapest way to check it**

- Whether a third shift or extra oven time is affordable. Get a rough cost of the extra labour, energy and any equipment, then rerun the margin on 4,200 loaves with that cost included. This is a morning's work and could settle the question.
- What the contract actually obliges Pellam to do. Ask for the draft terms and have a solicitor read the volume, termination, payment and penalty clauses before Friday.
- What your café and restaurant loaves earn per loaf, so you know what rationing them would cost.
- Whether Pellam would take less than 4,200 or phase in the volume. If the answer is yes, that is the best way to get this deal without hurting existing customers, and it costs nothing to ask before the deadline.

My advice is not to sign on Friday as it stands. Reply asking for the draft terms and a possible phase-in or lower starting volume, and ask Tomasz to rework the memo with the capacity gap and its cost in it. If a third shift pays for itself and the contract protects you, the deal may still be good. The memo hasn't shown that.
