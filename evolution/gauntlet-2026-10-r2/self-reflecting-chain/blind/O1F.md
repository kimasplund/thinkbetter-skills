Short answer: don't sign on the strength of this memo. The margin arithmetic is right, but the capacity claim, which was your actual question, is wrong, and the risk and track-record sections don't support the conclusion.

**1. The margin figure checks out, but it is only a starting point**

4,200 x £0.41 = £1,722 a day, and x 365 = about £628,500 a year. So "about £628,000" is correct as gross margin at the stated cost of £0.97 a loaf. Two things it doesn't tell you:
- Whether £0.97 is a full cost or only the cost of ingredients and direct labour. If it excludes the extra shift, delivery and overheads, the real margin is lower.
- That the price is fixed for three years. If flour, energy or wages rise, the £0.41 shrinks and you can't pass it on unless the contract has a price-review clause.

**2. Capacity: Pellam does not fit in what you have**

- Two shifts at 4,500 loaves = 9,000 loaves a day of capacity.
- Current demand is about 6,100, which leaves roughly 2,900 spare.
- Pellam needs 4,200, so total demand would be about 10,300.
- That is about 1,300 loaves a day over capacity, around 14% above what the ovens can produce.

The memo says "comfortably within the capacity we already have", but the numbers say the opposite. The shortfall has to come from somewhere:
- A third shift or more oven capacity. That means extra cost, which is not in the £628k.
- Or cutting supply to your café and restaurant accounts, which is exactly what you asked Tomasz to avoid.

Other capacity problems the memo doesn't mention:
- "Every day of the year" leaves no room for oven maintenance, breakdowns, holidays or a bad week. Ask what the penalties are if you miss a delivery.
- A 6 a.m. delivery to 11 stores needs baking overnight and a delivery run. Your café accounts probably want morning bread too, so the two peaks may collide.
- Your 6,100 is "around" a daily figure. If your busy days run higher, the gap is bigger than 1,300.

**3. Risk: the three-year term doesn't show Pellam is safe**

- A customer offering a long contract is not evidence that it is financially solid. Plenty of shaky retailers sign long contracts.
- The commitment protects you only if it binds Pellam to buy. Check whether it guarantees volumes or only states what they may order, and what happens if they cut orders, delist you, or terminate with short notice. Many supermarket contracts bind the supplier tightly and the buyer loosely.
- You'd be supplying 4,200 loaves out of about 10,300, roughly 40% of output, to one customer. If they leave or squeeze the price at renewal, you'd have a business shaped around them and may have lost café accounts in the meantime.
- Payment terms matter. Large grocers often pay in 60 days or more, which ties up cash. Get a credit check on Pellam.

**4. Track record: two friendly references are weak evidence**

- Two bakers chosen by Tomasz, who both say it went well, is a small and probably favourable sample. Bakers who were hurt by these deals are less likely to be the ones people mention, and "I haven't heard of one going badly" is only about what has reached him.
- It also says nothing about your situation. Ask them whether they had spare capacity, what they did about their existing customers, and what their margin is after the extra costs. Ask also what the contract terms were.

**5. The conclusion doesn't follow**

"£628,000 a year, so it clearly pays for itself" assumes the capacity is free, the cost figure is complete, Pellam pays reliably, and nothing is lost elsewhere. None of that is shown. A fair comparison would be:

- Margin from Pellam, minus the cost of adding capacity (shift, equipment, delivery, cash tied up).
- Minus whatever margin you would lose if any café or restaurant accounts are short-changed or leave. Those 74 accounts are probably at higher prices per loaf than £1.38.

**What I'd do before Friday**

1. Cost out a third shift or extra ovens to cover the 1,300 loaf gap, and recompute the margin.
2. Ask Pellam (or your solicitor) about volume guarantees, termination, price review, penalties for missed deliveries, and payment terms.
3. Run a credit check on Pellam.
4. Ask the two reference bakers the specific questions above.
5. If you can't get answers by Friday, ask for a short extension, or counter with a smaller volume (for example about 2,900 loaves a day, which fits in your spare capacity), or a phased start.

This could still be a good deal. But as written, the memo hasn't shown that you can take it on without letting down your existing customers, and on the numbers given, you can't without adding capacity.
