Short answer: I wouldn't circulate it as it stands. The journey-time figure is probably real, but the report doesn't support its conclusion, and its own dates contradict its method. Here is what I found, in the order the report makes its claims.

**1. The "12 weeks after" window doesn't fit the dates given**
The lane opened on 3 February and the data runs to 31 March. That is 8 weeks (56 days), not 12. Twelve weeks after opening would run to about 28 April. So either the after-period was only 8 weeks, which means the comparison isn't like for like, or the end date or the window is misstated. Mara needs to say which. Everything below depends on it.

**2. The journey-time result is arithmetically fine but thinly supported**
35 to 30 minutes is a 5-minute drop, which is 14.3% of 35, so "14%" is correct. What's missing is the context a member would ask for:
- It is one route (route 7) on one road. Kessel carries routes 22 and 31.
- There is no comparison group, such as a similar route with no lane. A before/after gap in winter could partly reflect lower traffic after the holidays, weather, roadworks or school terms.
- There is no spread, only an average. Did reliability improve, or just the mean?
- There is no mention of whether the 35 minutes is end to end or only the lane section.

**3. The ridership claim is a causal claim the data can't carry**
"That rise confirms passengers were getting faster journeys, and the faster journeys are what drew the extra riders" is the weakest sentence. A 9% rise coinciding with the lane doesn't show the lane caused it. The before period is roughly November to January and the after period is February to March, so seasonal change, fare changes, events or service changes could all explain a rise. Riders also take time to notice a faster service, so an effect within 8 weeks would be surprising. Defensible wording: "ridership rose 9% over the same period; we can't yet say how much, if any, is due to the lane." Better still, compare with ridership on routes without a lane over the same dates.

**4. The feedback section is one-sided, and the omitted half matters most for Kessel**
- 38 bus drivers are positive about a lane that makes their own job easier. That is useful operational feedback but not independent evidence that the lane works for the street.
- 61 shopkeeper comments about delivery access were set aside as "anecdotal and not representative." Yet the drivers' 38 comments are equally anecdotal, and the shopkeepers' comments outnumber them. Dismissing one group and counting the other is inconsistent.
- This matters because Kessel has 14 loading bays used by its shops, with one lane each way. The delivery access issue is the main risk to transferring the pilot to Kessel. The committee should see what those 61 comments said, summarised by theme (for example, how many reported blocked deliveries, lost trade or fines), plus any objective data such as parking or loading complaints and shop turnover on Varrow Road.

**5. "Under budget" is not evidence the lane worked**
£410,000 against £450,000 is £40,000 under, about 9% below budget. That is a statement about cost control, not outcomes. A project can be on budget and ineffective. Also, if Kessel's design is different (loading bays to retain or relocate, one lane each way), the Varrow Road cost is not a safe estimate for Kessel. Say what a Kessel lane would be expected to cost.

**6. The recommendation overreaches**
"Clearly worked" is stronger than one route, one winter and an 8-week window can support, and the committee is being asked to apply it to a different street. Key unanswered questions:
- How similar is Varrow Road to Kessel (width, lane configuration, number of loading bays, traffic volumes, bus frequency)?
- What happened to general traffic and to side-street traffic?
- What happened to delivery access and the shops on Varrow Road?

**What I would do before Thursday**
1. Ask Mara to confirm the actual after-period length and, if possible, extend the data to a full 12 weeks.
2. Reword the ridership paragraph so it reports the change without claiming it was caused by the lane. Add a comparison route if one is available.
3. Summarise the 61 shopkeeper comments and report them alongside the driver comments.
4. Add a short comparison of Varrow Road and Kessel, with the 14 loading bays set out explicitly.
5. Remove "clearly worked." Something like "journey times on route 7 fell by about 14% in the pilot; evidence on ridership and on impact on local businesses is incomplete" is accurate. The recommendation could then be conditional, for example approval subject to a loading-bay plan for Kessel, or a trial.

I haven't seen the underlying data, so the points above are about what the report says and doesn't say. The 14% figure may well stand once checked, and the lane may be a good idea for Kessel. The report just doesn't yet show that.
