**Recommendation: strengthen the arches and armour the pier (€2.4M), done in the July to September 2027 window. Keep the 26-tonne limit until then.**

I've assumed the €2.4M design gives a long life (50 years or more). Ask the consultants to confirm that in writing before December.

**Why the two options people favour don't hold up**

*Demolish and rebuild (€1.3M to you).* The 75% grant is conditional on spending by March 2028. Your own numbers don't fit:
- Work in the river is only allowed July to September, and the build needs two such summers. If the first is 2027, the second is 2028, which is after the deadline.
- Starting after a December decision, with design, procurement and consents, the 20 months run to late 2028 at best.
- Demolishing a bridge on the national heritage register needs consent, and that is far from certain to be granted when a strengthening scheme exists.
- The emergency limit is only good for about two years, to roughly August 2028. A slip leaves you with a failing bridge and no grant.

The €1.3M is real only if the fund will accept a different spend profile, for example committed or milestone spend, or an extension. Ask them now, in writing. If they say yes and heritage consent looks achievable, the answer changes. I wouldn't bet the town's main road on it.

*Permanent 7.5-tonne limit (€150k).* This looks cheap but fails on the facts you gave:
- Ferris Valley Coaches' double-deckers carry 380 pupils a day. They would be barred from the bridge.
- A double-decker is about 4.3 m or more tall, so it can't use Cott Lane's 4.0 m railway arch either. That leaves no sensible route for the school buses, and I'd check the Holm Ford and other alternatives before ruling it out entirely.
- A 7.5-tonne limit would also catch fire engines, refuse lorries and other essential vehicles.
- It doesn't fix the cracking or the scour. It only slows the decline, and the bridge still needs a long-term answer.

The tippers using Cott Lane without trouble is useful. It shows the 26-tonne limit costs little, because the heaviest vehicles have already moved. That is a reason to keep 26 tonnes, not to cut to 7.5.

**Why strengthening wins**
- It is the only option that fits the river window. One summer (2027) is achievable. Design and consents can run through winter and spring, and the work is done before the emergency limit's two years expire.
- It keeps the 1794 listed bridge, which is on every postcard and has been on the register since 1971. It also avoids the hardest consent fight.
- It keeps the school buses and the 9,000 vehicles a day on the existing route.
- At €2.4M it costs less than the €7M Holm Ford scheme, and it doesn't depend on a grant deadline you can't meet.

The downside is that the whole €2.4M is yours. That is €1.1M more than the best-case rebuild share, but you avoid the schedule and consent risk and the loss of a heritage asset.

**Why not the other two**
- Temporary steel bridge (€0.9M): it only buys time. The old bridge keeps deteriorating, you pay again later, and building it alongside means more river and heritage work. It makes sense only if the strengthening can't be finished in 2027.
- Footbridge plus Holm Ford (€7M, about 3 years): the most expensive option. It misses the grant deadline too, and it moves the main road. It also loses the vehicle crossing at the heart of the town.

**What I'd put to cabinet**
1. Approve strengthening and pier armouring for summer 2027. Start design, heritage consent and the Environment Board agreement now. Missing the 2027 window means waiting for 2028.
2. Keep the 26-tonne limit and enforce it. Add scour monitoring and a flood-trigger inspection through winter 2026-27, because the pier can't be armoured until July.
3. Ask the regional fund whether any part of the work could qualify, or whether the deadline can move. It costs nothing to ask.
4. Name a fallback. If consents or design slip past spring 2027, hire the temporary bridge (€0.9M) rather than lose a year.
5. Brief the cabinet member for transport early. They want the rebuild, so show them the March 2028 problem with the river-window dates.

**Main risks:** the strengthening design may prove harder than priced, the consultants' life estimate may be optimistic, and a winter flood could force an earlier closure. All three argue for acting quickly and keeping the fallback ready.
