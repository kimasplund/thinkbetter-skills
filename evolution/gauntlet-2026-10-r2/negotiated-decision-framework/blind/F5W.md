Short version: keep equal shares as the legal backbone, but fix the two things in your draft that quietly break it for the orchard growers (the August 1 closure and unrestricted-price trading), and check three facts before March 18 that could change who really holds the votes.

## What is actually in conflict

The orchard growers and row-crop growers look like they disagree about fairness. Underneath, they disagree about much less.

- **Orchards** don't need more water overall. They need a specific block of water at a specific time: post-harvest water for bud set, delivered to trees they can't replace for years. Priority for the whole season is their stated position, but a guaranteed late-season supply is their real interest.
- **Row-crop growers** need the bylaws honored and they need the right to trade. They don't need to take water away from trees. Many can fallow or deficit-irrigate in a way an orchard can't.
- **Orrin** has said he'll probably fallow half his hay. That means he is likely a net seller of water under your plan. Equal shares plus open trading is attractive to him for that reason, and the orchards will see it. Be ready for the objection that "equal shares" mostly means the biggest member gets a large tradable asset while growers on the east lateral pay for it.

So this is mostly a timing and delivery problem, not a split of the total. That is solvable.

## Problems in the draft itself

1. **The August 1 east lateral closure lands on the orchards.** Many of them sit on that canal, and August onward is exactly the post-harvest window they say matters most. As written, equal shares on paper becomes unequal delivery in practice: an east-lateral cherry grower gets 55% of an entitlement they can't physically receive after August 1. A row-crop grower on another lateral does get it. The orchards will read this as the plan quietly choosing against them, and they will be right. The 9% conveyance saving is real, but weigh it against the cost of an unratifiable plan.
2. **The Dunmere agreement.** The 1997 agreement is tied to seepage from the east lateral, and $40,000 a year depends on it. Closing the lateral early could reduce the seepage the town relies on for well recharge. Read the agreement's terms before March 18. If it requires minimum flows or a delivery season, an early closure could put that payment at risk or trigger a dispute, which would offset some of the conveyance savings. Also ask Dunmere early. They are a stakeholder with a financial and water-supply interest, and they should hear this from you, not discover it.
3. **Open-price trading.** With no price limit, a shortage year lets well-capitalized members buy water from those who can afford to sell, and orchards that must keep trees alive are price-takers. That is the likeliest way your plan produces a result the orchards will resent even if they voted for it. It also invites a pocket veto: members who feel squeezed stop cooperating.

## Check the vote before you design anything

- **The Bennings sold their land to Quillon Land Partners and lease it back.** Ratification is weighted by acreage owned. That means the 2,900 acres Neil farms may now carry Quillon's vote, not his. Quillon's interests (land value, lease terms, whether they want the water tradable or the tomatoes kept in production) may differ from Neil's, and they may not have been in your conversations at all. Find out who holds the vote and talk to them directly.
- **Orrin's vote.** Orrin owns or controls 3,400 acres. With acreage weighting, a small number of large owners can outweigh many small orchard parcels. Count the actual owned acreage, split by orchard and row crop, before you assume what a ratification vote would do. If large holders can carry the vote without the orchards, a plan that "wins" may still not be one the orchards stick to.
- **Is your plan a departure from equal shares?** Equal shares plus trading may need only a board vote. Any carve-out or priority for orchards triggers the weighted member vote. Ask your counsel whether a delivery-timing adjustment (rather than a priority on quantity) counts as a departure. This decides how you structure it.
- **Board conflicts.** All seven board members are growers, and the chair has a clear financial position. Record conflicts and have those members state them, or the orchards will challenge the process afterwards.

## What I would put in front of the board

**Core: equal 55% shares for all 64 farms, with the bylaws untouched.** That keeps the row-crop growers and Orrin on side and avoids the weighted ratification vote where possible.

**Add a delivery-timing guarantee.** Instead of priority, offer a reserved post-harvest block. For example, each member's allocation is scheduled so that a defined share is held back for delivery after harvest, and east-lateral farms get that share delivered through the lateral on a published schedule. Orchards get the water they actually need when they need it. Row-crop growers lose nothing on quantity. Check the numbers with your operations staff first: what volume the lateral must carry post-harvest, and what it costs in conveyance losses.

**Replace the hard August 1 closure with a conditional one.** Options, from least to most disruptive:
- keep the lateral open for scheduled post-harvest deliveries only, with set delivery windows, so losses fall without cutting off the orchards;
- close it on August 1 only if east-lateral members have already taken their post-harvest block, or if they can be served by another route;
- if it must close, deliver the post-harvest block by exchange or a different lateral, with the orchards' agreement.

The saving is 9% of conveyance loss, so test whether the windowed approach captures most of it.

**Keep trading, but give it guardrails.**
- Allow trades among members, with a price cap or a published reference price, or at least require trades to be reported. A rule that growers with permanent crops can have first call on offered water at the posted price for a short period (a "right of first offer" for tree-crop members) gives the orchards real protection without altering shares.
- Limit carryover of traded water for resale outside the district.
- Consider a district-run exchange so everyone sees prices and no one is squeezed in private deals.

**Make it a one-year plan with a review point.** Say it explicitly: this is a 2026 shortage plan, reviewed in the autumn. Allocation shortfall is uncertain (reservoir at 38%, authority guidance at 55%). Build in a trigger: if the allocation is revised below, say, 45%, the board reconvenes, with the post-harvest block protected first.

## Who gets what

| Group | What they get | What they give |
|---|---|---|
| Orchard growers | A guaranteed post-harvest block, delivered on schedule; first call on offered water | No whole-season priority; accept equal shares |
| Row-crop growers | Bylaws honored; trading kept; no quantity loss | A scheduled holdback for post-harvest; some trading limits |
| Orrin and large holders | Equal shares, open trading, mostly intact | Some price guardrails and reporting |
| District | Most of the conveyance savings, a plan that survives ratification, Dunmere kept whole | Slightly more complex operations and scheduling |
| Dunmere | Continued recharge | Nothing, provided the agreement is checked and respected |

## Getting it adopted so it holds

1. **Talk to the orchard growers first, individually**, then as a group. Ask what a failed bud set costs them and what delivery they need in what weeks. Their numbers will make the case better than you can.
2. **Brief Orrin before the meeting.** He is firmly for equal shares, and this plan keeps equal shares. Show that the holdback doesn't cost him quantity, and that the guardrails protect the district from a bad outcome that would come back on the board. Because he may be a seller, a modest reference price or right-of-first-offer costs him little.
3. **Reach Quillon directly** about the Bennings' acreage, and confirm who votes.
4. **Call Dunmere** about the lateral timing and the 1997 agreement.
5. **Bring the plan with the numbers**: allocation by lateral, post-harvest volume needed, conveyance savings under each closure option. Show the 9% saving beside the cost of losing the orchards' support.
6. **Write down what happens if someone doesn't comply**, such as over-diversion or failing to report trades, and who decides. Settle this before the vote; it's far easier than arguing about it after a violation.
7. **Put commitments in the resolution itself**, with dates: when the post-harvest schedule is published, when trades are reported, when the review happens. A plan with named dates is much harder to quietly drop or reopen.
