**Recommendation:** Keep equal 55% shares, because that is the one piece nobody can attack under the bylaws and it needs no weighted vote. Then change two things in your draft that actually decide whether the orchards stay in: when water can be delivered, and what happens to the east lateral on August 1. Put a few guardrails on trading so it works for the orchards instead of against them.

**Why the draft as written is likely to fail**

- **The August 1 shutoff lands on the water the orchards care about most.** Cherries come off in early summer. The post-harvest irrigation, which sets next year's fruit buds, falls right after that, and many of those growers sit on the lateral you want to close. You would be giving them equal shares of a volume they can't receive when they need it. They will fight it, and it is the reason they are asking for priority.
- **The Dunmere agreement may be a problem.** The $40,000 a year is tied to seepage from the east lateral recharging the town's wells. Closing the canal early may cut that seepage, and it could be read as a breach of the 1997 agreement or a reason to renegotiate it. Read the agreement's wording before March 18. Dunmere's staff are also a stakeholder: they can reopen the payment, and they would likely welcome an arrangement that keeps some recharge flowing.
- **Open trading at any price favors the people with surplus water.** Orrin says he'll probably fallow half his hay. That makes him the largest likely seller, and he is also the chair running the meeting. Whether or not he intends it, the orchards will see a plan that lets the board chair sell them their own survival water at whatever the market bears. Even if the price turns out fair, that perception will cost you the orchards' compliance.
- **The vote may not belong to the people sitting on your board.** Ratification is weighted by acreage owned. The Bennings sold their 2,900 acres to Quillon Land Partners, so on any ratification vote those acres appear to count for Quillon, not Neil. Neil's steady backing of Orrin tells you about his preferences as a tenant, not about how Quillon will vote. Quillon is not in your room and may not have a view yet, or may have one tied to lease terms and crop returns. Find out who votes those acres, and talk to Quillon before March 18.

**What to put in front of the board**

1. **Equal 55% volumes for every farm.** This stays inside the bylaws, so the row-crop growers get what they were promised and no ratification vote is triggered.
2. **Let each farm schedule its volume across the season.** Within its 55%, a farm can hold part of its allocation for later delivery, for example reserving a block for September. This is not priority for anyone. It is the same water on a different schedule, and the orchards get what they need without taking from anyone else. The row-crop growers lose nothing, since their own volume is untouched. Check that the reservoir and the basin authority's rules allow carrying water into late season.
3. **Delay the east lateral closure to after the post-harvest delivery window,** or run it in short scheduled pulses instead of closing it outright. You give up part of the 9% conveyance saving, but that is a known, bounded cost, and you can offset it by asking the lateral's users to take their reserved water in a few concentrated runs so the canal flows only when it is useful. Ask your operations staff for the actual loss number by week, because the saving is probably much smaller in September than in July.
4. **Keep trading, with a district-run exchange.** Trades go through a simple posted-offer board, with a price ceiling or a published index for the season, and a rule that district water can only move to farms inside the district. Fallowing growers like Orrin get paid for water they won't use. Orchards can buy extra water for buds without bidding against each other. The board stays out of setting prices beyond the cap, and any board member who is a party to a trade should disclose it.
5. **Write the closure date and the scheduling rule into the plan itself,** along with a mid-season review (for example June 15) at which the board can adjust if the basin authority revises the allocation.

**What each group is getting**

- **Orchards:** the post-harvest water arrives when it matters, on their own lateral, and they can buy more through a transparent board. Their concession is that they don't get priority, and that any water above their share costs money.
- **Row-crop growers:** the bylaws are honored, with no change to their volumes. Their concession is a later lateral closure, which costs the whole district a few percentage points of conveyance, and a price ceiling on trades.
- **Orrin and the fallowing growers:** they still sell. Their concession is a price cap and disclosure, which actually protects Orrin from being accused of profiting from the shortage.
- **Neil and Quillon:** equal shares and open trading are intact. Quillon's concern is probably lease economics and what a tomato crop is worth this season, so ask them directly what would change their view.
- **Dunmere:** continued recharge from the east lateral, at least through the shoulder season.

**How to run the next three weeks**

- Talk to Orrin first and privately. His own fallowing makes him the person most able to back a plan with a trading cap, and he is more likely to support it if it comes with a way to be seen as fair.
- Bring one or two cherry growers and one or two of the larger row-crop growers into a small working session to set the delivery schedule. People who helped design a schedule tend to keep to it.
- Contact Quillon about how it will vote and what it needs from its tenant. Contact Dunmere about the agreement language and what recharge volume it needs.
- Have the board adopt the plan with a clear record of what was agreed, and put the mid-season review date in the minutes, so nobody can reopen the allocation formula in July.

**What would change this recommendation**

If the Dunmere agreement specifically requires a minimum east lateral flow into the fall, the late closure is not optional, and your plan should be built around that. If instead it only guarantees payment in return for generally maintaining the lateral, you have more room. The cheapest check is to read the agreement and call Dunmere's utility manager this week. A second fact that could flip things: if the orchards are in practice a small share of the lateral's users, or if their buds can survive on a much smaller late-season volume than they say, then the delivery-schedule change matters less and your original closure date could survive with a smaller adjustment. Ask two or three orchard growers for their actual post-harvest water need, in acre-feet, before March 18.
