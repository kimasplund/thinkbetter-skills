The short version: keep equal shares, so the plan never needs the acreage-weighted ratification vote. Fix the two things in your draft that quietly work against the orchard growers, and let the orchards get their priority by buying water rather than by claiming a right to it. That gives the row-crop growers the bylaw outcome they want and gives the orchards what they actually need.

**Two problems in the draft to fix before March 18**

1. **The August 1 east lateral closure lands exactly on the orchards' critical water.** Cherries are harvested by early summer. The post-harvest irrigation the growers describe falls in August and September, and a lot of those trees are on the east lateral. As written, your plan is not neutral. It takes away the water the orchards say matters most, and it saves about 9% in conveyance losses. If you leave it, the orchards will fight the whole plan, and they'll have a fair point.
2. **The Dunmere agreement may limit the closure.** The 1997 agreement pays $40,000 a year for seepage from the east lateral. Before the board votes, read what it requires: whether the lateral has to carry water, for how long, and what Dunmere can do if flows stop early. If a closure triggers a breach or a loss of the payment, the "saving" may cost you $40,000 and a lawsuit. Ask counsel for a one-page reading this week. Also ask whether Dunmere would accept a reduced flow.

**What each side actually needs**

- **Orchard growers.** Their stated demand is priority. What they need is dependable water from after harvest through about October, and protection from losing trees that take years to replace. A guaranteed share of the district's water for them is one way to get that. It isn't the only way.
- **Row-crop growers.** Their stated demand is equal shares, and the bylaws support them. What they need is not to have their share quietly cut for someone else's benefit, and to keep the flexibility to trade or fallow.
- **The conflict is smaller than it looks.** Orrin has said he'll likely fallow half his hay anyway. That means a large block of water will be freed up voluntarily at a time when the orchards badly need more. The orchards want water, some row-crop growers have water they don't plan to use, and a trade can connect the two without any change to the bylaws.

**A package that can work**

1. **Equal 55% shares for every member.** This keeps you inside the bylaws and avoids the weighted ratification vote, which the orchards would probably lose.
2. **Trading inside the district, with a few rules.** Open trading at any price is the part most likely to cause a fight. Orrin and Neil are likely sellers, and the orchards are likely buyers with little bargaining power. I'd suggest:
   - A district-run trade desk, so every trade is visible and recorded.
   - A set trading window in spring and another right after harvest, so orchards can buy when they know their need.
   - Either a published reference price range or a district-run pool that buys fallowed water at a posted price and resells it. That limits price gouging in a dry year without taking on the question of priority. A posted price also protects sellers from being underbid.
3. **A time-limited east lateral schedule in place of a hard August 1 closure.** Keep the lateral open on a reduced schedule for post-harvest irrigation through a fixed date, probably mid-September to October. Decide the date with the orchards and the hydrology in front of you. This costs some of the 9% savings, but it also protects the Dunmere payment. If you can, recover the lost savings elsewhere, for example by running the lateral only on set days.
4. **An orchard water reserve, funded voluntarily.** Fallowing members who want to can sell into a pool earmarked for post-harvest tree water. The district could add some of the $40,000, or other budget room, to improve the price. Check this against the Dunmere agreement and what the payment is for. If the money is restricted, don't use it.
5. **A mid-season review.** Set a date, for example June 15, to look at the actual basin allocation and reservoir level. If the allocation comes in lower than 55%, shares go down for everyone equally, and the trade desk and the lateral schedule adjust. A fixed review date makes it harder for anyone to relitigate the plan in July.

**Who actually decides, and a conflict of interest to handle openly**

- **Check who owns the vote on the Benning acreage.** You say the Bennings sold that land to Quillon Land Partners and lease it back. If a ratification vote is ever needed, it is weighted by acreage owned. That means Quillon, not Neil, may hold the vote on 2,900 acres. Neil's backing of Orrin at board meetings may not tell you what Quillon wants. Find out whether Quillon is a member, who speaks for it, and what it cares about. Quillon may care more about lease economics or land value than about the board's politics. Quillon's position could matter more than Neil's.
- **Orrin has a stake in the rules he is supporting.** He chairs the board, he farms 3,400 acres of alfalfa, he plans to fallow half, and he supports open trading with no price rules. He may be sincere, but he stands to profit from selling water in a short year. The orchards will see this. To keep the process credible, I'd suggest he not chair the discussion on trading rules, or at least disclose his position and let another board member run that item. If you put the trading rules to a vote, ask conflicted members to say so on the record. This costs you little and prevents the plan from being called a deal among the big growers.
- **Who is on the board?** All seven are growers. Count how many are orchard growers, and how many have land on the east lateral. If the orchards have no seat or one seat, they have no way to be heard in the room, and may go to the vote or to the basin authority instead. Give them a formal part in setting the lateral schedule and the reserve rules.

**How to get it adopted**

1. **Before the meeting, talk to people separately.** Meet the orchard growers, then Orrin, then the other board members one by one. Ask the orchards what date and what volume they need after harvest, with real numbers: acre-feet per acre, and what happens to the trees at 55% without it. Ask Orrin how much he expects to fallow and what he would accept for it. Ask Quillon what it wants.
2. **Show the numbers.** Put one page in front of the board: what 55% means per acre for each crop, what the orchards need after harvest, how much fallowed water is likely, and what the east lateral options cost in conveyance losses and in Dunmere risk. Many disagreements shrink when everyone sees the same figures.
3. **Make the offer to the row-crop side in their terms.** Equal shares, bylaw-compliant, no ratification vote, trading stays. The cost to them is a few trading rules and a later lateral closing. Their benefit is that the orchards stop pushing for priority.
4. **Make the offer to the orchards in theirs.** They don't get priority, but they get water in the window that matters, a pool to buy from at a known price, and a lateral that stays open when they need it.
5. **Write it down and put in a way out.** Put the lateral dates, the trading rules, and the review date into the adopted plan. If a member thinks the rules aren't being followed, they bring it to a named person (a board member or an outside facilitator) within a set number of days, rather than leaving it to grow into a bigger fight.

**What could still go wrong**

- **The orchards won't accept anything short of priority.** In that case the plan stays at equal shares, which you can pass without a vote. They can still buy water. Tell them early that a priority change would require a weighted vote they are unlikely to win, and that the trade and reserve route is the realistic one.
- **Not enough fallowed water gets offered.** Then the reserve is too small to matter. Test this early by asking members what they would sell, and at what price, before you build the plan around it.
- **The Dunmere reading is bad.** If the agreement requires the east lateral to run late, the August 1 closure is off the table and the debate is mostly over, so find out first.
- **Prices spike anyway.** The posted-price pool and the review date are your safeguards. Be ready to use them.

I'd rate the chance of adoption as good if the Dunmere reading holds and the orchards get a real post-harvest window. It is poor if you keep the August 1 closure as drafted, because that gives the orchard growers a strong, fair objection. I haven't seen the bylaws, the Dunmere agreement, or the exact orchard acreage. Check each of those before the meeting.
