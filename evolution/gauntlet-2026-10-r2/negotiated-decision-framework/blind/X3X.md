The short version is that winning the vote and keeping people with you depend on the same thing. Before you lobby anyone, settle four facts that could stop the sale, and sequence the communication so families aren't asked to commit before they know what's happening. Below I'd treat the plan as a decision process rather than a campaign.

## Four things to resolve before you count votes

These are the items most likely to sink the plan, and each is cheaper to learn now than in January.

**a. The Halloran gift.** "For the education of the children of Brennock" may be only a plaque, or it may be a restriction in the deed or gift instrument, possibly with a reverter or a right of reentry. Sale to a developer for housing is the use a restriction would most plausibly bar.
- Have counsel pull the 1958 deed and any gift correspondence now.
- If there is a restriction, the usual routes are a court cy-pres or deviation petition, or the Halloran heirs' consent. In many jurisdictions the state attorney general or charity regulator must be notified. All of that takes months and sits outside your control.
- Even if there is no legal bar, the moral claim stays. Trustees, alumni and the local press will quote the plaque. Plan to answer it directly: proceeds stay in Brennock children's education, and the school might commemorate the family on the new building.
- Vellacott's letter of intent should be conditional on clear title and any required approvals. Check that it is. If it isn't, fix that before February 12.

**b. The Sycamore Lane conditions.** You describe the enrolment ceiling as one "we've never come close to". That is the condition that matters most, because the plan takes the south campus from 410 to 640 or more.
- Find the exact number. If it is below 640, or below your growth case, consolidation needs a permit modification, and the neighbours who fought you in 2004 get a say.
- The traffic marshals and the 9pm event curfew will also come under pressure once 230 more children arrive and drop-off volume rises by more than half. Your consultant's capacity of 700 is a physical figure and says nothing about legal capacity.
- Commission a traffic study and open talks with the neighbours early. A visible offer such as staggered drop-off, a second entrance or a shuttle will be worth more than a late surprise at a planning hearing.

**c. The Ardelle pledge.** The $800k still to come was pledged for the north-campus outdoor learning program. Closing the campus may let the foundation stop paying, or even ask for repayment.
- Read the pledge agreement and call the foundation yourself. Don't let them hear about the closure from a parent.
- Ask whether the funds can be redirected to an outdoor program on the south campus. If they can, that is a strong part of your story. If they can't, take the $800k out of the financial case.

**d. The financial case itself.** $13.5M against an $8M building leaves $5.5M, but check what's left once you account for these:
- transaction and legal costs
- any Halloran or Ardelle consequences
- construction overrun (a contingency of 15–20% on $8M is $1.2–1.6M)
- one-off costs of transition and severance
- lost revenue if families leave

You need to show that the annual deficit, which is $1.1M now, actually closes. A sale does not fix an operating deficit by itself. Savings from running one campus (staff, utilities, maintenance, transport between sites) have to be itemised, and the net should be shown with low, base and high re-enrolment. Ask whether 92% re-enrolment is realistic. If 8% of 230 lower-school families and a few upper-school families leave, check what that does to the budget. Then run it at 85%.

## The timeline problem

As you've described it, the order is:
- January 10: re-enrolment contracts go out
- February 12: board votes
- March 1: contracts are due

Families will have to decide whether to re-enrol before the board has decided anything, or else they'll decide after hearing a plan that has not been approved. The lower-school families have the strongest reason to leave, so this is where you are most exposed. Either way, you risk losing the families you're trying to keep and weakening your budget assumption before the vote.

Options, in rough order of preference:
1. **Move the vote earlier** (December, if the board can be ready), so contracts go out with a decided plan.
2. **Delay the contracts** for lower-school families, or extend their deadline to mid-March, with a clear explanation.
3. **Add a short, fair exit clause** for families whose circumstances change because of the closure. This costs something, but it removes the "trapped" feeling and lowers the cost of saying yes.
4. If the date can't move, state plainly in the contract letter what the board is considering and when it will decide. Don't let people sign in the dark.

Also be realistic about the 2028 date. A fall 2028 opening for a new $8M building requires design, approvals and construction. If any of (a) or (b) above go badly, that date slips. Say "fall 2028 target" rather than promising it.

## How to get ten of fifteen

You need two-thirds. That means you can lose only five trustees, so a "win" with ten barely-persuaded votes will be fragile when the first hard news arrives.

- **Count honestly.** Talk to each trustee one-on-one and sort them into supporters, persuadable and opposed. Ask each one what would change their mind, and what they are most afraid of. Do this before you present the plan.
- **Give trustees an option set, not one plan.** Show 3–4 alternatives with the same financial assumptions, for example: (1) close north and consolidate; (2) consolidate but keep a smaller early-years site on the north campus; (3) sell part of the north land and keep the school there; (4) keep both campuses and cut costs and raise tuition; (5) lease the north site rather than sell. If the board sees that you looked at alternatives fairly and why you prefer one, the vote is much more defensible. If you've only considered consolidation, say so and fix that.
- **Name the risks, and the conditions under which you would stop.** For instance: "We proceed only if (i) title is clear, (ii) the permit allows 650+, (iii) Ardelle's position is known, (iv) re-enrolment is at least X%." Trustees who are uneasy can vote yes more easily on a plan with explicit tripwires. Consider a two-stage resolution: a vote to proceed to due diligence, followed by a final vote when conditions are met.
- **Manage conflicts and independence.** Check whether any trustee has a connection to Vellacott, the Hallorans, or the neighbours, and handle recusals in the open. Consider whether an independent valuation or a second bid is warranted. At $13.5M you want to be able to say the price was tested.
- **Fiduciary framing.** Present the decision as the trustees' duty to the school's survival, with a credible account of the downside of doing nothing: a $1.1M deficit every year and falling enrolment, with a number for how many years of reserves that is.

## Parents, staff and community

Different groups are worried about different things. Treat them separately.

**Lower-school parents** (commute, little kids with teenagers):
- Design is the answer to the second worry. Plan a physically distinct lower-school building with its own entrance, playground, schedule for breaks and lunch, and drop-off lane. Show them drawings. Involve a few parents in the design committee.
- For the commute, offer a concrete package: a bus service between neighbourhoods and the south campus, early drop-off and after-care, and perhaps a fee reduction for the first year.
- Bring a few parents into a working group, with real input on things that can change (design, transport, schedule, transition), and be honest about what is not open to negotiation.

**Staff** (jobs):
- Give an early, clear commitment about what you can promise. If you can say "no layoffs due to consolidation; reductions only through attrition", say it. If you can't, say what you can: the process, the timeline, severance and placement support, and priority for roles in the new building.
- Meet staff before the parents learn about it, and before it leaks. Staff who hear it second will assume the worst.
- Name a point person for questions and publish a Q&A that you update.

**The wider community** (the Hallorans, alumni, neighbours, local press):
- Reach the Halloran family or descendants before the news is public. Ask for their blessing, and consider a naming or memorial element for the new building.
- Brief Sycamore Lane neighbours early, as above.
- Prepare a short public statement about the sale and the use of the money: the proceeds go to students' education.

## What the plan document should contain

1. The problem: enrolment trend, deficit, reserves, and the cost of doing nothing.
2. The options considered and why this one is preferred.
3. Legal and regulatory path: Halloran, planning conditions, Ardelle, LOI conditions.
4. Financial model with low, base and high cases, including a contingency.
5. Facilities and design: the lower-school building, separation from older students, traffic plan.
6. Timeline with decision gates and tripwires, and what happens if each gate fails.
7. Re-enrolment plan and contract mechanics.
8. Staff plan.
9. Communication plan: who hears what, in what order, from whom.
10. Fallback: what you do if the sale can't close or the permit isn't granted. This should be a credible plan B, not an afterthought.

## Suggested sequence

- **Now to mid-November:** legal review (Halloran, planning, Ardelle, LOI), the financial model, trustee one-on-ones.
- **November:** share the options with the full board, brief staff, approach the Hallorans, the foundation and the neighbours.
- **Late November to December:** open parent forums and working groups, publish the Q&A, and decide on the timing of the vote and contracts.
- **December or early January:** vote (preferably before contracts go out), or a staged vote.
- **January 10 onward:** contracts go out with a clear message.
- **February 12 at the latest:** final board decision if you've used the two-stage approach.
