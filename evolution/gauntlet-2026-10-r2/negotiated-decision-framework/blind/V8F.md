**Recommendation:** Keep equal 55% shares, which are what the bylaws promise and need no ratification vote. Change two other parts of your draft: when the east lateral closes, and how trading works. Then give the orchard growers their protection through timing and a district-run water pool, not through priority. That way nobody has to win a weighted vote, and the orchard growers get what they need.

**Why the draft as written won't hold**

1. **The August 1 closure hits the orchards where it hurts.** Many of them sit on the east lateral, and their critical water is the post-harvest irrigation that sets next year's fruit buds. Cherries come off in June and July, so an August 1 closure cuts that water off. The orchard growers will read it as equal shares on paper and a cut to trees in practice. They will fight it, and they could also stop complying quietly. The 9% conveyance saving is real, but it is smaller than the cost of losing the orchard growers.
2. **The Dunmere agreement may be at risk.** The $40,000 a year is tied to east lateral seepage that recharges the town's wells. Check the 1997 agreement's wording before March 18. If it requires seepage or flows through a given period, an early closure could breach it or give the town a reason to reopen the terms. Also ask Dunmere whether the town wants recharge flows in late summer and fall. If it does, that gives you a second reason to keep the lateral running longer.
3. **Open trading at any price favors sellers and could split the board.** Orrin says he'll fallow about half his hay, so he'll have surplus water to sell. The orchard growers would be the likely buyers, at the moment of greatest scarcity. They will say the plan lets the board chair profit from their need. That doesn't have to be his intent, but the optics will drive the vote. Orrin should recuse himself from the trading rules, or at least disclose his position openly.
4. **Check who holds the votes.** The Bennings sold their 2,900 acres to Quillon Land Partners and lease it back. The weighted ratification vote counts acreage owned, so those acres now probably belong to Quillon, not to Neil. Neil's backing of Orrin at board meetings may not tell you how Quillon would vote. Find out how the bylaws count leased-back land, and talk to Quillon directly. A lessee-operator wants cheap, reliable water. A landowner may care more about land value, and an orchard-friendly plan could look different to them. Don't assume a bloc.

**The plan I'd take to the board**

- **Equal shares:** 55% of entitlement per farm. This keeps the row-crop growers on side, since it is what they say the bylaws require.
- **Delivery timing:** Let any member schedule part of their allocation for late season. Set aside a minimum post-harvest delivery window, for example September through mid-October, to protect bud-set water. This changes when water arrives, not how much each farm gets. It should therefore not count as a departure from equal shares, but confirm that reading with counsel before you rely on it.
- **East lateral:** Don't commit to August 1. Keep it open through the post-harvest window, or close it in stages once the orchard growers' late-season needs are met. Ask the district engineer what the conveyance loss actually costs per acre-foot at the later date. The saving may be a few points of loss, which the district pool can offset (see next).
- **District water pool in place of unlimited trading:** Anyone who fallows can sell their unused water to the district at a published price. The district resells it, first to permanent crops (orchards) for post-harvest needs, then to anyone else. Private trades stay allowed within a price band you set. This turns Orrin's fallowed hay into the orchards' water supply, and he gets paid for it. He benefits without looking like he's profiting from the orchards' squeeze. Your budget can handle a modest revolving fund, but have your treasurer price it.
- **Tree-loss protection as a concession from row crops:** If a member's tree block dies from a shortfall, replacement takes years. A small orchard-protection reserve funded by a per-acre assessment (cheap per member, since it is spread across 21,000 acres) shows the row-crop growers sharing the risk and makes the plan easier for orchard growers to accept.

**Who has to be won, and how**

| Party | What it needs | How it could block | What it gets / gives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orchard growers (east lateral) | Late-season water for bud set; cherries are permanent | Refuse the plan, push for a priority vote, or quietly overdraw | Gets protected post-harvest water and a longer-running lateral. Gives up claim to priority. |
| Orrin Castellane (chair) | Equal shares and open trading, which he can use because he's fallowing | Opposes any departure from equal shares, which could stall the board | Gets equal shares and a market for his fallowed water. Gives up unlimited pricing and should declare his interest. |
| Neil Benning / Quillon | Cheap, reliable water for tomato acres | Votes on a ratification, if one is ever needed. Quillon may not follow Neil. | Gets equal shares. Gives up nothing material. Confirm who actually votes the acres. |
| Other row-crop growers | Bylaw-equal shares; no one jumping the line | Challenge any priority scheme as a bylaw breach | Gets equal shares. Gives a modest levy and some pooled water going to trees. |
| Dunmere | East lateral seepage for its wells | Contest an early closure under the 1997 agreement | Keeps recharge through the season. Cost: a later closure. |

**Getting it through the board on March 18**

1. Before the meeting, meet Orrin one-on-one. Show him that equal shares are untouched, and that the pool pays him for fallowed water. Ask him to disclose his fallowing plans and sit out the pool-pricing vote.
2. Meet the orchard growers separately. Show them the guaranteed post-harvest window and the later lateral closure. Ask what minimum volume per acre protects bud set, and write that number into the plan. Ask them to stop pressing for priority in exchange.
3. Call Quillon before the meeting. Don't let the plan's fate depend on a land company hearing about it at the table.
4. Put the plan in writing with a single mid-season review (for example, July 1) to adjust if the allocation changes. Reports on the pool's use, prices and the orchard deliveries go to all members. A written review point makes it harder for anyone to reopen the deal later.
5. Add a simple compliance rule: metered deliveries on the east lateral, with a shared monthly report. That addresses quiet overdrawing from either side.

**What would change this recommendation**

If the 1997 agreement does not actually require late-season flows, and the engineer shows that the lateral's losses after August 1 cost much more than I assume, the early closure is worth reconsidering. In that case offer the orchard growers delivery through a lined pipe or a different route for the post-harvest window. The cheapest first check is to read the Dunmere agreement and ask the engineer for the loss figure per acre-foot for August through October. Both can be done this week, before the draft goes to the board.
