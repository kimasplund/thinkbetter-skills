Your draft has three problems that will decide whether it passes. It also leaves out a design that could win both camps.

**Problems to settle first**

1. **The August 1 east lateral closure cuts off the water the orchardists say matters most.** Cherries are harvested by about July, and the post-harvest irrigation that sets next year's fruit buds falls in August and September. Many of those orchards sit on the east lateral. A 9% conveyance saving is real, but closing the lateral on that date takes the orchardists' key water away. They will see it as a priority decision made through the back door, and equal shares won't change that view.
2. **The Dunmere agreement may limit your ability to close the lateral.** The 1997 agreement covers seepage from the east lateral that recharges the town's wells, and it pays $40,000 a year. I haven't seen the text. Have counsel read it before March 18 for any delivery, flow or season obligations. An early closure could put the payment at risk or breach the agreement. The unlined canal that loses water is also what recharges the town's wells, so the "loss" has a counterparty.
3. **Conflicts of interest and who actually votes.**
   - Orrin plans to fallow about half his hay, so he will have surplus water. Under open trading at whatever price members agree, he is the likely seller in a short year, and he chairs the board that sets the rules. That doesn't make his position wrong, but the orchardists will notice it. Orrin should disclose it, and ideally someone else should chair that agenda item.
   - Neil Benning's land now belongs to Quillon Land Partners. If a departure from equal shares needs an acreage-owned vote, Quillon holds those votes, not Neil. Find out who in Quillon's chain is deciding, and whether Neil has the standing to hold a board seat and speak for that land. Quillon is also a landlord with its own interests, such as lease terms and what happens to water rights in a short year. Talk to them directly.

**A package both sides can accept**

- **Keep equal base shares, so no ratification vote is needed.** Give every farm 55% of its normal entitlement. If the bylaws truly promise equal shares, this is the default that needs no acreage-weighted vote. That is a strong point for the row-crop growers.
- **Make the trading rules do the work for the orchardists.** Trading is where they can get protection without any priority rule:
  - Allow temporary trades (this season only) and leases. Don't allow permanent transfers.
  - Add a district-run clearing step or a posted price board. The district can also hold a small reserve pool, for example 3-5% of supply, funded from fallowed or voluntarily relinquished water. Orchardists can buy from that pool for permanent-crop needs.
  - Consider a price cap or a "permanent crop first right" to buy surplus. That is a modest departure from pure open trading, so check whether it triggers the ratification vote.
  - Let growers who fallow be paid by those who buy. That is the voluntary route to priority, and it is cheaper than a fight.
- **Move the lateral date to fit the crop, and verify the numbers.** Either keep the east lateral open through a post-harvest window of roughly August 1 to September 30 for deliveries to orchards only, or close it on the earlier date but guarantee orchard delivery by another route. Ask your engineers what the post-harvest window actually costs in extra losses. You may find it is less than 9% across the full season. Compare that cost with the risks of the Dunmere agreement and of orchard loss.
- **Phase the shortage.** Fix the 55% allocation now, and set a rule that reopens the plan in mid-season if the basin authority revises its forecast. That avoids re-arguing everything and shows both camps the rules will apply in both directions.
- **Add enforcement and trust.** Publish metering and trade records. Set a clear penalty for taking water outside your allocation. Use a neutral party, not the chair, to report on compliance. Compliance holds when each side can see the other is bound too.

**Process before March 18**

1. Get legal answers on the Dunmere agreement, the bylaws' wording on equal shares, and what counts as a departure that triggers ratification.
2. Meet the orchardists, the row-crop growers and Quillon separately. Ask each what the minimum is that they can live with, not what they want. Orchardists likely need post-harvest water and a way to buy it. Row-crop growers likely need no change to their base share and freedom to sell.
3. Circulate one written proposal before the meeting, with the lateral numbers and trade rules, so no one is voting on a surprise.
4. Ask for conflict disclosures from the board at the start of the meeting, including Orrin's fallowing plan and the Quillon lease.

**Assumptions and gaps.** I don't know the bylaw text, the Dunmere terms, or the real post-harvest volumes. I also don't know whether the 9% conveyance saving is for the whole season or only the August to September period. Each of these could change the details, but I think the structure holds: equal base shares, managed trading with an orchard safeguard, a lateral schedule that serves post-harvest needs, and open disclosure of conflicts.
