Your doubts are well placed. As it stands, the plan has four unresolved items that could each stop it, and a sequencing problem on top. I'd treat winning the vote as the last step. The first job is to find out whether the sale can legally and financially happen at all.

**1. Gates that could kill the plan (settle these before any trustee vote)**

- **The Halloran gift.** "For the education of the children of Brennock" on a plaque is more than sentiment. Depending on the deed or gift instrument, it could be a restriction, a reverter, or a charitable-purpose condition. A sale to a housing developer could need court approval (cy pres), attorney-general involvement, or the family's consent, or it could be barred outright. Get a title search and a written opinion from counsel now. Vellacott's letter of intent should be conditional on clear title. Also find out who the Halloran heirs are and what they think. Hearing about this from a lawyer first is much better than hearing it from a newspaper after the vote.
- **The Ardelle pledge.** $800k is still to come and is tied to the north-campus outdoor learning program. Closing that campus may breach the gift agreement, so the foundation could stop paying or ask for money back. Read the agreement, then talk to Ardelle before the board votes. They may be willing to redirect the gift to a south-campus program, but you need that in writing. Until then, treat the $800k as at risk in the financial model.
- **The 2004 planning conditions.** You say the enrolment ceiling is one you "never came close to". Please check the actual number. Today you have 640 students across both sites. If the ceiling is, say, 500 or 600 for the south campus, adding 230 students would break it. The consultant's 700 is a physical capacity figure and is not a legal one. The same conditions (traffic marshals, no events after 9pm) will also be reopened by any new building. Expect a planning-board process and the Sycamore Lane neighbours, who fought you once already. Ask a land-use attorney whether you need a modification, how long it takes, and how likely it is to succeed. If the answer is 12 to 18 months with real risk, fall 2028 may not be feasible.
- **The numbers.** $13.5M less transaction costs, less $8M for the building, leaves roughly $5M or less. That is about four years of a $1.1M deficit, and it doesn't fix the deficit. The closure saves operating costs, but it also puts the 92% return assumption at risk. Model what happens at 85% and at 80%, because lower-school families are the ones most likely to leave. Also ask whether the $8M is a firm quote and whether construction would finish before fall 2028.

**2. Fix the sequence**

The current calendar has the board voting on February 12, before you know how families will respond. Contracts go out January 10 and are due March 1. That is a problem in both directions. If you announce before contracts go out, you risk early attrition. If you vote before March 1, trustees decide without the one number that matters most. Options:

- Make the February 12 vote a conditional approval, or a vote to proceed to due diligence. The final sale approval would then come after legal clearances and early re-enrolment signals.
- Or push the final vote past March 1 and ask Vellacott to extend the LOI. A 4 to 6 week extension is a small price compared with a failed consolidation.
- Whatever you choose, tell families the plan, the timeline and what is still undecided before contracts go out. Surprising them after they have signed would be worse.

**3. Winning ten of fifteen votes**

You need two-thirds, so count trustees individually and don't talk about "the board" as one unit. Meet each trustee privately in the next few weeks. Sort them into supporters, persuadable and opposed, and find out what each persuadable trustee needs to see. Typical concerns will be the Halloran legacy, financial risk and personal reputation in the community. Give them:

- A single decision document with the legal opinions, the financial scenarios (base, downside, severe downside) and the alternatives.
- A real comparison with at least two alternatives, so the board sees that closure was chosen over other options. Examples are a partial lease of the north site while keeping it open, a phased approach, cost cuts or tuition changes, a merger or partnership with another school, or selling part of the six acres. If you can't show why these fall short, expect the opposition to bring them up.
- Defined trigger points, for example "if re-enrolment falls below X% by March 1, we pause."

If you can't find ten votes, that is useful information. A consolidation carried by 10-5 with a hostile minority is more fragile than the numbers suggest.

**4. Keeping parents and staff with you**

- **Lower-school parents.** They have two specific worries. For the commute, offer funded bus routes (nine km is manageable), extended before- and after-care, and a pickup point arrangement. For sharing a campus with teenagers, show the design: a separate entrance, a separate play area, separate timings, supervision plans, and an architect's drawing. Let parents sit on the design committee, because involvement tends to reduce opposition. A small group of north-campus parents who help plan the transition will be worth more than any newsletter.
- **Staff.** Say as soon as you can what you know about jobs. Even if the answer is "we expect X positions to be affected, and this is our process", that is better than silence. Commit to a clear process, internal priority for open roles at the south campus, and severance or support where roles end. Check your employment contracts and any legal notice requirements. Teachers who feel they heard it first from you will be much more likely to stay and reassure parents.
- **Neighbours.** Open a conversation with Sycamore Lane early, before the planning application. Offer to keep or strengthen the old conditions. Winning them over, or at least neutralising them, matters as much as winning the board.
- **Community and the Halloran legacy.** Give the north site a respectful story, such as a commemorated space on the south campus, a plaque moved with the school, or a scholarship in the family's name. Better still, ask the family what would honour the gift. If you can't, don't present the plaque as a minor detail.

**5. What the plan document should include**

1. Legal clearance: title and deed opinion, Halloran position, Ardelle agreement, planning-conditions analysis, and the timeline for each.
2. Financial model: sale net proceeds, building cost with contingency, operating savings, and re-enrolment scenarios (92%, 85%, 80%) with the effect on the deficit and reserves.
3. Alternatives considered and why each was rejected or kept as a fallback.
4. Decision calendar: due diligence, family communication, January 10 contracts, board vote, March 1 data, planning submission, construction, fall 2028.
5. Stakeholder plan: trustees, parents, staff, neighbours, donors, Halloran family.
6. Transition commitments: transport, care, campus design, staffing process.
7. Triggers and fallbacks: what you will check, when, and what happens if a gate fails (extend LOI, pause, scale back).
8. Risk register with owners.

**Assumptions I made.** I don't know the deed wording, the exact Ardelle terms or the planning ceiling, so I've treated each as possibly binding. If all three turn out to be clear, the plan is much stronger. If any one is a blocker, you'll want to know now.

**Bottom line.** Don't try to win the vote first. Spend the next few weeks finding out whether the plan is allowed and affordable, and then bring trustees a package that shows you've tested it. If it passes those checks, you will have a strong case. If it doesn't, you'll have avoided a very costly mistake. Your doubts are a good instinct, and trustees will probably share them.
