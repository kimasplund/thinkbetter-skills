The short version: keep equal shares as the legal base, so you never need the acreage-weighted ratification vote. Then fix the two things in your draft that will actually break it, the August 1 east lateral closure and trading at "whatever price they agree." Give the orchard growers their priority through a trading and timing mechanism rather than a priority tier.

**Problems in the draft to fix before March 18**

1. **The August 1 closure hits the orchards where it hurts most.** The cherry growers' strongest argument is post-harvest water for next year's fruit buds. A lateral that many of them sit on shuts just as that water matters. A 9% conveyance saving will not persuade anyone who is losing the water that sets next year's crop. If the closure stays as drafted, expect the orchard growers to treat the whole plan as aimed at them, and they are the faction most likely to fight it or ignore it.
2. **Check the Dunmere agreement before you propose any closure.** The 1997 agreement covers seepage from the east lateral that recharges the town's wells, and it pays $40,000 a year. I haven't seen its terms. Find out whether it requires the lateral to carry water in particular months, whether an early shutdown is a breach, and whether the payment can be reduced. Dunmere is also a possible ally. Its wells benefit if the lateral runs a short, scheduled post-harvest flow, and it may be able to help with the 9% loss issue.
3. **Unlimited-price trading favors sellers, and the board chair is probably one.** Orrin has said he'll fallow about half his hay anyway. He would have a lot of water to sell and no need to use it, so open pricing could make him the biggest beneficiary of a shortage. I'm not saying he's acting in bad faith. But the orchard growers will see it, and they will be the buyers in a scarce year. An unregulated market is the part of your draft most likely to make "equal shares" look like a cover for a transfer from trees to hay.
4. **Check who votes, and whose position is whose.** Neil Benning has backed Orrin at every meeting, but he sold his 2,900 acres to Quillon Land Partners and leases it back. If any plan departs from equal shares, the acreage-weighted vote counts acreage owned, and Quillon now owns that land. So Quillon, not Neil, may hold that vote, and its interest is probably the land's value and the lease economics, not Neil's tomatoes. Also find out who holds the water entitlement on that land, whether it passed with the sale, and who is the board's member for it. Treat Quillon as a stakeholder you haven't met.

**What I would propose**

*Keep a single shared shortage plan. Every farm gets 55% of its normal entitlement, with no priority tiers.* That is what the bylaws promise and it is what the row-crop growers will defend. It also avoids a contested ownership-weighted ratification vote that you might lose. Then make three additions:

1. **Timing, not volume, as the orchard accommodation.** Let each member schedule their 55% across the season. An orchard grower can shift more of their share to post-harvest deliveries (the weeks after harvest, which the growers can name) and take less in midsummer. That meets the orchards' real need, which is water at the right time, without taking a gallon from anyone else. Ask a local extension agronomist or the orchard growers' consultant to put numbers on the post-harvest need, so the board is debating an agreed figure and not a claim.
2. **A district-run trading pool with guardrails, in place of free bilateral trading.** Keep trading, since the row-crop growers and Orrin want it and it is the fair way to move water to its highest-value use. But post a floor and a ceiling, or a reference price, for the season. Require trades to be logged with the district. Cap how much one member can buy so no one hoards. Give a first-call window to members with permanent crops before the general market opens. Because you're paying nothing for the pool, this costs little. Voluntary fallowing becomes an option for growers who would rather sell than irrigate marginal ground, and the orchards get a legitimate way to buy the extra water they say they need.
3. **Replace the fixed August 1 closure with a conditional one.** Keep the east lateral open on a published schedule through the post-harvest window, then close it once those deliveries are done, or run it in short scheduled pulses. You give up part of the 9% savings, so quantify how much, and ask which months the loss is concentrated in. If most of the saving comes from the late-season weeks anyway, the orchards may be giving up very little. Also work out whether the closure saving is 9% of east lateral flow or 9% of district conveyance. Those are very different numbers, and the draft doesn't say.

**Why this should hold**

- The orchard growers get what they actually need (water after harvest, plus a way to buy more), and no one has to concede a principle.
- The row-crop growers keep equal shares, the bylaw language, and open trading in a form they can use. The guardrails cost them little unless they were planning to sell at a peak price.
- Orrin and Neil (or Quillon) get a plan that matches the bylaws. The guardrails also protect Orrin from the appearance of profiting from his chair. Suggest that he and any board member who expects to sell or buy water disclose that interest and, ideally, recuse from the vote on the trading rules. That protects the plan's legitimacy more than it costs him.
- Dunmere keeps its payment and its seepage, as long as the agreement is respected.

**How to get it adopted**

- Before the meeting, talk separately with the chair, a lead orchard grower, and one or two row-crop growers who are not on the board. Ask each one what outcome they could not accept, not what they want. Do the same with Quillon's representative if Quillon holds the entitlement.
- Ask the orchard growers to bring their numbers on post-harvest need to the board before March 18, so the schedule is built on their data and they have helped write it.
- Present the plan as the board's, not yours. Have the orchard and row-crop sides each propose one element. Growers keep to a plan they helped draft.
- Make adoption conditional on a mid-season review, for example after harvest or when the basin authority updates its allocation. If the allocation turns out better or worse than 55%, the plan scales equally. Write down now what happens at 45% and at 70%, so no one reopens the whole plan when the number moves.
- Put the rules for disputes in writing: who rules on a contested trade, and what happens to a member who exceeds their allocation. Plans like this usually fail from quiet non-compliance, not open opposition, so metering and a visible monthly report on use matter as much as the vote.

**Things I have assumed and you should confirm**

- That "55% of normal" is measured against the same entitlement base for every farm, and that the bylaws really do preclude any tiering without the weighted vote.
- That post-harvest water is the orchards' real constraint. If it is, the timing fix is enough. If they also need more total water, the trading pool is the only legitimate way to get it, and that needs to be said plainly.
- That the Dunmere agreement allows a scheduled, shortened flow. If it doesn't, the August 1 date may not be available to you at all.

If the orchard growers still insist on a formal priority tier after seeing this, that would need the ratification vote. Count the acreage-owned votes first (including Quillon's) before you put it to the board, because you should know whether it can pass before the argument starts.
