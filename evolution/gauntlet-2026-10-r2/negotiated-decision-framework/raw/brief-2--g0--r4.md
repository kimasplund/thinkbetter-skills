**Short answer:** Don't bring the draft to the board as written. Change four things in it, and settle the process questions before March 18. The draft has three weak points: the east lateral closure, unrestricted trading, and the conflicts on the board.

**1. The east lateral closure is the biggest risk**
- The orchards sit mostly on the east lateral, and the water they need most comes after harvest. An August 1 closure cuts them off at the worst time. Cherries are usually harvested by mid-July, so the post-harvest water for bud set falls right after your cutoff. Closing the canal to save about 9% of conveyance losses works directly against the group you most need to win over.
- The 1997 Dunmere agreement covers seepage from that lateral, and it brings in $40,000 a year. Check it before you propose any closure. I'm assuming it may require delivery or flow, or give the town a claim if flows stop. If it does, an August 1 shutdown could put the payment and the town's well recharge at risk. Have counsel read it first.
- Alternatives:
  - Keep the lateral open at a reduced schedule: pulse or rotation deliveries after harvest, with a firm end date set by when the buds are set rather than by the calendar.
  - Close it only after a post-harvest irrigation window, for example mid-September.
  - Work out what the 9% saving is worth in acre-feet. Offer to pay for the extra losses from a shared pool, so the whole district carries that cost and not just the east-lateral growers.

**2. Keep equal shares as the base, and add a limited orchard accommodation**
- Equal 55% shares match the bylaws and avoid a weighted ratification vote. Any departure from equal shares needs a member vote weighted by acreage owned, which would be hard to win and would fuel a fight.
- You can protect the trees without leaving equal shares. Let each farm choose when to use its 55%, with a **seasonal timing option** for orchard blocks. They could bank part of their share for post-harvest use, with the water scheduled accordingly.
- Give orchards a **voluntary, compensated priority through trading** and not a formal priority. Row-crop growers who plan to fallow can lease water to orchards at agreed or capped prices. Orchards get the water, and row-crop growers get paid for something they would otherwise lose.
- If the board wants a true priority tier, define it narrowly: permanent tree crops, post-harvest only, a capped volume. That will probably need the weighted vote, so ask counsel whether it does.

**3. Add rules to the trading provision**
"Any price they agree" has risks in a shortage:
- Price spikes, with orchards desperate and large holders able to name a price.
- Water leaving the district or concentrating in a few hands, which would undermine the equal-share principle.
- Conveyance losses on trades across the east lateral that nobody has accounted for.

Suggested guardrails:
- Trades are allowed only within the district, are reported to the district, and show the actual delivery path.
- Consider a price ceiling or a published reference price, plus a **per-farm cap on sales** (for example, no one can sell more than a fixed percentage of their share).
- Trades must account for conveyance losses.
- Tell the board whether and how to handle a fallowing program. A district-run fallowing payment could be simpler than open trading and give you better control.

**4. Handle conflicts and process before the vote**
- **Orrin Castellane** chairs the board and plans to fallow about half his 3,400 acres of alfalfa. Open trading would let him sell the water he doesn't use, so he benefits directly from the trading rules. That doesn't make him wrong, but it's a conflict, and the orchard growers will notice. Have the board adopt a disclosure-and-recusal approach, or at least a written disclosure, and consider having someone else chair the vote on the trading provision.
- **Neil Benning** sold his 2,900 acres to Quillon Land Partners and leases them back. Check two things: whether he is still a qualified member and board member under the bylaws, and who holds the water entitlement and the acreage-owned vote. Quillon may be the voter on any ratification, which means the real constituency may be an outside owner who has its own incentives. Find out what Quillon wants before you count on Neil's support or the weighted vote.
- Together, Orrin's and Neil's acreage is about 6,300 of 21,000 acres, so they hold a large block of any acreage-weighted vote. That is another reason to keep the plan close to equal shares and avoid needing a ratification vote at all.

**5. Making the deal stick**
- Hold one pre-meeting with the orchard growers and one with the row-crop growers, and bring each side the same data: reservoir level, the 55% allocation, an acre-foot schedule by crop and month, and what the east lateral closure would save and cost.
- Write the plan so each side gets something concrete it can point to: the orchards get post-harvest water, and the row-crop growers keep equal shares and market access.
- Build in a review trigger, for example a mid-season check in June and again in early August, with the lateral date and any trading caps open to adjustment if the allocation or the weather changes. A deal with a built-in checkpoint is easier to accept than a fixed one.
- Include a simple dispute process and a reporting requirement, so a grower who thinks the rules are being bent has somewhere to go besides the board meeting.

**What I'd bring on March 18**
1. 55% equal shares for every farm, as the base.
2. Growers may time their delivery, with a post-harvest window protected for tree crops.
3. Trading allowed with guardrails: reporting, loss accounting, and a sale cap.
4. East lateral kept open through an agreed post-harvest period, or closed on a date tied to the Dunmere agreement and the buds, not August 1.
5. Conflict disclosure for Orrin and clarity on Neil and Quillon.
6. A June and August review.

I also assumed cherry harvest finishes before August 1 and that the 9% loss figure is for the whole district. If those are wrong, the numbers and dates above need adjusting.
