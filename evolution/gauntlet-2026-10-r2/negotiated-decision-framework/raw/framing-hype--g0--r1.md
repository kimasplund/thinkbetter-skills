Consolidating onto one campus is a legitimate option for a school with an 11% enrolment decline and a $1.1M deficit. But several things in your message could stop the plan or sink the vote, and I'd deal with them before you worry about winning. I'd change the question from "how do I win this vote" to "what has to be true by February 12 for the board to vote yes with its eyes open."

**Four things to resolve first**

1. **The Halloran gift.** The 1958 gift was made "for the education of the children of Brennock," and the plaque says so. I don't know if the deed or gift instrument has a restriction, a reverter, or a right of return. I also don't know if your state requires court or attorney-general approval to sell charitable assets. If either applies, the Vellacott letter of intent is worth much less than it looks, and a sale could end in litigation or a public fight with the Halloran family and the town. Get counsel to read the original deed and gift documents now. Contact the family and the AG's office early, before they hear about it from someone else.

2. **The 2004 enrolment ceiling.** "700 students" is your consultant's physical-capacity figure. The planning board's conditions are a separate legal limit, and you only said the ceiling is one you've "never come close to." That is not the same as being above 640. Find the actual number. If it is below 640 plus any growth, or if adding a lower-school building needs a new approval, you'd be going back to the Sycamore Lane neighbours who fought you for years. Even if the ceiling is fine, the traffic marshal and 9pm conditions will matter more once 640 students share one site, and the neighbours will notice. Ask counsel and the consultant what approvals the new building and the extra traffic need, how long they take, and whether 2028 is realistic.

3. **The Ardelle pledge.** The $2M pledge is for a north-campus outdoor learning program, and $800k is still unpaid. If you close that campus, the foundation may be entitled to stop paying, ask for money back, or require a conversation about the purpose. Don't count the $800k as secure until you've talked to them. Tell them before the vote, not after, and ask whether they would redirect the gift to the south campus. Also, "fundraising is healthy" is true only if the donors who gave for the north campus stay engaged. Closing it may cost you other gifts too.

4. **The numbers.** $13.5M less an $8M building leaves about $5.5M before transaction costs, taxes if any, and cost overruns. That helps, but a $1.1M annual deficit would eat $5.5M in about five years if nothing else changes. Consolidation saves operating costs, but you haven't shown me the amount, and it is partly offset by losing north-campus families. Build a model with three cases: your 92% re-enrolment, and two worse ones, say 85% and 80%, with lower-school families hit hardest. Every point of attrition at an average tuition of roughly $30k (about $19M over 640 students) costs about $190k a year per 1% of enrolment, so a drop from 92% to 85% would cost more than the deficit you are trying to fix. Show the board that the plan still works in the downside case, or what you'd do if it doesn't.

**Timing problem**

Re-enrolment contracts go out January 10 and are due March 1, but the board votes February 12. That means families sign (or don't) without knowing whether the north campus will close, or they sign after the vote with very little time. Either way, your 92% assumption is weak. Options: send contracts with an honest note that a decision is pending, move the vote earlier, or push the vote after the contract deadline with a firm closing date. I'd lean toward not voting before you have evidence about how families will react, perhaps a short survey or a non-binding intent-to-return form for lower-school parents. Also check whether the LOI has an expiry date or exclusivity clause that forces the schedule. A letter of intent is usually non-binding, so you probably have more room to ask Vellacott for time than you think.

**The vote**

You need ten of 15, not a simple majority. That means you need at least some trustees who are doubtful today. Talk with each trustee one-on-one in the next few weeks, and find out what each of them would need to see. Expect questions about the deed, the neighbours, the donors, and the downside case. If you can answer them in writing with counsel's opinion and the three-case model, you'll earn votes. If you can't, a postponed vote is better than a failed one, or a passed vote followed by an injunction. Consider also whether any trustee has a link to Vellacott or the Hallorans, and handle that openly.

Think about alternatives the board will ask about, and be ready to compare them honestly: keep both campuses and cut costs, sell part of the north site, lease it instead of selling, or consolidate in stages. Your preferred plan will look stronger if the board sees you examined these.

**Keeping parents and staff with you**

- **Lower-school parents.** Their worries are real: the commute and little kids sharing space with teenagers. Don't treat these as obstacles to manage. Put concrete answers in the plan, such as a separate lower-school entrance, play area and schedule, a distinct building (which you already propose), transport or bus options, and before- and after-care. Show them design drawings and let a small parent group give input.
- **Staff.** Say early what will happen to jobs. If you can promise that north-campus teachers are offered roles at the south campus, say so, with numbers. If you can't, say that too and give a date by which they'll know. Staff who hear nothing assume the worst, and they talk to parents.
- **Community.** The Hallorans, neighbours, alumni and Brennock residents will all care about the north site. Ask what Vellacott plans to build there. Consider whether some of the land could stay in public or educational use, or a plaque or park could honour the gift. That is cheap compared with a hostile press story.
- **Tone.** Your message is full of excitement, and that is understandable, but families and trustees will respond better to candour about the costs and risks than to "game-changer." Present it as a hard choice you've thought through.

**What the plan should include**

1. Legal clearance: the Halloran deed and any court or AG approval, the 2004 planning conditions and what new approvals are needed, and the Vellacott LOI terms and expiry.
2. A financial model with base, downside and severe cases covering sale proceeds after costs, building cost with contingency, operating savings, attrition, and the Ardelle pledge.
3. The Ardelle and other donors' positions, in writing.
4. A construction and approvals timeline for fall 2028, with a fallback if the building isn't ready (temporary classrooms, or a delayed closure).
5. A lower-school transition plan: location, safety, transport, care, and parent involvement.
6. A staffing plan with dates.
7. A communications calendar tied to January 10, February 12 and March 1, with a decision on what families are told when.
8. A comparison with the alternatives.
9. Clear decision criteria for the board: what would make you recommend proceeding, and what would make you stop or delay.

If those answers come back good (deed clear, ceiling fine, Ardelle on board, plan survives the downside case), you'll have a strong case and a much better chance of getting ten votes. If one of them comes back bad, you will have found out in time to change the plan instead of discovering it after the vote.

I made some assumptions: I used your figures as stated, averaged tuition over the whole $19M budget (some of it is probably not tuition), and I don't know your state's law on charitable assets, so please confirm those points with counsel.
