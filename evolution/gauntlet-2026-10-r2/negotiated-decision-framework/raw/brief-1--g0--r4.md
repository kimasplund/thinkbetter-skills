Short version: don't try to "win" the vote with persuasion alone. Four facts in your own message could stop the sale or sink the plan after the vote, and you need to resolve them before February 12. Once they are resolved, the stakeholder work gets much easier.

## 1. Four items to settle before you count votes

**a) The Halloran gift.** The plaque says the land was given "for the education of the children of Brennock." I'm assuming you haven't had counsel read the original deed or gift instrument. That document might contain a reverter, a restriction, or a charitable-trust purpose. Selling to a homebuilder could breach it. The family, the state attorney general, or a court could then challenge the sale. Vellacott's letter of intent is not a closing, and a title company may refuse to insure.
- Get a written legal opinion on the deed, the gift history, and whether you need family consent, a release, attorney general notice, or court approval (cy pres).
- Ask counsel what the school's duty is to the Halloran heirs, if any. Contact them early and privately rather than letting them find out from the press.
- Trustees will ask about this first. Without an answer, some of the ten votes won't be there.

**b) The 2004 Sycamore Lane conditions.** You describe an "enrolment ceiling we've never come close to." Consolidation is exactly what brings you close to it.
- Find the actual number. Your consultant says the campus can *hold* 700, but the permit may cap enrolment lower than that, and a new $8M building will almost certainly need new planning approval anyway.
- Moving 230 more students means more drop-off traffic. The approval history was a long fight, and the same neighbours will show up.
- Get a land-use attorney's view on whether the building triggers a reopening of the 2004 conditions. Plan the timeline and the cost of that process. A 2028 opening that depends on an approval you haven't sought is a risk to the whole financial case.
- Talk to the Sycamore Lane neighbours before the application, not after. Offer improvements such as traffic management, a separate lower-school entrance, and event limits you keep.

**c) The Ardelle pledge.** $800k is still to come, and it is tied to the north-campus outdoor learning program. Closing that campus could breach the gift terms.
- Read the pledge agreement and talk to the Foundation *before* the vote. Ask whether the money can follow the program to a redesigned outdoor space at the south campus.
- If it can't move, treat the $800k as at risk in your model. Don't let the Foundation hear about this from a parent.

**d) Sequence.** Re-enrolment contracts go out January 10 and are due March 1, but the board doesn't vote until February 12. Families will be deciding whether to re-enrol before they know whether the school will be closing their campus. That is a problem in both directions.
- If you announce the plan before contracts go out, you risk early withdrawals at the worst moment. Lower-school families are already upset.
- If you don't, families will feel they signed without knowing. You also risk the story leaking mid-window.
- My suggestion is to announce the *proposal* (not a decision) before January 10, with a fixed decision date and a clear promise about what families can expect. Consider a short, fee-free withdrawal right if the board approves the closure. That reduces fear-driven early exits and shows good faith.
- Test your 92% assumption now. Ask what happens to the budget at 88% and at 85%. Lower-school families may leave at a higher rate than the average, and you've said they're the angriest group. A 5-point shortfall on $19M is real money and could erase the benefit of the sale.

## 2. Check the money

- $13.5M sale price less transaction costs (and possibly taxes, depending on your status) leaves less than $13.5M. Subtract the $8M building and any contingency (construction overruns, approval costs, temporary space, moving). Be explicit about what's left and how it covers a $1.1M annual deficit. A one-time sale doesn't fix a structural deficit unless operating costs genuinely fall.
- Show the board the operating savings from closing one campus, the staffing plan, and enrolment scenarios in a single table.
- Check the Vellacott letter of intent for conditions, such as rezoning, due-diligence outs, and a closing date. Are they binding, and what happens if they walk or lower the price? Consider whether a lease, a partial sale, or a deed restriction on use is something Vellacott or others would accept.
- Have a "Plan B" ready, because trustees will ask. That might be a smaller sale, shared-use arrangements, or merging the lower grades into the south campus without selling right away.

## 3. Winning ten of fifteen

You need two-thirds, so a bare majority isn't enough. You can't win this by working the room in the last week.
- **Count now.** Talk one on one with every trustee. Sort them into committed, leaning, and worried. For each worried trustee, find the actual concern (legal exposure, legacy, the numbers, parents' reaction) and answer *that*.
- **Give trustees things they can defend.** An independent legal opinion, an independent financial review of the model, and a clear risk register. Trustees have fiduciary exposure, and a vote for a sale that later gets challenged is a personal worry for them.
- **Offer a real alternatives analysis.** Show what you looked at (stay and cut, partial sale, lease, merger, phased closure) and why this option beats them. Trustees who feel the decision is already made will resist, even if they agree with you.
- **Name the legacy question directly.** The Halloran plaque is a symbol, and some trustees will feel the sale betrays it. Consider whether proceeds can honour it: a named lower-school building on the south campus, a Halloran scholarship fund for Brennock children, and a commemorative marker or garden. Do this only if counsel confirms it is consistent with the gift, and involve the family early.
- **Keep a path if you fall short.** If you are at eight or nine votes in January, decide in advance whether you delay the vote, amend the proposal, or withdraw it. Don't discover this on February 12.

## 4. Parents

Your lower-school parents have two distinct concerns, and each needs its own answer.
- **Commute.** Offer concrete things: bus routes or a shuttle, extended before- and after-care, staggered start times, and transport support for families who most need it. Publish the plans with dates and costs.
- **Little kids with teenagers.** Describe the physical design before they ask: a separate lower-school wing or building with its own entrance, separate play areas, separate lunch and recess times, and supervised transitions. Let a parent group review the plans, and consider a parent advisory group with real input on the design.
- **Process.** Hold listening sessions *before* the decision, with a published summary of what you heard and what you changed in response. Say plainly which parts are open (design, transport, transition support) and which aren't (the financial case). People forgive a hard decision more easily than a feeling of being managed.
- Protect the lower school's identity: keep the same head of lower school, the same teachers where possible, and visible traditions.
- Consider a guarantee for current north-campus families, such as tuition held flat for a year or priority in the transition planning. That costs money, so model it.

## 5. Staff

- Say early and clearly what the plan means for jobs. If you expect no layoffs, say so and commit to it only if it's true. If some roles will be consolidated, say how many, when, and how decisions will be made.
- Offer redeployment first, then retention incentives, then voluntary separation, with fair severance and outplacement support as a last resort. Follow your employment contract obligations and get HR and legal input.
- Involve teachers in designing the new lower-school building and the transition. They are your best source of credibility with parents.
- Keep a stable channel for questions, such as a weekly FAQ and open staff meetings, so rumours don't fill the gap.

## 6. What the plan should include

1. **Decision gates** with dates: legal opinions (Halloran, planning), Ardelle conversation, financial review, trustee one-on-ones, announcement of the proposal, listening sessions, board vote.
2. **Legal workstream:** gift and deed, planning conditions, LOI terms, charitable approvals.
3. **Financial model:** sources and uses, enrolment scenarios (92%, 88%, 85%), operating savings, deficit path, sensitivity to the Ardelle money and the planning approval.
4. **Communications plan:** one source of truth, a FAQ, a timeline, and who tells whom first (trustees, then staff, then families, then the neighbours and the wider community).
5. **Family retention plan:** transport, building design, transition support, and a re-enrolment strategy with early check-ins on at-risk families.
6. **Staff plan:** job commitments, redeployment, involvement in design.
7. **Neighbour and community plan:** Sycamore Lane, the Halloran family, the town, and the press.
8. **Risk register and fallback options:** what you will do if the vote fails, the sale falls through, the approval is denied, or re-enrolment comes in at 85%.
9. **A contingency for timing:** the vote could be a decision to proceed *subject to* conditions (legal clearance, planning path, minimum re-enrolment), which can win more votes than an unconditional sale.
