**Short answer:** Anya's note doesn't show that recurring revenue will be higher in a year, and the evidence she cites points the other way. The change only wins if at least 90% of monthly clinics move to annual. Her 95% figure isn't supported by the data she gives.

**The numbers (my assumptions: new sign-ups are the same with or without the change, and "recurring revenue" means annualised recurring revenue, ARR)**

Today:
- Monthly: 820 x £90 x 12 = £885,600
- Annual: 330 x £900 = £297,000
- Total: £1,182,600

A monthly clinic pays £1,080 a year, and annual costs £900. Every clinic that switches gives up £180 a year, about 17%.

One year out, without the change:
- Monthly: 820 x 75% = 615 clinics, so £664,200
- Annual: 330 x 94% = 310 clinics, so £279,180
- Total: about £943,400

One year out, with the change, if a share s of monthly clinics switch:
- Switchers: 820 x s x £900. They have just paid for a full annual term, so none of them has had a chance to churn again within the year.
- Existing annual clinics: £279,180, as before.

Break-even is s = 0.75 x 1,080 / 900 = **90%**. Each percentage point of switch rate is worth about £7,400 of ARR.

| Share of monthly clinics who switch | ARR vs. no change after 12 months |
|---|---|
| 95% (Anya) | +£36,900 (+3.9%) |
| 90% | £0 |
| 80% | -£73,800 |
| 70% | -£147,600 |
| 59% (her test result) | -£229,600 (-24%) |

**Problems with the reasoning**

1. **"Churn across the base falls to 6%" is a cause-and-effect leap.** Annual clinics churn less partly because loyal clinics choose annual. That is selection. Clinics forced onto annual aren't the same population, and their first renewal on a plan they didn't choose could churn much more than 6%. Even if 6% held, it only matters after the first annual term, which falls outside the board's 12-month window.

2. **The 95% switch rate comes from the wrong evidence.** The test was on new prospects. 212 of 360 is **59%**, not 95%, and it measures a different decision. Those prospects were deciding whether to buy at all, and they had no existing monthly relationship or alternative. Existing clinics might switch more readily or less readily. But even taken at face value, the test implies a clear loss, not a gain. I don't see any basis in the note for "at least 95%".

3. **"Clinics that leave were never committed, so we'd have lost them anyway" is wrong on your own figures.** Monthly clinics churn at 25%, so about 75% would have stayed and kept paying £1,080. Losing them is a real loss. The only clinics you'd have lost anyway are the ones in that 25%. The right comparison is the 90% break-even above.

4. **Cash and fees aren't recurring revenue.** Cash brought forward helps liquidity, and lower card fees help margin. Neither raises ARR, so they don't answer the board's question. They are fair secondary benefits, but they should be quantified separately.

5. **The conclusion answers a different question.** "Annual clinics are worth more over their lifetime" may be true at steady state. At 6% churn, the expected life is about 17 years, compared with about 4 years at 25%. But that is a long-run claim, partly confounded by selection (point 1). The board asked about revenue in 12 months, and in that window the 17% price cut hits immediately.

**What I'd say on Tuesday**

"Recurring revenue is higher after a year only if at least 90% of monthly clinics move to annual. The one data point we have, from new sign-ups, suggests about 59%, which would cut ARR by roughly £230k. The long-term loyalty benefit is plausible but unproven for clinics we push onto annual."

**Before you present, I'd suggest**
- Pull the history of monthly clinics who were offered annual, and any past upgrades. That is a better guide than the new-sign-up test.
- Run a small pilot on one renewal cohort, and track both the switch rate and the first-renewal churn of switchers.
- Consider softer options: an annual discount for monthly clinics who opt in, a more expensive monthly plan, or an annual-only rule for new sign-ups only. These keep the churn benefit without betting the base on a 90% switch rate.
- Work out how far the churn of forced switchers could rise before the long-run case disappears.
