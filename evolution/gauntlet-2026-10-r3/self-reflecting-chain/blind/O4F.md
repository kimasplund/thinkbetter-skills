**Short answer:** Anya's reasoning does not support the claim. On the evidence in her note, you would most likely have less recurring revenue a year from now, not more. The change only wins if about 90% or more of monthly clinics end up on annual. Her own test points to roughly 59%.

## The numbers

The plans are priced differently per year. A monthly clinic pays £90 x 12 = £1,080 a year, and an annual clinic pays £900. Every switcher gives up £180 a year, about 17%.

Today's recurring revenue (annualised):
- Monthly: 820 x £1,080 = £885,600
- Annual: 330 x £900 = £297,000
- Total: £1,182,600

**A year from now, without the change** (using your three-year churn rates, and assuming new sign-ups are the same either way):
- Monthly: 820 x 75% = 615 clinics = £664,200
- Annual: 330 x 94% = 310 clinics = £279,180
- Total: about £943,400

**A year from now, with the change**, if a share *s* of today's 820 monthly clinics are on annual by then:
- Monthly clinics: none left
- Annual: 820 x s x £900 = £738,000 x s, plus the existing annual clinics' £279,180

The break-even is £738,000 x s = £664,200, so **s = 90%**. Above that, the change wins. Below it, it loses.

| Share of monthly clinics ending on annual | Revenue in a year | vs. no change |
|---|---|---|
| 59% (what the test showed) | about £713,800 | -£229,600 (-24%) |
| 80% | about £869,600 | -£73,800 (-8%) |
| 90% | about £943,400 | break-even |
| 95% (Anya's expectation) | about £980,300 | +£36,900 (+4%) |

Even if her 95% is right, the gain is small. A modest miss turns it into a loss. Note that 90% is a high bar: without any change, only 75% of monthly clinics are still customers after a year.

## Where the note goes wrong

1. **The 95% is not supported by the test.** 212 of 360 is 59%, not 95%. There are also three reasons the real figure for existing clinics could be lower. The test was on new sign-ups, who are choosing a product for the first time and have no £90 plan to compare against. Existing clinics are being asked to pay £900 in one go instead of £90 a month, which is a cash-flow shock for a small practice. And a forced change tends to prompt people to review their alternatives. Anya is also expecting only 5% to be lost, when 25% of monthly clinics leave in a normal year anyway.

2. **"Churn falls to 6%" is a selection effect.** Annual clinics churn at 6% partly because the clinics that choose to prepay a year are the ones already committed. Moving a reluctant monthly clinic onto annual is unlikely to give it a 6% churn rate. The 6% belongs to a self-selected group and may not transfer. Even so, this barely matters for a one-year view, because a clinic that switches during the year has paid upfront and has not yet reached a renewal point.

3. **"Clinics that leave were never committed" is wrong on the facts.** Most monthly clinics (75%) stay a full year at £1,080. A clinic that leaves because of the change is revenue you would otherwise have kept. Only the 25% who would have left anyway are a free loss. Everything beyond that is a real cost.

4. **Cash forward is not recurring revenue.** Annual billing improves cash and cuts card fees, which are real benefits. But the board asked about recurring revenue, and prepayment does not change that. Treat these as secondary points, not part of the answer.

5. **The conclusion answers a different question.** "Annual clinics are worth more over their lifetime" is probably true. A rough lifetime value is £900 / 6% = £15,000 for annual against £1,080 / 25% = £4,320 for monthly, before discounting. But that compares clinics that chose each plan. It says nothing about what happens when you force the choice, lose a share of the clinics, and cut the price per clinic by £180. The board asked about a year from now.

## Also worth noting

- **New sign-ups.** If annual-only quotes convert at about 59%, you may be losing sign-ups that a monthly option would have won. I could not size this, and it is a further downside to the figures above.
- **Longer horizon.** If switched clinics really do retain at close to 6%, the picture improves in year two and beyond, because they stop churning at 25%. That is an honest argument for the change, but it is a different case from the one in the note, and it depends on the retention assumption in point 2.
- **Timing.** I assumed every monthly clinic reaches its renewal decision within the year. If some are allowed to stay on monthly until later, the effect lands partly outside the year.

## What I would say on Tuesday

On current evidence, annual-only is more likely to reduce recurring revenue at the one-year mark than increase it. It needs at least 90% of monthly clinics to move to annual and stay, and the only data we have suggests about 59%.

If you want to keep the idea alive, it is worth testing before committing. Offer existing monthly clinics a switch at the current annual price, or a smaller discount, and see how many take it. You could also keep a monthly option at a higher price. Either would give the board a real take-up number instead of an assumption.

I used only the figures in your message. If actual renewal dates, new-sign-up volumes or the current gap between plans differ from what I assumed, the break-even moves, but the direction of the conclusion is unlikely to change.
