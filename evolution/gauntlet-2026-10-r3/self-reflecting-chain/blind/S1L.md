I wouldn't send this to the board as it stands. You asked whether the island will be at least as well served through the winter. On the paper's own facts the answer is no, and the paper never addresses it.

**The decisive problem: the winter refit**

Every vessel on the route must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. Today you refit one boat at a time, so the other keeps sailing and the island never goes without a service. With only the Calloway, the refit takes out your whole fleet for about 21 days. Your contract says the island never goes more than 24 hours without a sailing, except in storms. A scheduled refit is not a storm, so you would breach the core condition of the contract you are renewing in January. This would happen every winter, and the refit is statutory, so you can't skip or defer it.

A single ship has the same exposure to any breakdown outside the refit. Today a fault on the Linnet leaves the Tern running. With the Calloway, a fault leaves nothing running.

The paper's weather argument doesn't help here. The 31 versus 9 cancellation days concern storms, which the contract already excuses. The refit and breakdown gaps are not excused.

**The other claims, in the order they appear**

- **Capacity arithmetic.** The paper says 6 round trips a day. A round trip is 50 + 20 + 50 + 20 = 140 minutes, and harbours are open for 720 minutes. That gives 5 round trips (700 minutes), not 6, which would need 840 minutes. So the Calloway offers 5 × 400 = 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400. Your boats' 1,200 figure does reconcile: 5 trips × 120 × 2 boats. The real gain is about 67%, not 100%. It also only matters if you are short of seats, and it doesn't count at all while the Calloway is the only ship and she is out of service.
- **Demand.** The 1,500 a day is a summer forecast, and the paper justifies it by saying the ship will release demand, which is also the reason given for buying the ship. That is circular, and it is no evidence about winter. You asked about winter service, so summer demand has no bearing on it. If winter loads are well below 1,200 each way, the extra seats add nothing.
- **Pier.** The £1.2 million is already spent whether or not you buy the Calloway, so it shouldn't count for or against the purchase. It also covers only Port Skerrow. The paper says nothing about whether the island-side harbour can take a ship of her length and draught.
- **Conclusion.** "Double the capacity and far fewer cancellations" is therefore overstated on capacity. It leaves out the one thing the contract is built around, which is continuity of service.

**What I'd do before the 9th**

Ask Callum to add a section on cover during the refit and breakdowns, with costs. The realistic options are:

1. Keep one of the existing boats as a relief vessel. This reduces the sale proceeds, but it is the simplest way to keep the contract.
2. Arrange a charter of a suitable relief vessel for the three weeks each winter. Check that one is available, that it fits both piers, and what it costs.
3. Get the island council's written agreement that a planned refit gap is acceptable. I wouldn't rely on this, and I'd treat it as unlikely.

Also ask him to correct the capacity figure, give winter demand figures in place of the summer forecast, and confirm the island-side berth. With cover for the refit costed in, the Calloway may still make sense. You could present it as a good ship whose proposal needs one more section. As written, it risks the contract.
