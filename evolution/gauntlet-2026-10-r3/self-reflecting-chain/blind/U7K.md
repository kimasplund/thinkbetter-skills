The paper doesn't answer your question, and as written the answer is no. With one ship the island would not be at least as well served through the winter, and for three weeks it would be in breach of the contract.

**The deciding gap is the refit.** The Calloway is a vessel on the route, so she needs the same three-week statutory refit each winter. Today you cover that by refitting one boat at a time, so the other keeps sailing. With a single ship there is nothing to sail for about 21 days, against a condition of no more than 24 hours without a sailing, and a refit is not a storm exception. Any breakdown has the same effect. The paper never mentions this, and it is the point your question was really about. The options are:

- Keep one of the boats, probably the Tern, as relief cover. That costs you the sale proceeds on one boat.
- Charter a relief vessel for the refit window. The paper would need a quote, and the council would need to confirm that a chartered vessel counts as service under the contract.
- Ask the council in writing for a refit exception. Don't assume you'll get one.

Whichever you choose, the paper should cost it and put it against the savings.

**The capacity sum is overstated.** A 2 hour 20 minute round trip fits 5 round trips in a 12-hour harbour day, not 6. The sixth would leave Port Skerrow at about 18:40 and reach the island at about 19:30, after the harbour closes. That makes 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400. The "1,200 today" figure also looks low. With the same 20-minute turnaround, a 50-minute crossing gives a 2-hour round trip, so each boat could make up to 6 a day, or 1,440 seats each way for the pair if you ran flat out. Seats are only part of it, though. Five sailings a day replaces ten to twelve, so the island gets far fewer departure times. For a lifeline service that probably matters more than seat count, and the paper should say so.

**The demand paragraph can't carry weight.** It says 1,500 a day will come because the ship is big, and that this is why the ship is needed, which assumes its own conclusion. It is also a summer figure, and you asked about winter. What the paper needs is last winter's actual passenger counts and how often the boats were full or turned people away. If winter demand is well below capacity today, extra seats add nothing to the winter question.

**The weather claim is the strongest part, but it is untested.** Going from 31 cancellations to 9 assumes every cancelled day was a wave-height cancellation. Wind in the harbour approaches, berthing limits and visibility also cause cancellations, and a bigger hull can behave differently alongside. Check it against your cancellation log, day by day and by reason, with the wave records. That is a half-day's work, and it should be done before the 9th. It is also worth asking how many of the 31 days the contract would have treated as storms anyway.

**The pier money is spent either way.** The £1.2 million is a reason the decision may look natural, but it says nothing about whether the Calloway is the better buy from here. Only future costs and benefits count: purchase price against sale of two boats, running costs, and crew. The paper gives none of these. Check too that the island harbour can take a ship her length and draught. The paper says only that Port Skerrow was lengthened.

**What I'd tell the board.** Her sea-keeping may well be better, and that may be worth pursuing. But the paper hasn't shown that the island is as well served, and it hasn't addressed a three-week hole in the sailing record that would breach the contract. I'd send it back to Callum before the 9th to add the following:

1. A plan and cost for refit and breakdown cover.
2. The sailing count and timetable corrected to 5 round trips.
3. A winter-only demand analysis.
4. The weather claim checked against the cancellation log.
5. A forward-looking cost comparison with the pier cost left out.

The cheapest first step is a call to the council about whether charter cover would satisfy the contract. If it wouldn't, the proposal as framed can't go ahead.
