Your doubts are justified. The paper doesn't show the island would be at least as well served through the winter, and on its own terms it fails the one condition your contract treats as core. I would not take it to the board as it stands.

**The refit breaks the 24-hour rule.** The paper never mentions the statutory refit. Today you refit one boat at a time, so the other keeps sailing for the three weeks. With only the Calloway, the route has no vessel for three weeks every winter. That is about 21 days without a sailing, and none of it is storm. The storm exception doesn't cover it, so you'd be in breach of the lifeline condition in the first winter. The same applies to any non-storm breakdown. Today a mechanical fault on one boat costs you half the service. With one ship it costs you all of it, and a repair of more than a day puts you outside the contract. The fix is a committed relief vessel for the refit and for breakdowns. That could be a charter or keeping one of the current boats. Its cost has to go into the comparison. It may well erase the saving from selling both.

**The capacity figure is overstated.** A round trip is 50 + 20 + 50 + 20 = 140 minutes. Harbours are open 12 hours, which is 720 minutes. That allows five round trips, not six, because a sixth would finish around 21:00. Five round trips is 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400. Compared with 1,200 today that is still about two-thirds more seats, so the direction survives. But you would be moving from ten departures a day each way to five. That is a real loss for passengers who care about timetable choice, and the paper says nothing about it. If you tell the board 2,400 and someone does the sum, the rest of the paper loses credibility with it.

**The demand argument is circular.** The paper says summer traffic will reach 1,500 a day because the bigger ship will release the demand. It then says that demand is why you need the bigger ship. The only support for the 1,500 is the conclusion it is meant to justify. It is also a summer figure, and you asked about winter. On the paper's own numbers, 1,500 passengers a day sits comfortably inside what two boats carry now (1,200 seats each way, 2,400 across both directions). Unless the 1,500 is each way, it doesn't show you are turning anyone away. Ask Callum for the evidence: actual turn-aways, waiting-list or overflow counts from last summer, and some winter figures.

**The weather claim is the strongest point, but it is thinner than it looks.** Cancellations falling from 31 days to 9 would be a real gain. It's an estimate, though. It assumes the 22 days in between were driven only by wave height and that the Calloway can berth in the wind and swell on those days at both harbours. I'd ask for the day-by-day log with wave and wind readings. It also helps less than it appears for contract purposes. Storm cancellations are already excused. The real exposure is the non-storm outages above, and the single-ship design makes those worse.

**The pier is a sunk cost.** The £1.2 million is spent whether or not you buy her, so it can't count as a reason to proceed. The question is whether the Calloway earns more than she costs from here on. The paper also only mentions Port Skerrow. Check that the island harbour can take her length and draught, as the paper doesn't say. If it can't, the plan fails regardless of everything else.

**What the paper should say instead.** The Calloway may well be a better ship in good weather and in summer, but the paper doesn't show the island is at least as well served in winter. For that it needs three things.
1. A refit and breakdown plan that keeps a sailing every 24 hours, with its cost. This is the decisive one.
2. A corrected capacity and timetable (five round trips, 2,000 seats each way).
3. Confirmation that both harbours can take her, plus winter demand data.

The cheapest first step before the 9th is to ask Callum two questions. Who sails the route during the three-week refit? And can the island harbour berth the Calloway? If his answer to the first is "we'll charter someone", ask for the quote. It will tell you quickly whether the switch still makes financial sense.
