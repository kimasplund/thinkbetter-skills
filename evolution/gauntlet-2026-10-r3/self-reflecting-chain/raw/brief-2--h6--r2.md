Short answer: Anya's note does not show that recurring revenue will be higher in a year, and on the numbers you gave it probably will be lower. Her conclusion is about customer lifetime value, but the board asked about recurring revenue a year out. Those are different questions, and the note skips the price change that sits between them.

**The numbers (recurring revenue annualised, new sign-ups excluded in both cases)**

- Monthly: 820 x £90 x 12 = £885,600 a year. That is £1,080 per clinic.
- Annual: 330 x £900 = £297,000. Total today is £1,182,600.
- An annual clinic pays £900 against £1,080 for a monthly one. Every monthly clinic that switches therefore cuts its recurring revenue by £180, or 17%. The note never mentions this.

**Without the change, a year out, using the historic churn rates**

- Monthly: 820 x 0.75 = 615 clinics, which is £664,200.
- Annual: 330 x 0.94 = 310 clinics, which is about £279,200.
- Total is about £943,400.

**With the change**

If a share s of monthly clinics switch, they bring in 820 x s x £900. The annual base is unchanged at about £279,200.

| Share who switch | Recurring revenue from switchers | Total | vs £943,400 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 59% (the test rate, 212/360) | £435,600 | about £714,800 | about £229,000 lower |
| 95% (Anya's figure) | £701,100 | about £980,300 | about £37,000 higher |

The 95% row assumes none of the switchers churn during the year. If they churn at 6%, it falls to about £938,300, which is slightly below doing nothing.

Break-even is about 90% of monthly clinics switching and staying, or about 96% if the switchers also churn at 6%. The change only wins if almost nobody leaves.

**Where the reasoning breaks down**

1. **The base will not churn at 6%.** The 6% belongs to clinics that chose annual. That is a self-selected group, and loyalty and the plan are tangled together. Clinics pushed onto annual against their preference are likely to churn more. Churn falling to 6% is an assumption, not a result. It also doesn't matter much for the one-year question, since the revenue drop comes from price and from leavers.
2. **The test does not support 95%.** The test said 212 of 360, which is 59%, and that is below her own "at least 95%". The test was also on new prospects who were quoted annual-only pricing at sign-up. They had no existing monthly relationship to lose. Existing clinics facing a forced change, an upfront £900 and a loss of flexibility may react worse. A 59% result is a strong warning against 95%. It is not evidence for it.
3. **"They would have left anyway" is wrong.** Monthly clinics churn at 25% a year, so about 75% would have stayed through the year, and each is worth £1,080. A clinic that leaves because of the change is revenue lost that would otherwise have been kept. Leaving rather than switching does not prove it was uncommitted. It may only reflect a clinic that doesn't want to pay £900 upfront. The note also doesn't separate leavers who would have left anyway (about 25%) from those the change causes to leave.
4. **Cash and fees are real but separate.** Cash brought forward and lower card fees are benefits, but they are not recurring revenue. They are worth stating as secondary points, not as support for the board's question. A one-off cash boost is partly offset because the discount makes each clinic worth less.
5. **The conclusion changes the question.** "Annual clinics are worth more over their lifetime" compares two different kinds of clinic. It doesn't show that moving a monthly clinic to annual makes it worth more. Per year, an annual clinic pays less. It would need to last about 20% longer just to break even on revenue (1,080 / 900 = 1.2). Annual clinics, at 6% churn, have an expected lifetime of about 16 years against 4 for monthly, which is a large gap. But that gap partly reflects who chooses annual, so the benefit of converting could be much smaller than the gap suggests.

**What I would say on Tuesday**

"On our figures, the change raises recurring revenue in a year only if about 90% or more of monthly clinics convert and stay. Our one data point suggests 59%. At that rate we'd be roughly £230,000 lower in a year. The longer-term value case depends on churn falling, which we haven't shown."

**Assumptions and caveats**

- New sign-ups are ignored in both scenarios. Under annual-only they would also be affected, and the test suggests fewer would convert, which could widen the gap.
- Churn is applied once across the year. Timing within the year, and whether revenue is measured as ARR or as billings, would shift the figures a little.
- Taking the 25% and 6% churn rates as given, I haven't checked them.

**Ways to make the case stronger**

- Test the offer on existing monthly clinics, perhaps a small cohort, before committing. That tells you the real conversion rate.
- Consider a middle option: keep the monthly plan at a higher price, or offer annual at a smaller discount (for example £1,000 instead of £900). Break-even conversion falls sharply when the discount is smaller.
- Present a range of scenarios (for example 60%, 80%, 95% conversion, with and without extra churn) so the board can see how sensitive the answer is.
