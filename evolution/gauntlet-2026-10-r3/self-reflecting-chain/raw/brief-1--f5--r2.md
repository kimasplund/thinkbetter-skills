Short answer: no, the paper doesn't show the island would be at least as well served through the winter, and as written the switch would break your contract's core condition. I wouldn't send it to the board on the 9th without the changes below.

**The refit is the deciding problem, and the paper never mentions it.** Every vessel on the route must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. Today you refit one boat at a time, so the other keeps the island served. With the Calloway as your only ship, she is out of service for about 21 days, with no sailing at all. That is far beyond the 24-hour limit, and a refit is not a storm, so it is not an exception. This would put you in breach in the first winter. Any breakdown in the other months has the same effect: with two boats you have cover, and with one you don't.

The paper has to deal with this before anything else. The options I can see:
- Keep one of the small boats as relief. Then the Calloway's refit is covered by the Linnet or the Tern. But you would only be selling one boat, so the sale income and running costs change, and Callum needs to redo the numbers.
- Charter a relief vessel for three weeks every winter. It must be approved for the route and able to use both piers, and the annual cost has to go into the comparison.
- Get the council to agree in writing that a statutory refit period counts as an exception. Nothing you've told me suggests it does, so I wouldn't assume it.

**Capacity is overstated, though it still favours the Calloway.** Harbours are open 12 hours. A round trip is 50 + 20 + 50 + 20 = 140 minutes, so six round trips need 14 hours (the final turnaround isn't needed, so about 13 hours 40 minutes). Starting at 07:00, the sixth crossing would arrive at about 19:30, after closing. She can make five round trips, which is 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400. Your own figure of 1,200 today fits five trips per boat, so five is the right comparison. The gain is about 1.7 times, not double. It is still a real gain, but it's the winter question you asked about, and in winter you are not short of seats on a route already running five trips per boat.

**The demand paragraph doesn't support anything.** It says demand will reach 1,500 a day because the ship is big, and that this demand is why a big ship is needed. The conclusion is being used as its own evidence. Nothing independent is offered, such as booking data, turned-away counts or a forecast. It is also about summer traffic and you asked about winter. I'd cut it or replace it with the turn-away numbers you already hold.

**The weather figure is suggestive but unproven.** Cancelling on 9 days instead of 31 is the strongest point in the paper, and it is a real advantage for the Calloway. But it assumes your cancellations were driven by wave height alone, and that a bigger ship would have sailed on every one of those 22 days, including harbour and berthing conditions and wind at the piers. Check it against your logs for those 31 days, using recorded wave height and the master's reason for each. Also check how many of the 31 would have counted as storms under the contract anyway, because those don't put you in breach. The days that matter are the non-storm ones. One more thing runs the other way: if she is your only ship, a cancelled day leaves a gap of well over 24 hours (07:00 one day to 07:00 or later the next), so the days she doesn't sail count more than they do now.

**The pier is a sunk cost.** The £1.2 million is spent whichever way you decide. "Not buying her would waste that money" can't be a reason to buy. The pier only matters if it makes the Calloway cheaper to operate than the alternatives, and the paper should say so if that is the case. It also matters that the pier was lengthened before the board has decided anything, which makes the paper read as justifying a decision already taken. Say plainly whether the pier works for the smaller boats too.

**Conclusion.** "Double the capacity and far fewer weather cancellations" is not enough to say the island is better served, because it leaves out the one thing the contract measures: no gap over 24 hours. A fair summary today is that the Calloway looks better on capacity (about 1.7 times) and probably on weather, and clearly worse on resilience, with a guaranteed three-week gap each winter unless you pay for cover.

What I'd ask Callum for, cheapest first:
1. A statement of how the three-week refit is covered, and what it costs. A charter quote could take a day or two.
2. The 31 cancellation days from your logs, each marked storm or non-storm and with the recorded wave height.
3. A redone timetable of five round trips and the seat numbers that follow from it.
4. The option of buying the Calloway and keeping one small boat, costed alongside the sole-ship option.

If he can show a relief arrangement that is approved and costed, the case could still stand, and it would be a stronger paper for including it.
