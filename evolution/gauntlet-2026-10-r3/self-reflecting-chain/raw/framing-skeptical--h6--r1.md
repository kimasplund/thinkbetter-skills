**Short answer:** the paper doesn't show the island will be at least as well served through the winter. As written, the switch would breach the contract's core condition. Callum may still be right about the Calloway, but this paper doesn't prove it.

**1. The refit breaks the 24-hour rule, and the paper never mentions it.**
- Every vessel must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. You refit one boat at a time, so today one boat is always sailing.
- With a single ship, the island has no sailing for about 21 days. Refit isn't a storm, so it isn't covered by the exception.
- The same applies to any winter breakdown or survey. There is no second vessel to cover.
- Unless the paper shows a charter or relief vessel for those three weeks, with its cost and a firm booking, the proposal fails the condition the contract renewal depends on.
- This is the point to expect the board to raise first.

**2. The capacity figure is wrong.**
- A round trip is 50 + 20 + 50 + 20 = 140 minutes. Harbours are open 12 hours, which is 720 minutes.
- Six round trips would need 840 minutes, so they don't fit. Five do, ending at about 18:20.
- That gives 5 x 400 = 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400.
- Your current 1,200 does assume 5 round trips per boat (2 x 5 x 120).
- The Calloway is still well ahead on capacity, but the paper overstates it by 20%.
- The capacity gain also doesn't help the winter question. The 24-hour condition is about frequency and continuity, not seat count. One large ship offers 5 sailings a day each way when she runs, and none during refit.

**3. The demand argument is circular and irrelevant to winter.**
- "Demand will reach 1,500 because the ship is big, and that demand is why we need a big ship" assumes its own conclusion.
- It is also a summer forecast, and you asked about winter.
- Your current capacity is 1,200 each way, so 1,500 passengers a day is only a problem if it is all one-directional. The paper doesn't say, and it gives no evidence for the forecast.

**4. The weather claim needs more care.**
- Cancelling 9 days instead of 31 sounds strong, but it is a back-cast without its method.
- The contract excepts storms, so some or most of those 31 days may not count against you today.
- Check how many of the 31 were actual breaches of the 24-hour rule. With two boats you may rarely have been at risk. With one ship, any weather day she can't sail is a risk, as is any day a mechanical fault takes her out.
- A higher wave rating helps. But concentrating all the risk in one hull cancels out part of that benefit.

**5. The pier is a sunk cost.**
- The 1.2m pounds is spent whether or not you buy the Calloway, so it shouldn't drive the decision.
- It only matters if the pier would otherwise have no use. It also suggests the decision was effectively made before the analysis.
- Expect the board to see this as pressure rather than an argument.

**What's missing entirely**
- Purchase price against the sale value of the Linnet and Tern.
- Crew, fuel and running costs for the larger ship.
- The Calloway's age, condition and refit history.
- Whether the Calloway can be handled safely at the island-end harbour.
- Whether the council would accept a single-vessel service.

**What to ask Callum before the 9th**
1. How will the Calloway cover a three-week refit, and what will it cost, so that no 24-hour gap occurs?
2. What is the cover plan for an unplanned breakdown?
3. Can he redo the schedule showing 5 round trips, with the real timetable?
4. Can he show how many of last winter's 31 cancellation days would have counted as breaches?
5. Can he give a winter, not summer, demand case?

**Assumption:** I've taken the refit as taking the whole vessel out for the full three weeks, as you describe it. If a shorter or split refit is allowed, say so, because that would change point 1.

**Bottom line:** I'd send the paper back. A fix is possible, such as keeping one small boat or arranging contracted relief cover, but the board needs that case made, with costs, before it can vote.
