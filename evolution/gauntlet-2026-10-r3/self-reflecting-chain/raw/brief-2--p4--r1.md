**Short answer for the board: on the numbers you've given me, no. A year from now you would most likely have less recurring revenue with the change than without it, and even Anya's own assumptions only get you to roughly break-even.** Her note has four problems, and the first one decides the answer.

## 1. The price per clinic drops

A monthly clinic pays £90 x 12 = £1,080 a year. An annual clinic pays £900. Every monthly clinic that switches therefore gives up £180 (about 17%) of recurring revenue on the day it moves. Anya's note never mentions this, and the board's question is about recurring revenue, not lifetime value.

## 2. The comparison I ran (12 months out, existing clinics only)

I left new sign-ups out because they are the same in both scenarios, except for the acquisition risk in point 4. Starting ARR is 820 x £1,080 + 330 x £900 = £885,600 + £297,000 = £1,182,600.

**Without the change.** Monthly: 820 x 75% = 615 clinics, or £664,200. Annual: 330 x 94% = 310 clinics, or £279,180. Total about **£943,000**.

**With the change.** The annual book is the same £279,180. The monthly book becomes (820 x share who switch) x 94% x £900 = £693,720 x the switch share.

| Share of monthly clinics who switch | Switchers churn 6% | Switchers churn 12% |
|---|---|---|
| 100% | £973k (+£30k, +3%) | £929k (-£14k) |
| 95% (Anya's expectation) | £938k (-£5k) | £896k (-£47k) |
| 80% | £834k (-£109k) | £799k (-£145k) |
| 59% (what her own test shows) | £688k (-£256k, -27%) | £662k (-£282k) |

Compared with about £943k without the change, the change only wins if almost every monthly clinic switches and the switchers then churn as little as clinics that chose annual themselves. Break-even needs about 96% to switch, assuming the best case of 6% churn afterwards. At 95% it is a small loss before you allow for anything going wrong.

One caveat in the plan's favour. Monthly clinics switch at different points during the year, so some of the 6% churn applies for less than a full year. That helps the "with" case a little. It doesn't change the picture.

## 3. Where the note's reasoning breaks

**"Once every clinic is on annual, churn falls to 6%."** This reads a selection effect as a cause. The clinics on annual today chose to prepay £900, which means they were already the committed ones. Forcing a reluctant monthly clinic onto an annual contract doesn't give it the loyalty of a clinic that picked annual. The 25% and 6% figures describe two different kinds of clinic. They don't show what annual billing does to a given clinic. The 6% is the best case for switchers, and a likely outcome is somewhere above it. The table shows how fast the answer worsens as that figure rises.

**"At least 95% will switch, because 212 of 360 went ahead."** Two problems.
- 212/360 is 59%, not 95%. The evidence supports the bottom row of the table, not the top.
- Those were new prospects evaluating a product for the first time, with nothing to lose by walking away. Existing clinics have booking data and staff habits invested in the product. That could make them stickier, or the sudden loss of a monthly option could make them angrier. The test can't tell you which, so it supports no figure close to 95%.

**"Clinics that leave were never committed, so we lose nothing."** This is the weakest step. Without the change, about 75% of monthly clinics would still be paying at the end of the year, and the 25% who leave would be spread over twelve months. With the change, every clinic that leaves rather than switch takes away revenue that would otherwise have stayed. At a 59% switch rate that is about 337 clinics lost, against about 205 expected to churn anyway. Calling the extra leavers uncommitted doesn't make their £1,080 a year disappear from the comparison.

**"Cash forward and lower card fees."** Both are true and worth having, but neither is recurring revenue. They answer a different question (cash flow and margin) and should be shown to the board separately. Their size is also unknown. A bounded estimate would be useful, for example fees saved on £1,080 x 820 of monthly payments.

**The conclusion.** "Annual clinics are worth more over their lifetime" is a different claim from "a year from now we will have more recurring revenue". On lifetime value alone, the figures look attractive: £900 / 6% implies about £15,000 per annual clinic, against £1,080 / 25%, about £4,300, per monthly clinic. But that arithmetic assumes the churn gap is caused by the plan, which is the unproven step. It also isn't what the board asked.

## 4. Things the note leaves out

- **New sign-ups.** If annual-only converts only 59% of the people you quote, the effect on acquisition could be large. We don't know what the monthly-available baseline conversion rate is, so I can't size it, but it should be checked.
- **Whether the 360-clinic test had a control group.** Without a comparison group at the same time, it doesn't show whether annual-only pricing helps or hurts.
- **Renewal timing.** The plan switches clinics at their next renewal. Confirm that all 820 monthly clinics will reach a decision within the twelve months. If any don't, they sit in the monthly book and are not forced to leave.

## What I would tell the board on Tuesday

1. Under the plan's own best-case assumptions, ARR a year out is about break-even, and under the evidence actually presented it is about 27% lower.
2. The plan is a bet that nearly all monthly clinics will accept a 17% lower annual price per clinic and then behave like today's annual clinics. Nothing in the note supports that.
3. If annual billing is the goal, there are less risky ways to pursue it. For example, offer an annual discount as an option, or phase the change by cohort starting with the most engaged clinics and measure real switch and churn rates before extending it. I haven't modelled these, so treat them as ideas to test, not recommendations.

The assumptions I made were that all clinics renew on their anniversary, that there are no price changes, and that new sign-ups are equal in both scenarios. If any of those is wrong in your business, send me the correct figure and I'll rerun the table.
