Anya's note doesn't answer the board's question, and her key number is on shaky ground. On her own 95% switch assumption the change wins only narrowly. At the take-up her test actually measured, it loses badly.

**What the board asked, in numbers**

Today: 820 monthly clinics at £1,080 a year is £885,600, and 330 annual clinics at £900 is £297,000. That is £1,182,600 in total. I've left new sign-ups out of both scenarios, assuming they are the same either way.

- **Without the change:** after a year, 615 monthly clinics remain (25% churn), worth £664,200. The annual book is 310 clinics (6% churn), worth £279,180. Total: about £943,000.
- **With the change:** the annual book is the same £279,180. Recurring revenue from the old monthly clinics is 820 × switch rate × £900.

The switch rate where the two scenarios tie is **90%**. Every point of take-up is worth about £7,380.

| Share of monthly clinics that switch | Recurring revenue in a year | vs. no change |
|---|---|---|
| 95% (Anya) | about £980,000 | +£37,000 (+4%) |
| 90% | about £943,000 | 0 |
| 80% | about £870,000 | -£74,000 |
| 59% (what her test showed) | about £714,000 | -£229,000 (-24%) |

**Where the reasoning breaks**

1. **The price cut is missing.** A monthly clinic pays £1,080 a year and an annual clinic pays £900. Each clinic that switches gives up £180, or 17%. Anya compares loyalty and ignores this. So the change has to keep roughly 90% of the monthly base just to match doing nothing. The no-change baseline already loses 25% of them, but each survivor pays 20% more than they would on annual.

2. **The 95% figure doesn't follow from the test.** The test was on new sign-ups, who had no plan to lose. Existing clinics are losing something they already have, and many chose monthly because they didn't want to commit £900 upfront. Even on the test, 212 of 360 is 59%, not 95%. Nothing in the note supports "at least 95%". The test also had no control group, so we can't tell whether sign-ups were put off by annual-only pricing.

3. **"Churn falls to 6%" borrows a number from a different group.** The 6% comes from clinics that chose annual. Clinics forced onto it are the more price-sensitive ones, and their churn at the first renewal is likely to be higher. That doesn't affect the one-year figures above, because the switch-or-leave decision happens at renewal. It does undermine the claim about the future base.

4. **"Clinics that leave were never committed" is circular.** It defines every departure as one that would have happened anyway, so no outcome could count against the plan. The baseline already tells you how many would have left anyway: 25% of monthly clinics. Anything above that is a loss caused by the change, and it is the quantity the board needs.

5. **The conclusion answers a different question.** "Lifetime value per annual clinic is higher" is probably true. Roughly £15,000 at 6% churn compares with about £4,300 for a monthly clinic at 25%. But that compares two self-selected groups, and it says nothing about what happens to total recurring revenue when you remove a choice. Cash brought forward and lower card fees are real benefits. They don't add to recurring revenue, so they belong in a separate line.

**What I'd say on Tuesday**

"The change increases recurring revenue only if more than 90% of monthly clinics switch. We have no evidence for that figure. The one data point we have, from new sign-ups, suggests about 59%, which would cost us roughly a quarter of recurring revenue."

The cheapest way to settle it is to offer annual-only terms to a sample of existing monthly clinics, or to the next cohort coming up for renewal, before committing the whole base. A variant would be to keep monthly at a higher price. At around £100 a month, monthly clinics would pay £1,200 a year, which would make the annual option more attractive without forcing anyone out.

I assumed new sign-ups, pricing and the churn rates are otherwise unchanged. If monthly clinics at renewal are billed at different rates, or if you count revenue as cash received rather than annualised, the break-even point moves, and I can rerun it.
