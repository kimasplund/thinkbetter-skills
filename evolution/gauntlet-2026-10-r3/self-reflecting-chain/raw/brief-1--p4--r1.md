**Short answer: no. As written, the paper does not show the island would be at least as well served through the winter, and on the one condition your contract turns on, it would be worse off.**

**1. The refit breaks the lifeline condition (decisive)**

Every vessel on the route must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. Today you refit one boat at a time, so the other keeps sailing and the island is never without a service. With only the Calloway, her refit leaves 21 days with no vessel at all. A refit is not a storm, so the exception doesn't cover it. That is a breach of the core condition for three weeks every winter, in the very season the question was about. The paper never mentions the refit.

This also affects ordinary winter risk. With two boats, a breakdown or a cancelled sailing on one leaves the other. With one ship, any breakdown, or a day when she can't sail for non-storm reasons, risks a gap of more than 24 hours. Selling both boats removes your only fallback.

The paper only works if there is cover during her refit. That could be a retained boat, a chartered relief vessel, or a written agreement with the council. Each of those has a cost and has to be priced in.

**2. The capacity arithmetic is wrong**

A round trip is 50 + 20 + 50 + 20 = 140 minutes. Six round trips need 14 hours (about 13 hours 40 minutes if you drop the last turnaround), but the harbours are open for 12. She can make 5 round trips, not 6, so 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400. That is still well above today's 1,200, so the capacity advantage survives, but the paper's figure is overstated by a sixth. Check that 5 trips fit the exact timetable once the first and last sailings are fixed against the 07:00 and 19:00 limits.

More seats also do not matter if there is no sailing on the days that count.

**3. The demand argument is circular and about the wrong season**

"Summer traffic will reach 1,500 a day because a ship her size releases the demand" is a forecast offered as its own evidence, and the conclusion ("that is why we need a ship her size") is just the premise restated. It is also about summer. You asked about winter, and the paper gives no winter demand figure. Your current boats' winter loads would show whether capacity is a winter problem at all. If it isn't, the extra seats don't help the lifeline.

**4. The weather figures don't show what the paper says they do**

- 31 cancellations last winter against 9 is a retrospective estimate. It needs checking day by day against actual sea state, not just the rating. Wave height is not the only reason a crossing is cancelled; wind in harbour, visibility and berthing limits also matter.
- Storm days are already excepted from your 24-hour condition. Fewer weather cancellations is a better service for passengers, which is worth having. It does not protect the contract, and it does not offset a three-week gap.
- One ship also concentrates the risk. On a day when she is cancelled, the island has no sailing. Two boats cancelled on the same day give the same result, but on days when only one is unavailable for other reasons, you still sail.

**5. The pier is a sunk cost**

The £1.2 million is already spent. Not buying the Calloway doesn't make it more or less spent. Whether the pier work was worthwhile is a separate question, and it should only count as an asset you can use, for example if the larger ship is later added as a third vessel. It is not a reason to buy.

**What the board paper should say instead**

- A refit plan: how the island is served for three weeks, with the cost and a council-approved arrangement (relief charter, keeping one boat, or a negotiated exemption in the renewal).
- Corrected timetable and capacity (5 round trips, 2,000 seats each way).
- Winter demand, not summer, from your own passenger counts.
- A per-day weather comparison, and the risk of a single point of failure.
- A full costing: sale value of the two boats, purchase and running costs, relief cover, against keeping the current fleet.

**Bottom line:** the Calloway may well be the better ship, and the capacity gain is real even after the correction. But Callum's paper recommends a fleet that cannot meet your contract's core condition for three weeks each winter, and it doesn't say so. I would not send it to the board on the 9th without a refit-cover plan and corrected numbers. Keeping one of the current boats alongside the Calloway is the obvious option to cost first.

One assumption: I've taken the 24-hour condition to mean at least one sailing in every 24 hours, and a refit as a non-storm event. If the council's wording allows planned maintenance as an exception, check it in the contract text, but I would not rely on that.
