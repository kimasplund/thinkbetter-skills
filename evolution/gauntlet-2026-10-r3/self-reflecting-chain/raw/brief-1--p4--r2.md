**Short answer: no. As written, the paper does not show that the island would be at least as well served through the winter. On the facts you gave, a one-ship fleet would breach the contract condition during the refit.**

**1. The refit breaks the 24-hour rule (the decisive point)**

- Every vessel on the route must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. You currently refit one boat at a time, so the other keeps sailing and the island is never without a service.
- With only the Calloway, her refit means about 21 days with no vessel on the route. The condition is no more than 24 hours without a sailing, and the only exception is storms. A statutory refit is not a storm.
- The paper does not mention the refit at all. Its capacity and weather figures describe the days the ship is running. They say nothing about the 21 days she isn't.
- The same weakness applies to any breakdown, crew shortage or damage. Today a fault on one boat leaves the other sailing. With one ship, any such fault stops the service entirely.

**2. The capacity figure is overstated**

- Each leg takes 50 minutes of sailing plus 20 minutes of turnaround, so a round trip is 2h20, as the paper says.
- Harbours are open 12 hours, from 07:00 to 19:00. Six round trips need 14 hours, or 13h40 if the final turnaround is skipped. That doesn't fit.
- Five round trips fit, taking 11h40 of the 12 hours. That is 5 x 400 = 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400.
- This is still an increase on today's 1,200 (two boats x 5 x 120). It also has a cost: the Calloway has no slack in the day. Any delay pushes the last sailing past 19:00.
- More seats also don't help in winter unless winter demand is above what you carry now. The paper gives no winter passenger figures.

**3. The demand argument is circular and about the wrong season**

- "Summer traffic will reach 1,500 a day because a ship her size releases demand" assumes the result it is meant to prove, and it is a forecast, not evidence.
- The 1,500 figure is for summer. Your question was about winter service, and nothing in the paper addresses winter loads.

**4. The weather figure needs checking, and it isn't the contract risk**

- The comparison of 31 cancelled days with 9 is a claim about last winter. Ask for the day-by-day wave data and the cancellation log behind it.
- A 3.5 m rating is not a guarantee. Harbour entrances, wind and passenger-comfort limits can also stop sailings. Also ask how many of the 31 days counted as storms, because storm days are already exempt from the 24-hour condition.
- Fewer weather cancellations would be a real gain for passengers. But it only improves your contract position on non-storm days, and the refit and breakdown exposure outweighs it.

**5. The pier is a sunk cost**

- The £1.2 million is already spent whichever way the board decides. It shows that the pier can take a longer ship. It is not a reason to buy one.
- The question for the board is whether the Calloway is the best use of the money from here on.

**What I would send back to Callum**

1. A plan for the 21-day refit that keeps a sailing at least every 24 hours. The options are a chartered relief vessel approved for the route (with costs and availability), retaining one of the existing boats as relief (sell only one), or a written agreement from the council to a derogation. Don't assume the council will agree to one.
2. A breakdown plan, covering the maximum time to bring in a replacement and what the council expects.
3. Corrected timetable and capacity (five round trips, not six).
4. Winter passenger numbers, not summer forecasts.
5. The weather data behind the 31-versus-9 claim.
6. Full costs: sale prices for the Linnet and Tern, the Calloway's purchase price, running costs and insurance, and the relief-vessel cost from point 1.

The Calloway could still be a good purchase, particularly if you keep one existing boat or secure reliable relief cover. But the board shouldn't approve the paper as it stands, because its recommendation fails the one condition you asked about.
