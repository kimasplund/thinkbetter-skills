Short answer: the paper doesn't show that the island would be at least as well served through the winter, and on the evidence in it, it probably wouldn't be. I wouldn't send it to the board as it stands.

**1. The refit breaks your core contract condition (the biggest problem)**
Every vessel on the route must spend three weeks in dry dock each winter. Today you refit one boat at a time, so the other boat keeps sailing and the island is never without service. With only the Calloway, the route has no vessel for about three weeks, which is far more than 24 hours with no sailing. A statutory refit isn't a storm, so the exception wouldn't cover it. The paper doesn't mention this. Unless Callum has a plan, such as chartering a relief vessel for three weeks every winter (with its cost, availability and berth suitability), the switch puts the January renewal at risk. Any relief vessel also needs to be able to use the piers.

**2. One ship means one point of failure**
The same applies outside the refit. A breakdown, a crew problem or a missed survey on one ship stops all service. With two boats you can usually cover. The paper compares seats, but the contract condition is about continuity.

**3. The capacity arithmetic is wrong, and frequency halves**
- A round trip is 2h20 (50 + 20 + 50 + 20), and harbours are open 12 hours (07:00 to 19:00). Six round trips is 14 hours, which doesn't fit. Five fit (700 minutes, arriving back at 18:40). So the figure is 5 x 400 = 2,000 seats each way, not 2,400.
- Your current 1,200 each way is consistent with each boat doing 5 round trips (10 x 120). So the real comparison is 2,000 against 1,200, not 2,400 against 1,200. That is still more seats.
- But sailings each way drop from 10 a day to 5, so gaps between departures roughly double. Winter demand is thin, and seats may not be what limits it. Frequency, and the length of the longest gap between sailings, matters more to islanders. The paper should show a timetable.

**4. The demand argument is circular and answers the wrong season**
"Summer traffic will reach 1,500 a day because a ship her size releases demand" is an assertion with no evidence, and it is then used to justify the ship ("that level of demand is why we need a ship her size"). It is also about summer, while your question was about winter. I'd want actual winter passenger numbers, including peak days, to see whether 120-seat boats were ever the constraint.

**5. The weather figures need checking, and they cut both ways**
- Cancelling 9 days instead of 31 is plausible given the higher wave rating, but it is a back-of-envelope claim. Callum should show it against actual wave and wind records, and check that sea state is the only cause (wind in the harbour, berthing limits and visibility also cancel sailings). A larger ship may also have bigger berthing limits.
- Her rating is also relevant to the contract, since cancellations in storms are excused. Fewer cancellations is a real benefit, but it doesn't offset a three-week gap.

**6. The pier is a sunk cost**
The £1.2 million spent on Port Skerrow pier is gone whichever choice you make. "Not buying her would waste that money" shouldn't count in the decision. The useful questions are whether the pier works for her, and whether the island-side harbour (draught, berth length, tides, linkspan or gangways) can take her too. The paper only mentions one pier.

**7. Things missing from the paper**
- Purchase price, sale value of the Linnet and Tern, running costs (fuel, crew, certification for a bigger passenger vessel) and the age and condition of a second-hand ship.
- Crewing, since a 400-passenger ship usually needs a larger crew and different certification.
- Cargo, vehicles and freight, if the boats carry any.

**What I'd tell the board**
The paper shows she may carry more and cancel less, but it hasn't answered your question. Ask Callum to come back with:
1. A plan for the three-week winter refit and for breakdowns (relief vessel, cost, which piers it can use), and how that satisfies the 24-hour condition.
2. A corrected capacity figure (5 round trips) and a winter timetable showing the longest gap between sailings.
3. Evidence for winter demand, not summer projections.
4. Weather figures against records, and confirmation that both harbours can take her.
5. A full cost comparison that treats the pier money as sunk.

Assumptions: I took the harbour hours as the only limit on the day's sailings, and I assumed there's no relief vessel arrangement in place already. If you do have one, point 1 becomes a cost question rather than a contract risk.
